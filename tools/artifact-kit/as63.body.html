Protectionism and Its Tools

6.2.1 to 6.2.2: the meaning of protectionism and the impact of tariffs, quotas, export subsidies, embargoes and red tape. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Protectionism is the use of government policy to protect domestic producers from foreign competition, restricting free trade and raising the price competitiveness of domestic firms. The main tools are tariffs (taxes on imports), import quotas (limits on the quantity of imports), export subsidies (payments to exporters), embargoes (bans on trade) and excessive administrative burdens or "red tape" (complex rules and delays). A tariff or quota raises the domestic price, reduces imports and raises domestic output, helping producers but harming consumers, who pay more and have less choice, and causing a welfare loss. How effective a tool is depends on the price elasticity of demand for imports, the possibility of retaliation and whether domestic industries can meet the extra demand.

1 · Tariffs (6.2.2)

  • A tariff is a tax on imported goods. It raises the price of imports above the world price, so domestic production rises, domestic consumption falls and imports fall.
  • The government gains tariff revenue (tariff per unit × quantity imported after the tariff).
  • Consumers lose consumer surplus (higher price, less choice); domestic producers gain producer surplus; there is a deadweight welfare loss, since resources move from efficient foreign producers to less efficient domestic ones.
  • Effectiveness: works better if demand for imports is price elastic and domestic firms can expand; may provoke retaliation and can raise costs for firms using imported inputs (cost-push inflation).

2 · Other tools

ToolHow it worksEffect
Import quotaA limit on the quantity of a good that may be importedRaises the domestic price; the price gap goes to importers or foreign suppliers (no government revenue unless licences are sold)
Export subsidyA payment to domestic firms for each unit exportedLowers export prices, raising exports; costs the government; may provoke retaliation or be challenged as dumping
EmbargoA complete ban on trade with a country or in a goodImports fall to zero; used for political or security reasons
Excessive administrative burdens ("red tape")Complicated customs rules, standards and delaysRaises the cost of importing and discourages trade; hard to measure and challenge
  • Tariff versus quota: a tariff gives the government revenue and keeps the price mechanism; a quota fixes the quantity more precisely but creates a quota rent for importers and may encourage corruption.
  • Voluntary export restraints are agreements by an exporter to limit sales, and work like a quota.
  • Not protectionism: a rise in domestic sales tax that applies equally to all goods, or import subsidies.

3 · Worked examples

A tariff in numbers. Demand is P = 100 − 0.8Q and domestic supply is P = 20 + 0.8Q. The world price is $40. A tariff of $10 is imposed.

ItemBefore the tariff (price $40)After the tariff (price $50)
Domestic quantity demanded(100 − 40) ÷ 0.8 = 75(100 − 50) ÷ 0.8 = 62.5
Domestic quantity supplied(40 − 20) ÷ 0.8 = 25(50 − 20) ÷ 0.8 = 37.5
Imports75 − 25 = 5062.5 − 37.5 = 25
Tariff revenue–$10 × 25 = $250
Welfare loss (two triangles)–½ × $10 × 12.5 + ½ × $10 × 12.5 = $125
  • Domestic firms gain from the higher price and extra output; the government gains revenue; consumers lose because they pay more and buy less. The loss to consumers exceeds the gains to producers and the government, leaving the welfare loss.

Tariff versus quota. A quota of 25 units would give the same fall in imports, the same rise in domestic price (to $50) and the same rise in domestic output, but the $250 would go to the importers or foreign suppliers who hold the right to sell at the higher price (quota rent), not to the government, unless licences are sold.

5 · Effects on the terms of trade

  • A tariff raises the domestic price of imports, which can raise the measured import price index and so lower the terms of trade. If the importing country is large, the tariff may reduce demand for foreign exporters' goods so much that they cut their prices, which would improve the importer's terms of trade.
  • If protection helps domestic firms to develop (for example infant industries), they may later raise export prices or export more, and the terms of trade could improve.
  • Retaliation by trading partners reduces exports, which may push export prices down and worsen the terms of trade.
  • Export subsidies lower export prices, which lowers the export price index and so the terms of trade, even though export volumes rise.
  • Other tools, such as quotas or manipulating the exchange rate, can have similar effects on prices.

6 · Why tariffs and other tools may not work

  • Inelastic demand for imports: consumers keep buying, so imports fall little and the trade deficit is not much reduced.
  • Retaliation and trade wars: other countries raise barriers against the exports of the country that protects, so everyone loses.
  • Higher costs for firms: producers using imported inputs face higher costs, which can raise prices and reduce competitiveness.
  • Inefficiency: protected firms face less competition and may have less incentive to cut costs.
  • International rules: membership of trade agreements can limit the use of tariffs and subsidies.
  • Consumers lose: higher prices and less choice, especially for poorer households.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A tariff on steel

A government puts a 25% tariff on imported steel. Steel becomes dearer, domestic steelmakers sell more and may hire more workers, but car makers and builders face higher costs, and trading partners may retaliate against the country's exports.

Exam use: a tariff's effects and costs.

A quota on clothing

A country allows only a fixed volume of clothing imports each year. The price of clothing rises because supply is restricted. Importers with licences earn extra profit, which does not go to the government.

Exam use: a quota and the quota rent.

Export subsidies

A government pays farmers for each tonne they export. Farm exports become cheaper abroad, harming foreign farmers, who may complain or retaliate, and costing taxpayers.

Exam use: export subsidies and their drawbacks.

Red tape at the border

Complex certification and long customs checks raise the cost and time of importing, discouraging foreign sellers without any tariff. It is hard to challenge because it is not an explicit tax.

Exam use: excessive administrative burdens.

A trade war

Two large economies each impose tariffs on the other's goods. Exporters in both lose sales, firms using imported parts face higher costs and consumers pay more, so many people lose even if some protected industries gain.

Exam use: retaliation and the costs of protection.

Quotas and quota rent

A country limits imports of sugar to a fixed amount. Importers who hold the licences can sell at the higher domestic price and earn extra profit, which does not go to the government.

Exam use: quota rent and the difference from a tariff.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/21 · Q5(a) 8 marks Explain the difference between an import tariff and an import quota and consider why a government would choose an import tariff rather than an import quota as a tool of protection in international trade.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): the meaning of an import tariff (up to 2) and of an import quota (up to 2) as tools of protection. Maximum 3.
  • Analysis (up to 3): the advantages and disadvantages of an import tariff (up to 2) and of an import quota (up to 2) as tools of protection. Maximum 3.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a valid judgement on why a government would choose a tariff rather than a quota, with a conclusion.
PlanTariff: tax on imports. Quota: limit on quantity. Tariff: raises revenue. Quota: gives rent to importers, certainty of quantity. Judge: tariff for revenue.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

An import tariff is a tax on imported goods, which raises their price. An import quota is a limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported, which restricts supply.

A tariff raises the price of imports and gives the government tax revenue, and the price mechanism still operates. A quota fixes the quantity of imports more precisely, but the higher price benefits importers or foreign suppliers who hold the licences, not the government, and may encourage corruption. A tariff can also be changed easily.

A government is likely to choose a tariff because it raises revenue and is more transparent, but if its main aim is to limit the quantity of imports exactly, a quota could be preferred. The choice depends on the government's objectives.

What the mark scheme wants

PlanDefine protectionism. Tariff: import price up, domestic firms more competitive. Problems: retaliation, inelastic demand, higher costs. Judge: effectiveness depends on elasticity and retaliation.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Protectionism is the use of government policy to protect domestic businesses from foreign competition. It restricts free trade and increases the price competitiveness of domestic firms against foreign rivals.

A tariff is a tax on imports, so it raises the price of imported goods, and domestic producers become relatively cheaper, so sales and output of domestic firms rise. But other countries may retaliate with tariffs on this country's exports, higher prices raise costs for firms that use imported inputs, and if demand for the imports is inelastic, imports fall by little.

Tariffs are likely to be effective when demand for imports is elastic and domestic firms can expand, but less effective if demand is inelastic or there is retaliation. They also cost consumers, who pay higher prices.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/23 · Q5(a) 8 marks Explain two methods of protection in international trade and consider which of these is likely to have the bigger impact on employment and output in the economy which imposes them.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): protectionism is when governments attempt to protect domestic businesses against foreign competition (1); an explanation of two methods, such as tariffs, import quotas, export subsidies, embargoes or red tape, and how each distorts the free market or supports domestic production (1 + 1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): how each method affects domestic employment (1 + 1), for example tariffs raise import prices and encourage expenditure switching to domestic production, which raises employment as more workers are recruited; but if demand for imports is price inelastic, the reduction in imports may be smaller, so the effect on domestic output is smaller.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): which method has the bigger impact on employment and output, with a conclusion.
PlanTwo methods explained. Effect on employment: more domestic output and jobs, but depends on elasticity and retaliation. Judge which is bigger.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Protectionism means governments protecting domestic firms against foreign competition. A tariff is a tax on imports, which raises their price and encourages consumers to buy domestic goods. An import quota limits the quantity of imports allowed in, which restricts supply.

Both reduce imports and so shift spending to domestic firms, which increases their output and employment. A tariff only does this if demand for imports is elastic, since with inelastic demand the quantity of imports falls little. A quota guarantees a fall in imports to the set limit.

A quota is likely to have the bigger and more certain effect on domestic output and employment because it fixes the quantity of imports, but both can be offset by retaliation from trading partners and higher costs for firms using imported inputs.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/21 · Q4(b) 12 marks Protectionism can take different forms in different countries. Discuss whether export subsidies are always a better method of protectionism than tariffs.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the advantages and disadvantages of export subsidies as a method of protectionism (up to 4): they allow firms to lower cost and price and undercut foreign competition, and are difficult for the WTO to deal with because they are not an overt method of protectionism; against that, they involve an opportunity cost in terms of other government spending and industries may become too dependent on the subsidy; the advantages and disadvantages of tariffs (up to 4): a tariff is a tax on imports making them more expensive and less competitive, if demand for imports is elastic demand should fall, and the government can use tariffs as a source of revenue; they will not be successful if demand for imports is price inelastic and may lead to retaliation.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether export subsidies are always a better method of protectionism than tariffs, with a reserved mark for a conclusion. No knowledge marks for merely defining or explaining terms used in the question.
PlanExport subsidy: lower prices abroad, hard to challenge; cost, dependence. Tariff: raises import price, revenue; inelastic demand, retaliation. Judge: not always; depends on aim and elasticities.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

An export subsidy lowers firms' costs so they can cut prices abroad and undercut foreign rivals, and because it is not an overt trade barrier it may be harder for the WTO to challenge. But it costs taxpayers money that could be spent elsewhere, and firms may become dependent on it. A tariff makes imports dearer, reducing demand if it is elastic, and raises revenue for the government, but it is ineffective if demand for imports is inelastic, and may provoke retaliation.

Export subsidies are not always better. They help exporting industries but cost the government money, whereas tariffs raise revenue and protect the home market. Which is better depends on the aim (protecting the home market or boosting exports), the elasticities and the risk of retaliation.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/23 · Q5(b) 12 marks Assess the extent to which different tools of protection can impact on the terms of trade in an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): an explanation of what is meant by tools of protection; an explanation of the different tools and how they may affect the terms of trade; for example tariffs increase import prices, which will reduce the terms of trade, but may improve them if domestic producers, including infant industries, are encouraged to invest and build up demand and increase export prices; retaliation and inelastic PED for imports may reduce the positive impact; at least one additional tool, such as import quotas, embargoes or exchange rate manipulation, should be explained and analysed with its advantages and disadvantages for the terms of trade. Level 2 for a one-sided response.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): must focus on the impact of the tools of protection on the terms of trade and lead to a justified conclusion. A one-sided response cannot gain any marks for evaluation.
PlanTariff: import prices up, ToT down; but infant industries may raise export prices; retaliation. Quota, embargo, subsidy: also affect prices. Judge: depends on elasticity and size of country.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Tariffs raise the price of imports, which increases the import price index and so lowers the terms of trade. However, if the tariff helps infant industries to grow and later to raise export prices, the terms of trade could improve. Retaliation by trading partners would reduce exports and push export prices down, and inelastic demand for imports would limit any effect. Import quotas restrict supply and raise import prices in a similar way, while export subsidies lower export prices, which worsens the terms of trade even though export volumes rise.

Protection is likely to lower the terms of trade in the short run, because import prices rise or export prices fall, but its long-run effect depends on whether it helps firms to become competitive, on how large the country is, and on the reaction of other countries. The impact on the terms of trade is therefore uncertain and may be small.

Top tips

  • Define protectionism. Protecting domestic firms from foreign competition.
  • Explain the mechanism. Import price up, imports down, domestic output up.
  • Draw the tariff diagram. World price, tariff, new quantities, revenue.
  • Name winners and losers. Producers and government gain; consumers lose.
  • Mention the welfare loss. Resources move to less efficient producers.
  • Tariff versus quota. Revenue to government versus rent to importers.
  • Limits. Retaliation, elasticity, higher costs.
