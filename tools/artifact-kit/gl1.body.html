Definition of Globalisation

What globalisation is, its main features and how to measure it. Syllabus 6.2.1.

The big idea

Globalisation is the increasing connection and integration of the world's economies, through growing flows of goods and services, money and investment, people, ideas and technology between countries. A phone designed in one country, with parts made in three others, assembled in a fifth and sold worldwide is a product of globalisation. The world acts increasingly like a single market.

Features of globalisation

FeatureWhat it meansExample
More international tradeExports and imports grow faster than outputCheap clothes from Asia sold in Europe
Multinational companies (MNCs)Firms produce and sell in many countriesGlobal car, phone and fast-food brands
Foreign direct investment (FDI)Firms build factories and offices abroadA Japanese car plant in the UK
Global supply chainsDifferent stages of production in different countriesDesign in the USA, chips in South Korea, assembly in China
Movement of people (migration)Workers, students and tourists move between countriesMigrant workers in the Gulf states
Movement of moneyCapital flows and shared financial marketsInvestors buying foreign shares
Spread of ideas, technology and cultureQuick sharing of knowledge, brands and tastesSocial media, streaming, global fashion

Measuring how globalised an economy is

Trade openness = (exports + imports) ÷ GDP × 100

A higher percentage means the economy is more open to trade. Small countries such as Singapore have very high openness; large countries such as the USA have lower openness because they have a large home market. Other measures include FDI as a share of GDP, the number of MNCs, and migration and communication links.

Why it matters

  • Consumers can buy a wider range of goods, often more cheaply.
  • Firms can sell in bigger markets and source cheaper inputs, but face more competition.
  • Events in one country (a financial crisis, a pandemic, a war) can spread quickly to others.
  • It affects jobs, wages, the environment and the gap between rich and poor (see 6.2.2).

Globalisation is not new, but it has speeded up since the 1980s with cheaper transport, the internet and the removal of many trade barriers.

Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain what is meant by a global supply chain, using an example.
ReminderStages of production in different countries; example; why.
A global supply chain is when different stages of making a product take place in different countries. For example, a phone may be designed in the USA, have chips made in South Korea, be assembled in China and sold worldwide. Each stage is done where it is cheapest or where skills are best, lowering costs. However, a problem in one country can disrupt the whole chain.

Top tips

  • Define it with 'increasing integration'. Of economies, through trade, investment, people and ideas.
  • Know the features. Trade, MNCs, FDI, supply chains, migration, technology, culture.
  • Trade openness = (X + M) ÷ GDP. Show working.
  • Globalisation can slow. It is not a one-way process.
