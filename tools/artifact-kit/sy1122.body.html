Fixed and Managed Exchange Rate Systems

11.2.2 to 11.2.4: how the exchange rate is determined under fixed and managed systems, how central banks defend a peg, devaluation and revaluation, and how the rate changes under each system. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Under a fixed exchange rate the central bank sets the rate (a peg) and defends it by buying or selling its own currency using foreign exchange reserves and by adjusting interest rates. Under a managed float the market sets the rate day to day, but the central bank intervenes to keep it within a band or to smooth sharp swings. A fixed rate can only be changed by an official decision: a revaluation (raise the peg) or a devaluation (lower it), whereas a floating rate rises and falls by market forces as appreciation and depreciation. Each system gives a different answer to the same shock, and the exam asks you to compare them.

1 · Determination of the exchange rate (11.2.2)

The currency market

  • The demand for a currency comes from foreign buyers of the country's exports, investors who want its assets, tourists and speculators. The supply comes from residents buying imports and foreign assets. On a diagram the exchange rate is on the vertical axis and the quantity of the currency on the horizontal axis.
  • In a free float, the equilibrium rate is where demand meets supply. A shift in demand or supply (for example higher interest rates abroad) changes the rate with no official action.

Fixed exchange rates

  • The government announces a rate (often against the US dollar, the euro or a basket) and the central bank defends it. The rate may be exactly fixed (a currency board or a peg) or held in a narrow band.
  • If the peg is above the equilibrium (the currency is over-valued): supply exceeds demand at the pegged rate. The central bank must buy the excess currency, paying with its foreign exchange reserves, and may raise interest rates to attract demand.
  • If the peg is below the equilibrium (under-valued): demand exceeds supply. The central bank sells its currency and builds up reserves, and may cut interest rates.
  • If reserves run low and the pressure continues, the government must devalue or give up the peg.

Managed float

  • The rate is mainly set by the market but the central bank intervenes, often within an unannounced or announced band, to stop it from rising or falling too far, too quickly. This is sometimes called a dirty float.
  • The tools are buying or selling reserves and changing interest rates. Within the band the market decides; at the edges the central bank acts.
  • A managed system gives some stability and some policy freedom but needs reserves and can be hard to run if markets think the band will not hold.

2 · Revaluation and devaluation (11.2.3)

  • Devaluation is a deliberate cut in the official value of a currency in a fixed (or managed) system. Revaluation is a deliberate rise.
  • Depreciation and appreciation are changes in the value of a floating currency caused by market forces. Examiners expect the right word for the system.
  • A devaluation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home, aiming to improve the current account and raise aggregate demand, at the cost of imported inflation. A revaluation does the opposite: it helps to lower inflation (cheaper imports and inputs) but may reduce export competitiveness.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Hong Kong's peg to the US dollar

Hong Kong's currency is allowed to move only in a narrow band against the US dollar. The monetary authority buys or sells Hong Kong dollars at the edges of the band, so interest rates in Hong Kong tend to follow US rates closely and local monetary policy has little independence.

Exam use: a fixed (pegged) system: how intervention defends the rate and why the country gives up monetary independence.

Black Wednesday, 1992

The UK was in the European Exchange Rate Mechanism, with sterling pegged within a band. As speculators sold pounds, the Bank of England spent large reserves and raised interest rates, but the peg became impossible to hold and sterling left the system. The pound then floated down.

Exam use: a speculative attack on an over-valued peg, the limits of reserves and a forced devaluation.

The Swiss franc floor

From 2011 to 2015 the Swiss National Bank held a minimum rate of 1.20 francs per euro, selling francs to stop the franc from rising. In 2015 it removed the floor and the franc jumped sharply, showing how costly it can be to hold a rate far from the market level.

Exam use: a managed or semi-fixed rate with a floor, the size of intervention needed and the risk of ending it.

China's managed float

China sets a daily central rate for the yuan against the dollar and allows the market rate to move within a band around it, with the central bank intervening at times. The yuan has appreciated gradually over the long run while remaining tightly managed.

Exam use: a managed float: market forces within limits, with policy influence over the rate.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/43 · Q7 25 marks A government's decision to move from a fixed exchange rate to a floating exchange rate will solve unemployment in the short run but cause higher inflation in the long run. Evaluate this statement.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explain the difference between fixed and floating exchange rates: a fixed rate is held by the central bank buying and selling currency, while a floating rate is set by supply and demand.
  • Unemployment: under a fixed rate, lower interest rates to raise demand put downward pressure on the currency, so the central bank must sell currency or raise rates, undermining the policy. Under a float, lower interest rates cause depreciation, which also raises export demand, so policy is more effective, mainly for cyclical unemployment.
  • Inflation: depreciation raises import prices and may cause cost-push inflation, and the central bank loses control over the money supply, which could be inflationary.
  • Evaluation: it works for cyclical but not structural or frictional unemployment, the size of the inflation depends on import dependence, global conditions and commodity prices, and the time frame matters.

A fixed rate set above the equilibrium rate means that, at the official rate, the supply of the currency is greater than the demand. This is an over-valued currency. To stop the rate from falling, the central bank could buy the surplus currency in the foreign exchange market, paying for it with its reserves of foreign currency. This raises demand for the currency until it matches supply at the pegged rate.

The central bank may also raise interest rates to attract hot money inflows, which raises demand for the currency. This could reduce domestic spending and growth. However, reserves are limited and high interest rates are costly to the domestic economy, so if the pressure continues the government may have to devalue.

Practice question · modelled on the 8 mark style (no past paper) 8 marks Distinguish between a depreciation and a devaluation of a currency, and explain one effect of a devaluation on an economy.
What the mark scheme wants

  • Effect: exports become cheaper and imports dearer, so net exports may rise (current account improves), AD and employment may rise, but import prices and inflation may rise.

Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A depreciation is a fall in the value of a floating currency due to changes in supply and demand. A devaluation is a deliberate cut in the official exchange rate under a fixed system.

A devaluation lowers the foreign price of exports and raises the home price of imports. If the Marshall-Lerner condition holds, net exports could rise, which may raise aggregate demand, output and employment. However, dearer imports could cause imported inflation, so the net effect may depend on the elasticities and the economy's spare capacity.

Top tips

  • Use the right word. Floating rate: appreciation and depreciation. Fixed rate: revaluation and devaluation.
  • Draw the currency market. Exchange rate on the vertical axis, quantity of currency on the horizontal axis. Show the peg as a horizontal line.
  • Show the gap. The distance between demand and supply at the peg is what the central bank must buy or sell.
  • Say what pays for it. Buying the currency uses foreign exchange reserves, which are limited.
  • Link to interest rates. Defending a peg often means giving up an independent interest rate.
  • Compare, then judge. Fixed gives certainty but less freedom; floating gives freedom but volatility; managed is a mix.
  • Effects of devaluation. Cheaper exports and dearer imports: net exports up if elasticities are high enough, imported inflation as the cost.
