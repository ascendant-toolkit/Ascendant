Policies to Control Inflation

Monetary, fiscal and supply-side policies to control inflation, matched to the cause, and how effective they are. Syllabus 4.7.5.

The big idea

The range of policies to control inflation includes monetary policy (raise interest rates, reduce the money supply, raise the exchange rate), fiscal policy (cut government spending, raise taxes) and supply-side policy (raise productivity and cut costs). The right policy depends on the cause: demand-side policy targets demand-pull inflation, while supply-side policy and a stronger exchange rate target cost-push inflation. Every policy has costs, such as lost output and jobs.

The range of policies

PolicyHow it reduces inflationBest for
Raise interest rates (central bank)Borrowing dearer, saving rewarded: consumption and investment fall, AD shifts leftDemand-pull
Reduce the money supplyLess lending and spending: AD shifts leftDemand-pull
Allow the exchange rate to riseImports and raw materials become cheaper, and exports dearer: costs fall, AD fallsCost-push (imported)
Raise taxes (income tax) or cut government spendingLower disposable income and G: AD shifts leftDemand-pull
Supply-side policy (training, infrastructure, competition, deregulation)Raise productivity and lower costs: AS shifts rightCost-push, and long-run
Controls on wages and pricesLimit increases directlyWage-price spiral (but difficult)

To avoid deflation a central bank can do the opposite: cut interest rates, increase the money supply (for example quantitative easing), and governments can raise spending or cut taxes.

Effectiveness and costs

Demand-side policy

  • Pros: can act fairly quickly; effective against excess demand.
  • Cons: lower output and higher unemployment (a conflict of aims); time lags; weak against cost-push inflation.

Supply-side policy

  • Pros: lowers costs without cutting output; gives growth and low inflation together.
  • Cons: slow and costly; uncertain results.
  • Higher interest rates hurt borrowers and may cut the investment that raises capacity.
  • A higher exchange rate makes exports dearer and may worsen the current account.
  • Higher taxes or lower spending are politically difficult.
  • Wage and price controls are hard to enforce and distort markets.
  • Policy must match the cause: raising interest rates does little against a supply shock such as an oil price rise.
  • Expectations matter: credible central banks with inflation targets can hold inflation down more easily.

Rate rises in 2022-23

The US Federal Reserve, the Bank of England and the European Central Bank raised interest rates in a series of steps to bring inflation back towards 2%. Mortgage costs rose and growth slowed.

Exam use: Contractionary monetary policy and its costs.

Inflation targeting

Many central banks have a target, such as 2%, and independence to set interest rates. This helps people expect low inflation, which makes it easier to achieve.

Exam use: Why credibility and expectations matter.

Austerity to control prices

Some countries cut spending and raised taxes during high inflation. Inflation fell but unemployment rose.

Exam use: Fiscal contraction and its trade-off.

Price controls

Some governments have capped the prices of fuel, bread or rents to limit inflation. Shortages and black markets often followed.

Exam use: Direct controls and their drawbacks.

Q1 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a central bank could reduce inflation. (Feb/Mar 2021, P22)
ReminderContractionary monetary policy: interest rates, money supply, exchange rate. Chain each. Avoid a list.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A central bank could use contractionary monetary policy. If it raises the interest rate, borrowing becomes dearer and saving more attractive, so households cut consumption and firms cut investment. Lower total demand reduces demand-pull inflation. It could also reduce the money supply, for example by selling government bonds, which restricts bank lending and spending. A higher interest rate may raise the exchange rate, lowering the price of imports and reducing cost-push inflation. However, it can slow growth and raise unemployment.
Q2 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how an increase in income tax can affect a country's inflation rate. (May/June 2022, P21)
ReminderLower disposable income, lower consumption, AD left, lower inflation. Mention possible cost effect and unemployment.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
An increase in income tax reduces households' disposable income. Consumption is likely to fall, so aggregate demand shifts left, reducing demand-pull inflationary pressure. Workers may also demand higher gross wages to keep their take-home pay, which raises firms' costs and could cause cost-push inflation. The net effect depends on whether demand-pull or cost-push dominates.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain how a decrease in borrowing could reduce the chance of high inflation. (May/June 2021, P23)
ReminderLess borrowing, less spending, AD falls, demand-pull falls.
If borrowing falls, households and firms have less money to spend and invest. Consumption and investment fall, so aggregate demand is lower. With less demand pressure, firms are less likely to raise prices, so demand-pull inflation is less likely.
Q4 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how an increase in a country's foreign exchange rate could reduce inflation. (Oct/Nov 2023, P23)
ReminderImports cheaper; raw materials; exports dearer reduces AD; competition.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A higher exchange rate makes imports cheaper in the home currency. Imported raw materials and components cost firms less, so costs of production fall and AS may shift right. Cheaper imported consumer goods directly lower the price level, and increased competition from imports may force domestic firms to hold prices down. Exports become dearer, so net exports fall and AD shifts left, reducing demand-pull inflation. However, exports and jobs may suffer.
Q5 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a central bank could avoid deflation. (May/June 2021, P21)
ReminderExpansionary monetary policy: lower rates, more money, lower exchange rate. Chain.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A central bank could use expansionary monetary policy. It could cut interest rates, so borrowing is cheaper and saving less attractive, raising consumption and investment. It could increase the money supply, for example by buying bonds, giving banks more to lend. It might allow the exchange rate to fall, making exports cheaper and imports dearer. These raise aggregate demand, so the price level is less likely to fall.
Q6 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not an increase in the rate of income tax will reduce inflation. (Oct/Nov 2020, P22)
ReminderFor: lower disposable income, lower AD. Against: cost-push if wages rise, effect weak, saving rather than spending, unemployment, time lag.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level and an increase in income tax is contractionary fiscal policy. Higher income tax reduces households' disposable income, so consumption could fall and aggregate demand could shift left. This reduces demand-pull inflation, particularly if the economy is close to full capacity. It is likely to work best when inflation is caused by excess demand. However, if inflation is cost-push, a tax rise will not lower firms' costs, and workers might demand higher wages, which could raise costs further. Households could also cut saving rather than spending, so the effect may be small, and lower demand could raise unemployment. Overall, a tax rise could reduce demand-pull inflation but it is unlikely to help with cost-push inflation, so the effect depends on the cause and the state of the economy.
Q7 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not a cut in the interest rate will reduce inflation. (Oct/Nov 2024, P23)
ReminderA cut normally raises AD, so inflation could rise; but if cost-push or it lowers firms' costs (borrowing costs), or the currency effect... evaluate.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A cut in the interest rate makes borrowing cheaper and saving less attractive. This is likely to raise consumption and investment, so aggregate demand could shift right and demand-pull inflation could increase, so a cut in the interest rate is more likely to raise inflation than reduce it. A lower exchange rate could also raise import prices. However, a cut could reduce firms' borrowing costs, which could lower their costs of production, and investment may raise capacity over time, which could reduce inflation. If the economy is in recession or deflation, a cut could raise demand towards a normal level and avoid deflation. Overall, a cut is more likely to raise inflation in the short run, except where it helps supply or the economy has spare capacity.

Top tips

  • Match policy to cause. Demand-pull: cut demand. Cost-push: cut costs, supply-side, stronger currency.
  • Name the policy precisely. Raise interest rates, cut the money supply, raise taxes, cut spending.
  • Use chains, not lists. Cambridge limits list-style answers to half marks.
  • Evaluate. Unemployment, time lags, borrowers hurt, exports less competitive, wrong cause.
  • To avoid deflation, do the opposite. Cut interest rates, increase the money supply.
