Supply-Side Policy: Effects and Evaluation

5.4.4: the effects of supply-side policy in AD/AS analysis, the short-run and long-run effects, the problems it suits and how to evaluate it. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and past paper questions.

This part in five sentences

On an AD/AS diagram a successful supply-side policy shifts LRAS (and usually SRAS) to the right, so real output rises, the price level falls (or rises by less) and employment increases, which is long-run economic growth with lower inflationary pressure. In the short run, however, much supply-side spending (on education, training, roads) is also government spending, so it raises AD first, which may raise prices before capacity improves. The policy is best suited to structural unemployment, cost-push inflation and long-run growth, and is less suited to cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment, where AD is the problem. Evaluation points are cost and opportunity cost, time lags, uncertainty, side effects (inequality, damage to consumers or the environment) and whether AD is high enough to use the new capacity. A balanced answer compares supply-side policy with fiscal and monetary policy and often concludes that a mix works best.

1 · The effect on the AD/AS diagram

  • LRAS shifts right: potential output rises, so the economy can grow without running into capacity limits.
  • SRAS shifts right: lower unit costs let firms supply more at each price level.
  • Result: real output up, price level down, unemployment (especially structural) down. Compare with demand-side policy, which raises output but also raises prices.
  • Net effect on prices depends on AD: if AD also rises, the price level may stay stable while output grows.

4 · Evaluating supply-side policy

  • Time lags: education and training take years to raise productivity; infrastructure takes years to build.
  • Cost and opportunity cost: the money could be spent elsewhere; large budget deficits may result, especially in countries with a small tax base.
  • Uncertainty: training may not match the skills employers want; workers may not respond; tax cuts may be saved or spent on imports rather than increasing work.
  • Side effects: tax cuts may widen inequality; deregulation may harm consumers or the environment; privatisation may create private monopolies; cutting benefits causes hardship.
  • Demand also matters: extra capacity is only used if AD is high enough; otherwise output stays below potential.
  • Compare with alternatives: fiscal and monetary policy act faster but cannot raise potential output; supply-side policy works over the long run. A mix of policies is usually most effective.
  • Context: in developing economies, funding is the main limit; inward investment and the private sector may help.
Exam tipAlways separate the short-run AD effect from the long-run supply effect. Draw LRAS shifting right, say what happens to output and the price level, and then evaluate with time lags, cost, uncertainty and the type of problem. Conclude with a judgement, ideally a mix of policies.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Roads and training as a package

A government funds new roads and training. At first, spending raises AD and may push up prices; later, better roads and skills lower firms' costs and raise capacity, shifting AS right and holding down prices.

Exam use: the short-run AD effect and long-run AS effect.

Retraining after a factory closes

Workers laid off in a declining industry have skills that no longer match vacancies. Retraining schemes help them move into growing sectors, reducing structural unemployment, but only after months or years.

Exam use: supply-side policy and structural unemployment.

A recession with falling spending

When unemployment is high because spending has collapsed, training alone cannot create jobs. Firms will not hire if they cannot sell, so demand-side policy is also needed.

Exam use: why supply-side policy suits structural, not cyclical, unemployment.

Cost-push inflation and productivity

Energy and wage costs rise, shifting SRAS left. Over the long run, investment in technology and training can raise productivity and reduce unit costs, easing the inflation.

Exam use: supply-side policy and cost-push inflation.

A developing economy with little revenue

A government wants to build schools and roads but has a small tax base. It may seek inward investment or aid, and phases spending, showing how funding limits supply-side policy.

Exam use: cost as a limit.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/23 · Q4(a) 8 marks A government embarks on a supply-side policy involving substantial additional funding for new road schemes and for new training schemes designed to raise labour productivity. Use an AD/AS diagram to explain the effects of this funding on the level of output in the economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): an accurate AD/AS diagram showing national output in equilibrium (2).
  • Application (up to 6): a clear understanding of aggregate demand (1), with the diagram showing that the AD curve shifts right because of the increase in government spending (1) and that this increases national output (1); a clear understanding of aggregate supply (1), with the diagram showing that the AS curve shifts right because of the increase in productivity (1) and that this increases national output (1).
PlanAD/AS diagram. Short run: spending shifts AD right, output up. Long run: productivity shifts AS right, output up again.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

On an AD/AS diagram with the price level and real output on the axes, the initial equilibrium is where AD meets SRAS.

Funding for roads and training is government spending, a component of AD, so AD shifts to the right and national output rises in the short run. Over the longer term, the training raises labour productivity and the roads lower firms' costs, so AS shifts to the right as well, raising national output further and holding down the price level.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/24 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain why expenditure on education and training is a supply-side policy and consider why its impact on the general price level may differ between the short run and the long run.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): supply-side policy consists of government policy tools designed to increase aggregate supply (1); the effect is to shift the aggregate supply curve to the right (1); government spending on education and training should increase worker skills and productivity and so productive capacity, shifting LRAS to the right (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): in the short run, increased government expenditure will increase AD, which may lead to an increase in the general price level (up to 2); in the long run, higher productivity and capacity shift LRAS right, which should lower the general price level (up to 2).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): an overall assessment of the effect of expenditure on education and training on the price level, with a conclusion.
PlanDefine supply-side policy; training raises productivity, LRAS right. Short run: spending raises AD, prices may rise. Long run: LRAS right, prices fall. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Supply-side policy consists of measures designed to increase aggregate supply and shift the AS curve to the right. Spending on education and training raises workers' skills and productivity, which increases productive capacity and shifts LRAS to the right.

In the short run the government spending is an injection that raises AD, so the price level may rise before any extra capacity appears. In the long run, higher productivity lowers unit costs and raises potential output, so the LRAS shift should lower the price level.

Overall the long-run effect on prices is likely to be downward, but the short-run effect could be inflationary if the economy is near full capacity. The net result depends on spare capacity and on how effective the training is.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/23 · Q4(b) 12 marks Discuss the extent to which a supply-side policy involving new road schemes and new training schemes will increase the rate of inflation in the economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the short-run effect: spending raises AD, which may raise prices if there is no spare capacity (up to 4); the long-run effect: higher productivity and capacity raise AS, which reduces inflationary pressure (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on the extent to which the policy increases inflation, depending on spare capacity, time lags and the size of the AS effect, with a conclusion.
PlanShort run: AD up, inflation risk if at capacity. Long run: AS up, price level falls. Judge: depends on spare capacity and time lags.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

In the short run, spending on roads and training is injected into the economy as government spending and raises AD. If the economy has no spare capacity, the rise in AD mainly raises the price level, so inflation could increase. Over time, better roads and a more productive workforce lower costs and raise capacity, so AS shifts right, which reduces inflationary pressure and may lower the price level.

The policy is more likely to raise inflation in the short run, particularly in an economy near full capacity, but the long-run effect is likely to reduce it. Overall the extent of any inflation depends on spare capacity and on how quickly supply improves.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess whether supply-side policy is the most effective way to achieve long-run economic growth.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): how supply-side policies, such as education, training, infrastructure and technology, raise productive capacity and shift LRAS right, giving sustainable growth (up to 4); the limits of supply-side policy (cost, time lags, uncertainty) and alternatives such as expansionary fiscal or monetary policy and their limits (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether supply-side policy is the most effective way to achieve long-run growth, with a conclusion.
PlanSupply-side: LRAS shifts right, sustainable. Limits: lags, cost. Demand-side: raises growth only if spare capacity. Judge: best for long-run growth, with demand support.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Supply-side policies raise productivity and productive capacity, shifting LRAS to the right, so output can grow without inflation. Education, training and infrastructure all do this. They take years to work, cost money and may not be well targeted, so they are uncertain. Expansionary fiscal and monetary policy raise AD and can raise output in the short run, but without spare capacity they only raise prices, so they cannot achieve long-run growth by themselves.

Supply-side policy is the most effective way to achieve long-run growth, because only increases in capacity can sustain higher output. But it needs sufficient demand to use the extra capacity, so a combination of policies is likely to work best.

Based on Feb/March 2025 · 9708/22 · Q5(b) 12 marks Assess the extent to which supply-side policy is the best method to reduce the rate of inflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): how supply-side policy reduces inflation by shifting AS to the right, lowering costs and the price level (up to 4); at least one alternative, such as contractionary monetary or fiscal policy, analysed, with the limits of each (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement depending on the cause of inflation, time lags and the costs of each policy, with a conclusion.
PlanSupply-side: lowers costs, price level falls, long run. Alternatives: monetary, fiscal: quicker but cut output. Judge by cause of inflation and time horizon.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Supply-side policies such as training and investment in technology raise productivity, lower unit costs and shift AS to the right, which reduces the price level without cutting output. They are particularly suited to cost-push inflation. However, they take years to work and are costly. Contractionary monetary policy, through higher interest rates, reduces AD more quickly but cuts output and employment, and fiscal policy has similar effects.

Supply-side policy is likely to be the best long-run method, because it reduces inflation while raising output, but it is too slow to deal with high inflation in the short run, when monetary policy is usually needed. The best choice depends on the cause of inflation and the time available.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess whether supply-side policy is likely to be equally successful in reducing both structural and cyclical unemployment.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the strengths and weaknesses of supply-side policy in solving both cyclical (demand-deficient) and structural unemployment: for example, investment in education, training and infrastructure can address structural problems, but supply-side policies are less successful against cyclical unemployment as they have less impact on AD, unless they involve a direct injection of government spending; other disadvantages include cost and the time taken to be effective. Analysing demand-side policies is acceptable.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether supply-side policy is equally successful for both, with a conclusion.
PlanStructural: retraining, mobility, information, works but slowly. Cyclical: AD is the problem, so supply-side is weak. Judge: not equally successful.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Structural unemployment arises from a mismatch of skills or location, so retraining, better information and investment in infrastructure can help workers move into available jobs, though slowly and at a cost. Cyclical unemployment arises from a lack of AD, so supply-side policies do little to create demand for labour unless the spending itself raises AD. Raising capacity cannot create jobs if firms cannot sell more.

Supply-side policy is therefore not equally successful. It is well suited to structural unemployment and less so to cyclical unemployment, where fiscal or monetary policy to raise AD is more effective. A combination would be best.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/23 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess whether supply-side policy is the only way of reducing long-term unemployment in an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): what is meant by supply-side policy and how it can reduce long-term unemployment, for example by retraining and updating skills, providing information on job vacancies and encouraging business start-ups; the advantages (may be non-inflationary, long term) and disadvantages (a long time to have an effect, opportunity cost, receptiveness of the workforce, may not work depending on the type of unemployment); and the advantages and disadvantages of at least one alternative macroeconomic policy. Maximum Level 3 (lower) if only supply-side policy is analysed.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether supply-side policy is the only way of reducing long-term unemployment, with a conclusion.
PlanSupply-side: retraining, information, start-ups: non-inflationary but slow. Alternatives: fiscal and monetary raise AD. Judge: not the only way; depends on type of unemployment.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Supply-side policies such as retraining, job vacancy information and start-up support may reduce long-term unemployment, particularly if it is structural, without raising inflation. But they are slow, costly and rely on workers responding. Expansionary fiscal or monetary policy could raise AD and create jobs more quickly, especially where unemployment is cyclical, but may raise inflation and does little to fix skill mismatches.

Supply-side policy is not the only way. Which approach is best depends on the cause of the unemployment, and a combination of supply-side and demand-side policy is likely to be most effective.

Supply-side policies could create a better trained and healthier workforce and an environment suited to enterprise, raising productivity and potential output. But they are costly, take years to work, and depend on workers and firms responding.

They can help to lead an economy back into growth over the longer term, but are unlikely to do so quickly, so they may need to be combined with measures that raise demand in the short run.

Top tips

  • Draw LRAS shifting right. Output up, price level down.
  • Separate short run from long run. Spending raises AD first; supply improves later.
  • Say which problem it suits. Structural unemployment, cost-push inflation, long-run growth.
  • Say which it does not suit. Cyclical unemployment: AD is the problem.
  • Evaluate. Time lags, cost, uncertainty, side effects.
  • Compare. Weigh against fiscal and monetary policy.
  • Conclude with a mix. Supply needs demand to be used.
