Opportunity Cost and Decision-Making

How consumers, workers, firms and governments weigh benefits against opportunity cost. Syllabus 1.3.2.

The big idea

Opportunity cost affects every decision. Each decision-maker compares the benefit of a choice with the opportunity cost. If the benefit is greater, they go ahead; if not, they choose something else. As opportunity costs change (prices, wages, incomes), decisions change too.

How it influences decisions

Decision-makerDecisionHow opportunity cost influences it
ConsumersWhat to buy with limited incomeBuying one good means giving up others, so consumers choose what gives the most satisfaction for the money; a price rise raises the opportunity cost of a good, so they may buy less of it
WorkersWhether to work, train, work overtime or retireTraining or study costs lost wages; overtime costs leisure; workers compare the extra pay with what they give up. Higher wages raise the opportunity cost of leisure, so some choose to work more
Producers (firms)What to make, how much to invest, which methodsFunds used on new technology cannot be used for wages or advertising; firms switch to the most profitable use of resources
GovernmentsHow to spend limited tax revenueSpending on healthcare means less on education, defence or transport; governments weigh the benefits to society of each

Changes in opportunity cost

  • If the price of a good rises, the opportunity cost of buying it rises, so quantity demanded tends to fall.
  • If wages rise, the opportunity cost of leisure or study rises, so more people may work rather than study.
  • If the price of one crop rises, the opportunity cost of growing a different one rises, so farmers switch.
  • If a government needs to fund defence in an emergency, the opportunity cost is less spending on other services.

Good economic decisions weigh benefits against opportunity cost. Mistakes happen when decisions ignore what is given up.

Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain the influence of opportunity cost on consumers' decisions. (Feb/Mar 2021, P12 style)
ReminderLimited income; giving up other goods; compare satisfaction; price changes.
Consumers have limited incomes, so buying one good means giving up others. They compare the satisfaction from each and choose the option that gives the most. If the price of a good rises, the opportunity cost of buying it rises, so they may buy less of it.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain, using an example, the influence of opportunity cost on government decision-making. (Oct/Nov 2021, P12 style)
ReminderLimited revenue; spending on one project means not on another; example: hospital vs school.
A government has a limited tax revenue, so spending on one project means less is available for others. For example, building a new hospital may mean a new school cannot be built. The government compares the benefits to society of each and chooses the one with the greater benefit.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain two possible opportunity costs for firms if they invest in the latest technology. (May/June 2024, P22 style)
ReminderAdvertising, higher wages, dividends, other projects.
One opportunity cost is spending on advertising. The money used for technology cannot be used to promote the firm's products. Another is higher wages for staff. The funds cannot be used to raise pay or recruit more workers.
Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain how opportunity cost will influence a government's decision on whether to spend tax revenue on a new road. (Oct/Nov 2023, P12 style)
ReminderOther uses of the revenue; compare benefits; choose.
Spending the revenue on a road means it cannot be used for something else, such as healthcare or education. The government has to compare the benefits of the road with the benefits of the alternatives. If the benefits of the road are greater than the benefits of the next best use, it is more likely to go ahead.
Q5 Analyse 6 marks Analyse, using a production possibility curve, the opportunity cost to an economy of producing more consumer goods. (May/June 2022, P22 style)
ReminderMore consumer goods means fewer capital goods; movement along PPC.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A PPC shows the maximum combinations of two types of goods an economy can produce with its resources. Moving along the PPC to produce more consumer goods means resources are shifted from capital goods production. The opportunity cost is the capital goods given up, which could reduce future growth. If the PPC is curved, the opportunity cost rises as more consumer goods are produced.

Top tips

  • Always name the alternative given up. Be specific to the context.
  • Compare benefits with opportunity cost. That is how decisions are made.
  • Use an example. Hospital vs school; study vs wages; machines vs advertising.
  • Say how a change alters the decision. A price rise raises the opportunity cost.
  • Limited resources first. Then choice, then opportunity cost.
