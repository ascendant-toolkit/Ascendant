Measurement of Unemployment

The Labour Force Survey and the claimant count, the unemployment rate formula, and why the figures can mislead. Syllabus 4.6.2.

The big idea

Unemployment is measured in two main ways: the Labour Force Survey (LFS) and the claimant count. The key statistic is the unemployment rate: the number of unemployed as a percentage of the labour force. Each method has strengths and weaknesses, so official figures may not tell the whole story.

The unemployment rate

Unemployment rate = (number unemployed ÷ labour force) × 100

Worked example (Australia, 2021): 0.56 million unemployed in a labour force of 14.0 million. 0.56 ÷ 14.0 × 100 = 4%. The rate is a percentage of the labour force, not of the whole population.

Two ways to measure

Labour Force Survey

A sample of households is asked questions: do you have a job? Have you looked for work in the last few weeks? Are you available to start? Those who say yes to the last two but have no job count as unemployed. It follows an international (ILO) definition.

  • For: includes everyone looking for work, whether or not they claim benefit; allows comparison between countries.
  • Against: based on a sample, so there are errors; costly; people may not answer truthfully.

Claimant count

Counts the people who are claiming unemployment benefit.

  • For: quick and cheap, from administrative records.
  • Against: misses people who are unemployed but not entitled to or not claiming benefit; includes some who are working and still claim; changes in benefit rules change the figures.

Why the figure may be wrong

  • Hidden unemployment: discouraged workers who have stopped looking are not counted.
  • Underemployment: part-time workers who want full-time hours are counted as employed.
  • Informal economy: unrecorded work, and sometimes people claiming benefit while working.
  • Definitions and sample errors differ between countries.

Interpreting a change: if the unemployment rate falls, it could be because more people found jobs, or because people left the labour force.

Q1 Identify 2 marks Identify two ways unemployment is measured. (Oct/Nov 2021, P23; Oct/Nov 2024, P21)
ReminderName both: claimant count and labour force survey.
The claimant count (number claiming unemployment benefit). The labour force survey (a survey of people without a job who are looking for work).
Q2 Calculate 1 marks In 2021 Australia had 0.56 million unemployed in a labour force of 14.0 million. Calculate the unemployment rate. (May/June 2023, P21)
ReminderUnemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
0.56 ÷ 14.0 × 100 = 4%.
Q3 Define 2 marks Define unemployment rate. (Feb/Mar 2024, P22)
ReminderFormula or words: unemployed as a percentage of the labour force.
The unemployment rate is the number of unemployed divided by the labour force, multiplied by 100 (the percentage of the labour force without a job).
Q4 Explain 2 marks Explain what is meant by an unemployment rate of 24%. (May/June 2020, P23)
ReminderUse the definition: 24 out of every 100 in the labour force.
24% of the labour force is without a job but is willing and able to work, so about 24 out of every 100 people in the labour force are unemployed.
Q5 Explain 4 marks Explain two reasons why official unemployment figures may understate the true level.
ReminderHidden unemployment, underemployment, claimant count gaps. Each with a link.
Some people become discouraged and stop looking for work. They are no longer counted as unemployed even though they would work if jobs were available, so the measured rate is lower. The claimant count misses people who are not eligible for benefit. Young people or partners of earners may be unemployed without claiming, so the figure could be too low.

Top tips

  • Two measures: labour force survey and claimant count. Learn one strength and weakness of each.
  • Rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100. Not ÷ population.
  • Show working in calculations. Even for 1 mark, write the formula.
  • A falling rate may not mean more jobs. Discouraged workers leave the labour force.
