Division of Labour, Specialisation and the Entrepreneur

1.3.4 to 1.3.5: division of labour and specialisation with their advantages and disadvantages, and the role of the entrepreneur in a contemporary economy. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Division of labour splits production into separate tasks, each done by a different worker, and specialisation means individuals, firms, regions or countries concentrate on the activities they do best. Specialising raises productivity and output and lowers average cost, because workers gain skill, save time switching and can use machines, but it can also cause boredom, loss of wider skills and dependence on other producers. The entrepreneur organises the other factors of production and takes the risk of production; in a contemporary economy entrepreneurs start new firms, create jobs, innovate and are rewarded with profit if they succeed.

1 · Division of labour and specialisation (1.3.4)

  • Division of labour: a production process is divided into many tasks, each done by a different worker. For example, in a factory making chairs, one worker cuts, another assembles and another paints.
  • Specialisation: a worker, firm, region or country concentrates on a limited range of tasks or products. Specialisation by countries follows comparative advantage (syllabus 6.1).
  • Specialisation needs exchange: people no longer make everything they need, so they sell their output for money and buy what they want.
  • Division of labour is more effective in a large market with stable demand, where the extra output can be sold. In a small market that wants custom-made goods it is less useful.

2 · The entrepreneur (1.3.5)

  • The entrepreneur has two core functions: organising (taking decisions, combining and coordinating the factors of production) and taking risk (investing money and time without knowing whether the product will earn a profit).
  • Risk and uncertainty: the entrepreneur pays wages, rent and interest in advance and gets paid only if sales cover costs. The reward for success is profit; the penalty for failure is a loss.
  • In contemporary economies entrepreneurs innovate (new products and methods), create jobs, introduce competition, meet changing needs quickly and so can increase the productive potential and long-run growth of an economy.
  • Encouraging entrepreneurship (supply-side policy): subsidies and start-up grants for small firms, lower taxes on business profits, training in management skills, easier access to finance, and less regulation. These have costs and may be slow to work.
  • Not every manager is an entrepreneur: a hired manager organises resources but does not bear the risk of loss.
Exam tipFor the entrepreneur, always give both functions: organising the other factors and taking risk. Each is worth a mark.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A modern car assembly line

Each worker or robot on the line does a small task such as fitting doors or installing seats. Cars are built far faster than if one worker built a whole car. The disadvantages include repetitive work, and when one station breaks down the whole line may stop.

Exam use: division of labour in a factory with both its advantages and its drawbacks.

A country that specialises in financial services

A small country may build a reputation as a banking centre. It benefits from expertise and low transport costs for online services, but depends heavily on one industry. If the financial sector suffers a crisis, the whole economy is hit.

Exam use: specialisation at the level of a country and the case for diversification.

Start-ups and risk

Most new businesses fail within a few years. Founders put in their own money and time and have no guaranteed income. Those who succeed, such as the founders of successful technology firms, earn very large profits, which is the reward for taking the risk.

Exam use: the entrepreneur's risk-taking and the link between risk and profit.

Policy support for entrepreneurs

Governments offer start-up grants, tax breaks for new firms, business training and loans guaranteed by the state. The aim is to raise the supply of enterprise, create jobs and raise the long-run growth rate, though the money has an opportunity cost.

Exam use: supply-side policy to increase entrepreneurship.

Based on Feb/March 2024 · 9708/22 · Q1(e) 6 marks A country is investing heavily in artificial intelligence (AI). Assess the likely impact of the growth of AI on the specialisation and trade of a country such as Japan.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Up to 3 marks: benefits of AI for specialisation and trade, such as higher productivity, lower costs and prices, development of comparative advantage and better international competitiveness.
  • Up to 3 marks: drawbacks, such as over-specialisation, over-reliance on one industry or on non-renewable resources, and structural unemployment.
  • Maximum of 5 marks for the points; 1 mark reserved for an evaluated conclusion.
PlanBenefits: productivity, costs, comparative advantage. Costs: over-dependence, jobs lost. Conclude.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

AI could raise productivity and lower the cost of producing goods and services in industries where Japan specialises, such as electronics and cars, which could strengthen its comparative advantage and increase exports.

However, relying heavily on AI-based industries could lead to over-specialisation, so the economy might be badly affected if demand or technology changes. Workers whose tasks are automated might face structural unemployment. Overall AI could improve trade performance, but the gains might depend on how well displaced workers are retrained and how diversified the economy remains.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/22 · Q3(b) 12 marks Entrepreneurs are vital in a modern economy to achieve long-run economic growth. Assess whether encouraging entrepreneurship or increasing another factor of production will be a more effective way to achieve long-run economic growth.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): entrepreneurs organise the other factors and take risks, and profit motivates them; their creativity and innovation can raise the productive potential of the economy (a shift of the PPC); modern economies are dynamic and need people who can respond quickly.
  • Costs and limits: it costs money to train entrepreneurs; success may be low; other factors such as subsidies and the quality of other factors matter; risk-takers may seek short-run gain.
  • Compare with increasing one other factor (land, labour or capital), including the advantages and disadvantages and availability of the other factors; a one-sided answer cannot score above Level 2.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): compare the importance of entrepreneurship with one other factor and conclude which is most important.
PlanCase for entrepreneurship. Case for one other factor (for example capital). Compare. Conclude.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Entrepreneurs organise land, labour and capital and bear the risk of production, and the profit motive encourages them to succeed. By innovating they can create new products and methods, which raises the productive potential of the economy and shifts the PPC outwards, increasing long-run growth. Policies to encourage entrepreneurship, such as start-up grants or tax breaks, may increase the number of new firms and jobs. But they cost money, many start-ups fail and risk-takers might seek short-term gains rather than long-term growth.

Increasing capital, for example investment in machinery and infrastructure, raises productivity and can also shift the PPC outwards, but it needs savings and may take time, and without entrepreneurs to organise it the capital may be poorly used. Overall entrepreneurship is probably the factor that makes the others effective, but it works best with good-quality capital and labour. So the most effective approach may be to encourage enterprise alongside investment in human and physical capital.

Based on Feb/March 2021 · 9708/22 · Q2(b) 12 marks Discuss the view that supply-side policies to increase the supply of labour to an economy are likely to be more successful than those designed to increase the supply of entrepreneurship.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis of at least two measures to increase the supply of labour: training, relaxing immigration controls, cutting income tax, raising the retirement age (up to 4; maximum 3 if only one measure).
  • Analysis of at least two measures to increase entrepreneurship: management training, lower business taxes, subsidies and start-up grants (up to 4; maximum 3 if only one).
  • Evaluation: which are likely to be more successful (3) and a reasoned conclusion (1).
PlanLabour measures (2). Enterprise measures (2). Compare success and time lags. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Policies to increase the supply of labour include training to help people enter the labour force, relaxing immigration controls and cutting income tax to raise the reward from working. These could raise the size and skills of the workforce, and productive capacity. Policies to increase entrepreneurship include management training, lower taxes on business profits and start-up grants, which could create new firms and jobs.

Which is more successful depends on the economy. If there are many unemployed workers but few new businesses, boosting enterprise may be more effective, since firms create the jobs. The effects of training take years, and entrepreneurship policy has a high failure rate. Overall policies for labour may give more reliable gains, while enterprise policies may bring bigger but riskier rewards.

Top tips

  • Define division of labour precisely. Splitting production into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.
  • Give the mechanism for productivity. Skill from practice, no time lost switching, and use of machines.
  • Always give a disadvantage. Boredom, loss of skills, interdependence, structural unemployment.
  • Two functions of the entrepreneur. Organises the other factors and takes the risk.
  • Link profit to risk. Profit is the reward for bearing risk; a loss is the penalty.
  • For a country, link to comparative advantage. Specialise where the opportunity cost is lower, but keep diversification in mind.
  • Compare in 12 mark answers. Weigh entrepreneurs against one other factor and conclude.
