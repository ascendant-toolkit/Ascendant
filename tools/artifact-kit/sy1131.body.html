Classifying Economies by Development and Income

11.3.1 to 11.3.2: how economies are grouped by level of development and by national income, why the groups matter and where they mislead. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Economies are grouped by level of development (developed, emerging and newly industrialised, developing, least developed), using features such as output per head, health, education, the structure of the economy and trade, and by level of national income, mainly the World Bank's four groups based on gross national income (GNI) per capita: low, lower-middle, upper-middle and high. Income is easy to measure, so it is used for lending and aid decisions, but it says little about distribution, health, education or sustainability. Development means improvements in people's well-being and choices, whereas growth is a rise in real output, so countries with the same income can sit at very different levels of development.

1 · Classification by level of development (11.3.1)

  • The UN Human Development Index (HDI) also groups countries as very high, high, medium or low human development, using income, health and education together.
  • Economists often compare developed and developing economies using the indicators in 11.3.3 and the characteristics in 11.4: population, income distribution and economic structure.
  • Development is not the same as growth. Growth is a rise in real GDP. Development adds improvements in life expectancy, health, schooling, poverty reduction, freedom and sustainability. A country can grow without developing (for example when gains go to a few or the environment is damaged).

2 · Classification by level of national income (11.3.2)

  • The World Bank ranks countries by GNI per capita (using an Atlas exchange rate to smooth movements) into low, lower-middle, upper-middle and high income. The cut-offs are updated every year for inflation, and in recent years have been roughly $1,200, $4,500 and $14,000. Always say “roughly” and do not quote exact thresholds in an exam.
  • Countries move between groups as incomes rise (or fall). For example, Bangladesh moved from the low-income to the lower-middle-income group in the mid-2010s.
  • Uses: to compare countries, to set eligibility for concessional loans and aid, to monitor progress and to target policy.
  • Limitations: GNI per capita is an average that hides inequality; exchange rates and prices differ (so PPP may be better for living standards); some oil-rich countries have high incomes but weaker health and education; groups are wide, so countries in the same group may be very different; the thresholds are somewhat arbitrary.
Income group versus development groupThe two systems overlap but are not the same. A high-income oil exporter may have a lower HDI than an upper-middle income country with strong health and education. Use this contrast as evaluation when asked whether income is a good way to classify countries.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Bangladesh moves up

Bangladesh grew steadily for decades, helped by garment exports, remittances and rising productivity, and moved from the World Bank's low-income group to lower-middle income in the mid-2010s. Its HDI also rose, though many workers remain in low-paid, informal jobs.

Exam use: a country changing income group and the difference between growth and development.

South Korea's rise

In the 1960s South Korea was among the poorer economies. Rapid investment, education and exporting of manufactured goods moved it to high-income status within a few decades, and it is often described as a newly industrialised economy that became developed.

Exam use: emerging economies, structural change and how a country can move between groups.

High income, uneven development

Some oil-exporting countries have very high GNI per capita because of resource revenue, yet in the past have had weaker education, health or political freedoms than countries with similar incomes. Income alone does not show how well the money is used.

Exam use: why income classification is limited and why HDI is also used.

The least developed countries

The UN identifies a group of least developed countries, mostly in Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia, that have low incomes, low human capital and high vulnerability to shocks. They receive special support in trade and aid.

Exam use: the difference between a developing and a least developed country, and how classifications guide aid.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/42 · Q6(a) and May/June 2022 · 9708/42 · Q6(a) 12 marks Compare the characteristics of developed and developing countries.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Develop at least three characteristics, saying how each differs. The mark scheme lists population growth and structure, income distribution, economic structure and employment composition, external trade, urbanisation, GDP per head, education, health care, foreign debt and infrastructure.
  • Level 4 (9 to 12) needs three or more characteristics explained and analysed, including the link between them (for example low income, low savings and low investment).
  • Diagrams are credited where they help, for example a Lorenz curve or a population pyramid.
PlanPick three or four: income per head, population, structure of production and employment, trade, income distribution. For each: developed versus developing, and why.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Developed economies have high real income per head, while developing economies have low income per head, which means less is available for saving, investment and health or education spending. Population also differs. Developing countries often have higher birth rates and a young population with a high dependency ratio, so more spending goes on supporting dependants and less on investment. Developed economies usually have low birth and death rates and an ageing population.

The structure of production differs. Developing economies often employ a large share of workers in the primary sector, often with low productivity, whereas developed economies have a large tertiary sector. This links to trade: developing countries may export primary products, whose prices are volatile, while developed countries export manufactures and services, which may have higher value added. These groups are broad, so some emerging economies show features of both.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/41 · Q7(a) 12 marks Developing countries have low levels of productivity and a high dependency ratio. Explain the meaning of these two characteristics and consider whether they are linked.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Productivity: output per unit of input, such as output per worker; the dependency ratio: the number of dependants (young and old) per person of working age.
  • Links: low productivity gives low incomes, which restricts spending on education and health, so productivity stays low; a high dependency ratio lowers saving and investment per head, reducing capital and productivity.
  • Evaluation: the links are not automatic; for example, other factors such as technology and institutions affect productivity, and a young population may become a future benefit (demographic dividend).
PlanDefine both terms. Link 1: productivity to income to human capital. Link 2: dependency to saving and investment. Judge: linked but not the only cause.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Productivity is output per worker or per unit of input. The dependency ratio is the number of people not of working age, such as children and the elderly, relative to those of working age. The two may be linked because a high dependency ratio means that a large share of income is spent on supporting dependants. This could reduce saving and investment, so each worker has less capital and productivity may be lower. Low productivity then means low incomes and less spending on schools and health, which can keep productivity low.

However, productivity also depends on technology, infrastructure and institutions, so the link may not be the main cause. A young population could also raise future output if education improves.

Practice question · modelled on the 8 mark style (no past paper) 8 marks Explain two limitations of using GNI per capita to classify economies.
What the mark scheme wants
  • An average that hides income inequality, so many people may be much poorer than the figure suggests.
  • Exchange rates and price levels differ, so GNI at market exchange rates may not show real living standards; it ignores non-income factors (health, education, environment) and unrecorded activity.
  • Each limitation developed with a reason and an example.
PlanLimitation 1 with a chain. Limitation 2 with a chain.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

GNI per capita is an average, found by dividing total income by population. It does not show how income is shared. In a country with a high Gini coefficient a small group may take most of the income, so the typical citizen could be much worse off than the average suggests, and the country could be placed in a higher group than most people's experience.

It also ignores non-income aspects of development. Two countries with the same GNI per head may have very different life expectancy, schooling and environmental quality, so the classification may give a misleading picture of development. Prices also differ, so converting at the market exchange rate may understate real living standards in low-price economies.

Top tips

  • Compare, do not list. For each characteristic say how developed and developing economies differ and why.
  • Use three or four characteristics. Income per head, population, structure of production, trade, income distribution.
  • Say 'roughly' for thresholds. World Bank cut-offs change every year. The idea matters, not the number.
  • Growth is not development. Growth is higher real GDP. Development is better lives: health, education, poverty, freedom.
  • Link the features. Low income leads to low saving, low investment, low productivity, so low income again.
  • Evaluate classifications. Averages hide inequality; groups are broad; some high-income countries have weaker development.
  • Use the HDI groups too. Very high, high, medium and low human development.
