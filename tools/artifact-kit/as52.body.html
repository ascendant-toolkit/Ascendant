Taxation: Types, Rates and Reasons

5.2.4: direct and indirect, progressive, regressive and proportional taxes, marginal and average rates of taxation, and the reasons for taxation. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Governments tax to raise revenue to pay for public goods and services, to redistribute income, to discourage demerit goods and correct externalities, and to influence aggregate demand. Direct taxes (income, corporation, inheritance) are paid straight to the government by the person or firm they are levied on, while indirect taxes (VAT, duties) are paid on spending and collected by sellers; a tax can be progressive (the share of income taken rises with income), regressive (it falls) or proportional (it is constant). The marginal rate of taxation (MRT) is the percentage of extra income taken in tax, while the average rate (ART) is the percentage of total income taken; under a progressive tax the MRT is above the ART. When raising revenue, governments weigh how fair, efficient, easy to collect and certain a tax is, and its effects on incentives and on AD.

2 · Marginal and average rates (5.2.4)

  • MRT = change in tax ÷ change in income × 100. Example: income rises $100 and $40 more is taken in tax: MRT = 40%.
  • ART = total tax ÷ total income × 100. Example: income $10,000 and tax $3,000: ART = 30%.
  • Progressive: MRT > ART, so ART rises with income. Regressive: MRT < ART. Proportional: MRT = ART.
  • A high MRT can reduce the incentive to earn extra income, which is a possible disincentive effect.

3 · Reasons for taxation (5.2.4)

  • Raise revenue to fund public goods, merit goods and transfer payments.
  • Redistribute income and wealth through progressive taxes and benefits.
  • Discourage consumption of demerit goods and correct externalities, such as taxes on tobacco, alcohol, sugar and carbon.
  • Influence AD: higher taxes reduce disposable income and spending (contractionary), lower taxes increase them (expansionary).
  • Protect domestic industries through tariffs on imports.

4 · Worked examples

An income tax with three bands. The first $12,000 of income is tax-free, income from $12,000 to $40,000 is taxed at 20%, and income above $40,000 is taxed at 40%.

$12,000$00%0%
$30,00020% × $18,000 = $3,60012%20%
$40,00020% × $28,000 = $5,60014%20%
$60,000$5,600 + 40% × $20,000 = $13,60022.7%40%
$100,000$5,600 + 40% × $60,000 = $29,60029.6%40%
  • The tax is progressive: the average rate rises with income, and the marginal rate is always at or above the average rate.
  • When income rises from $40,000 to $60,000, the extra tax is $8,000 (13,600 − 5,600), so the MRT = 8,000 ÷ 20,000 = 40%. A worker keeps only 60 cents of each extra dollar, which may reduce the incentive to work more.

A regressive indirect tax. A 10% sales tax is paid on spending. A household with income of $15,000 spends $14,000 and pays $1,400 (9.3% of income). A household with income of $120,000 spends $70,000 and pays $7,000 (5.8% of income). The ART falls as income rises, so the tax is regressive, although it has the same rate on spending.

  • Developing economies often rely more on indirect taxes and trade taxes because incomes are low and many people work informally, which makes income tax hard to collect.
  • Whether an indirect tax raises more revenue depends on the price elasticity of demand for the taxed goods: with inelastic demand, quantity falls little and revenue rises most.

6 · The features of a good tax

  • Equity (fairness): people should pay according to their ability to pay (vertical equity) and people in similar circumstances should pay the same (horizontal equity).
  • Efficiency: low cost of collection and little distortion of work, saving and spending choices.
  • Certainty: taxpayers should know what they have to pay and when.
  • Convenience: easy to pay.
  • Flexibility: revenue should respond to the economy and allow governments to change rates.
  • No tax is best on all criteria, so governments use a mix of taxes.

7 · Taxes and the macroeconomy

  • Higher taxes reduce disposable income, consumption and AD: they are contractionary. Lower taxes raise AD: they are expansionary.
  • Supply-side effects: lower income tax may encourage work; lower business taxes may encourage investment.
  • Automatic stabilisers: progressive taxes take less revenue in a recession and more in a boom, which cushions the economy without any decision.
  • Redistribution: progressive taxes, with benefits, reduce inequality.
Do not write thisProgressive means paid by high earners only: lower earners may pay too, but a smaller proportion. Do not confuse a progressive tax with a direct tax, or a regressive tax with an indirect tax (most indirect taxes are regressive but this is not a definition). Do not use the marginal rate as the average rate.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Income tax bands

A worker earns $50,000. The first $10,000 is tax free, the next $20,000 is taxed at 20% and the rest at 40%. The total tax is $12,000 (average rate 24%), but the tax on the next dollar is 40% (the marginal rate).

Exam use: marginal versus average tax rates.

Tax on tobacco

A duty on cigarettes raises large revenue because demand is inelastic, and also discourages smoking. But it is regressive, because poorer households spend a higher share of their income on tobacco.

Exam use: indirect taxes: revenue, demerit goods and regressivity.

Raising taxes in a boom

When the economy is overheating and inflation is rising, a government may raise taxes. Disposable income and spending fall, AD shifts left, and inflationary pressure eases.

Exam use: contractionary fiscal policy.

Tax avoidance

High marginal rates give wealthy people an incentive to move income abroad or into lightly taxed forms, so the revenue raised may be less than expected.

Exam use: a limit of direct taxes.

Income tax bands

A government taxes the first slice of income at 0%, the next at 20% and income above a threshold at 40%. A worker earning just above the threshold pays 40% on only the extra income, not on all of it.

Exam use: marginal and average rates in a progressive tax.

VAT in low-income countries

Where many people are paid in cash and do not file tax returns, a sales tax collected at the till raises more revenue than an income tax. Poorer households, however, pay a larger share of income.

Exam use: why indirect taxes are common in developing economies, and their regressive effect.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/22 · Q5(a) 8 marks With the help of examples, explain the difference between the marginal rate of taxation and the average rate of taxation and consider whether a government should decide to increase the rate of indirect tax to raise additional revenue.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): the marginal rate of tax is the proportion of extra income taken in tax (1); the average rate is the proportion of the whole of income that is taxed (1); an accurate example of each, for example an extra £100 with £40 taken as tax is a marginal rate of 40%, and total earnings of £10 000 with £3000 tax is an average rate of 30% (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): discussion focuses on whether the gains in revenue exceed the disadvantages of indirect taxes: they are easier and quicker to put in place than direct taxes; they may be less easy to avoid; but they are regressive, may raise prices and inflation, and reduce spending.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a judgement on whether indirect tax should be increased, with a conclusion.
PlanMRT: tax on extra income, with example. ART: total tax ÷ total income, with example. Indirect tax: easy to raise, hard to avoid, but regressive and inflationary. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The marginal rate of taxation is the proportion of extra income taken in tax: if someone earns an extra $100 and $40 is taxed, the MRT is 40%. The average rate is total tax divided by total income: if total earnings are $10,000 and tax is $3,000, the ART is 30%.

Raising indirect taxes such as VAT can bring in revenue quickly, is relatively hard to avoid and does not reduce the incentive to work. But indirect taxes are usually regressive, because low-income households spend a larger share of income, they raise prices and can add to inflation, and they reduce spending on the taxed goods.

An increase in indirect tax may be a reasonable way to raise revenue if demand for the taxed goods is inelastic and some necessities are exempt, but if equity matters more, increasing progressive direct taxes may be fairer. The best choice depends on the government's aims.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/21 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain the reasons why governments impose taxes and consider whether an increase in income tax is fairer than an increase in sales taxes.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): a clear understanding of the various reasons why governments impose taxes.
  • Analysis (up to 3): the distinction between a direct tax, such as income tax, and an indirect tax, such as a sales tax, with appropriate reference to fairness.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a valid judgement on whether an increase in income tax is fairer than an increase in sales taxes, with a conclusion.
PlanReasons: revenue, redistribution, discourage demerit goods, influence AD. Income tax progressive; sales tax regressive. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Governments impose taxes to raise revenue for public services, to redistribute income, to discourage consumption of demerit goods and to influence aggregate demand.

An increase in income tax usually falls more heavily on those with higher incomes, because the marginal rate is above the average rate, which makes it progressive and generally seen as fairer. Sales taxes are indirect and tend to be regressive, because poorer households spend a larger share of their income on goods and services.

On grounds of fairness an increase in income tax is probably fairer, but it may reduce the incentive to work and can be avoided, while sales taxes are easier to collect. The answer depends on whether fairness or simplicity matters more.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/22 · Q1(e) 6 marks Assess whether adjusting the collection of direct tax or indirect tax is likely to be more effective in increasing the tax revenue collected by a government.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 4): one method of adjusting direct tax, such as introducing a more progressive income tax, with the advantages and disadvantages for raising revenue (up to 3; 1 mark if only advantages or only disadvantages); one method of adjusting indirect tax, such as raising the rate of VAT, with the advantages and disadvantages (up to 3; 1 mark if one-sided); a maximum of 3 marks if only one type of tax is analysed. Issues include fairness and equality, the amount that may be raised and the costs of ensuring compliance.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): compare and weigh up the likely effectiveness of both types of tax in raising revenue, considering the problems the government faces in raising more, and reach an overall conclusion on which is more effective.
PlanDirect: progressive rates raise revenue from high earners, avoidance, incentives. Indirect: VAT wide base, easy to collect, regressive. Compare and conclude.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A more progressive income tax would raise more revenue from higher earners, which is fairer, but high marginal rates may reduce work effort, encourage avoidance, and in a country with a large informal sector few people would pay. Raising VAT would raise revenue from all spending and is easy to collect, but it is regressive, hitting poorer households hardest, and could raise inflation and reduce demand. In a country with a large informal economy, indirect taxes are probably more effective at raising revenue because they are easier to collect, although direct taxes are fairer. A combination is likely to work best.

Top tips

  • Give examples of MRT and ART. Use numbers: extra $100, $40 tax.
  • MRT is tax on extra income. ART is total tax ÷ total income.
  • Progressive: MRT above ART. Regressive: MRT below ART.
  • Direct versus indirect. Who pays and how it is collected.
  • List several reasons for tax. Revenue, redistribution, demerit goods, AD.
  • Evaluate indirect taxes. Easy to collect but regressive and may raise prices.
  • Mention incentives. High MRT can discourage work.
