Indirect Taxes, Subsidies and Tradable Permits

The price-based tools for market failure: how a tax shifts supply and who bears it, how a subsidy works, and how permits cap pollution at the lowest cost. Animated diagrams built from numbers, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

An indirect tax raises the cost of supply, so price rises and quantity falls, and an external cost can be corrected if the tax equals it. A subsidy lowers the cost of supply, so price falls and quantity rises, which suits goods with external benefits. Tradable permits fix the total amount of pollution and let firms trade the right to emit, so the cap is met at the lowest cost. All three use the price mechanism rather than orders, but each depends on the government getting the size or the cap right.

1 · Indirect taxes and who pays

A per-unit tax shifts the supply curve up by the amount of the tax. The price consumers pay rises, the price producers receive falls, and the quantity falls. The incidence (who bears the burden) depends on elasticities.

  • If demand is inelastic (steep), consumers bear most of the tax and quantity falls little.
  • If supply is inelastic, producers bear most of it.
  • A tax on a demerit good with inelastic demand raises revenue but cuts consumption little. It is also often regressive (takes a bigger share of low incomes).

A subsidy works the other way: supply shifts down, the price falls and the quantity rises. The cost to the government is the subsidy per unit times the quantity.

3 · Subsidies

A per-unit subsidy shifts the supply curve down by the subsidy. In the market with demand P = 100 − Q and supply P = 20 + Q, a $20 subsidy moves the equilibrium from 40 units at $60 to 50 units: consumers pay $50, producers receive $70, and the cost to the government is $20 x 50 = $1000. The gain is shared, here equally, and again the split depends on elasticities.

  • Why: positive externalities and merit goods are under-consumed, so a subsidy moves output towards the social optimum.
  • Costs: paid by taxpayers (an opportunity cost), may go to people who would have bought anyway, and is hard to set at the right level.

4 · Tradable pollution permits (cap and trade)

The government sets a cap on total emissions and issues permits. Firms that can cut pollution cheaply sell permits to firms for which cutting is costly. The total cap is met, but at lower total cost than forcing every firm to cut by the same amount. Over time the cap can be lowered.

  • With no trading, each of two firms must cut by 5 units. A cuts a unit for $2 and B for $8, so the total cost is 5 x 2 + 5 x 8 = $50.
  • With trading, A cuts all 10 units for $20 and sells its spare permits to B, which avoids the dearer cuts. The cap is the same, but the total cost is $20.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Taxes on tobacco and sugary drinks

Governments tax cigarettes heavily and several countries tax sugary drinks. Demand for cigarettes is inelastic, so the tax raises large revenue but may cut smoking only modestly. Much of the burden falls on lower-income smokers.

Exam use: a tax on a demerit good. Link the effect to elasticity, mention regressivity, and compare with information campaigns.

Emissions trading

The European Union runs a cap and trade scheme covering power stations and heavy industry. Firms that cut emissions cheaply can sell spare permits. The cap is tightened over time.

Exam use: tradable permits. Say the total is capped and the cost of cutting is minimised. Evaluate: the cap may be set too high, and permit prices may swing.

Subsidies for electric vehicles and solar panels

Many governments have paid part of the cost of electric cars or solar panels to speed up a switch away from fossil fuels. Prices to buyers are lower and demand is higher, but taxpayers pay, and some buyers would have bought anyway.

Exam use: a subsidy for a good with positive externalities. Evaluate: cost, who benefits, and whether the subsidy equals the external benefit.

Congestion and parking permits

Some cities have tried tradable parking permits or congestion charges to cut road use. The idea is to put a price on a cost drivers otherwise ignore.

Exam use: a Paper 3 question asked why tradable parking permits might fail: if too few households own extra cars, the permit market is very small.

A · Practice question with a model answer

Practice (not a past paper question) 12 marks Using a diagram, explain who is likely to bear most of an indirect tax on cigarettes.
PlanDefine an indirect tax and show S shifting up. Mark the price consumers pay, the price producers receive and the tax revenue. Link the split to elasticity. Evaluate.
KANE

An indirect tax is a tax on spending, charged per unit on producers, which raises their cost of supply, so the supply curve shifts up by the amount of the tax.

Demand for cigarettes is likely to be price inelastic because they are addictive and have few substitutes, so the demand curve is steep. When supply shifts up, the price rises by a large part of the tax, and the quantity falls by only a small amount. Smokers are therefore likely to bear most of the tax, and producers keep a larger share of the price, while the government collects large revenue because so many units are still sold.

The split could differ in the long run, because demand may become more elastic as people quit or switch to alternatives, and because tax is often a larger share of low incomes. Overall, consumers are likely to bear most of the burden, which suits raising revenue more than cutting consumption.

Top tips

  • Draw the tax as a shift of supply up by the tax, then label the price consumers pay, the price producers receive, the new quantity and the revenue box.
  • Talk about elasticity and incidence. Inelastic demand means little change in quantity and more of the burden on consumers.
  • A tax equal to the external cost internalises it. Say so, and then say that the government may not know the right figure.
  • For a subsidy, shift supply down and show the cost as subsidy times quantity. Link it to positive externalities and merit goods.
  • For permits, say the total is capped and the cost of cutting is minimised. Evaluate the level of the cap and permit price swings.
  • Compare the tools in evaluation: cost, revenue, certainty about the outcome and who is affected.
