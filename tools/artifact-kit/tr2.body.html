Reasons for Trade Restrictions

Why governments restrict trade: infant, declining and strategic industries, dumping, the current account, tax revenue, demerit goods and the environment. Syllabus 6.2.5.

The big idea

Governments restrict free trade for eight main reasons: to protect infant (sunrise) industries, to protect declining (sunset) industries, to protect strategic industries, to avoid dumping, to reduce a deficit on the current account, to raise tax revenue, to restrict imports of demerit goods and to promote environmental sustainability. For each reason, explain what the government is trying to achieve, how the restriction helps, and the possible problems.

The eight reasons

ReasonWhy a government may restrict tradePossible problem
Protect infant (sunrise) industriesNew industries are small and have high costs; they need time to grow, gain skills and economies of scale before facing foreign rivalsMay never become efficient; consumers pay more meanwhile
Protect declining (sunset) industriesKeeps jobs and gives time for workers to retrain and the area to adjust, reducing structural unemploymentDelays change; keeps inefficient firms; consumers pay more
Protect strategic industriesFood, energy, defence and steel are needed for national security and self-sufficiencyHigher costs; may be hard to justify
Avoid dumpingForeign firms may sell below cost to drive out local firms; duties keep competition fairHard to prove dumping; retaliation
Reduce a current account deficitTariffs and quotas cut imports and may raise demand for domestic goodsWorks only if import demand is elastic; retaliation may reduce exports
Raise tax revenueTariffs are easy to collect, especially in low-income countries with few other taxesRevenue falls if imports fall; higher prices
Restrict demerit goodsLimit imports of tobacco, alcohol, drugs and weapons to protect health and safetySmuggling; black markets
Promote environmental sustainabilityRestrict imports of goods made unsustainably (illegal timber, ivory, high-pollution products) or that damage the environmentMay be protection in disguise; hard to monitor

Infant industry argument in detail

A new industry has a small output and so high average costs. Foreign firms with a large output have lower average costs and can sell more cheaply. If the infant industry is protected, it can grow and its average costs fall through economies of scale until it can compete without help. The protection should be temporary.

Dumping

Dumping is selling goods abroad at a price below their cost of production. A foreign firm may do this to win market share and drive out rivals, then raise prices. Anti-dumping tariffs aim to keep competition fair.

Evaluating the reasons

  • Do the benefits to protected firms and workers outweigh the cost to consumers?
  • Is the protection temporary or permanent? Permanent protection may make firms inefficient.
  • Will other countries retaliate?
  • Is the elasticity of demand for imports high enough for a tariff to cut imports?
  • Are there better policies, such as subsidies, training or retraining?

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Protecting Korean and Japanese infant industries

In the 1960s to 1980s South Korea and Japan protected young industries such as cars and electronics, then opened markets as firms became competitive.

Exam use: Infant industry protection that worked.

EU anti-dumping duties on steel and solar panels

The EU has imposed duties on cheap steel and solar panels from China, arguing they were sold below cost.

Exam use: Avoiding dumping.

Food security and rice

Countries such as Japan and the Philippines protect their rice farmers because rice is a staple food.

Exam use: Strategic industry protection.

Import bans on ivory and illegally logged timber

Many countries ban imports of ivory and illegally logged timber to protect wildlife and forests.

Exam use: Environmental reasons.

Tariffs as revenue

Customs duties are a major source of government revenue in many low-income countries with few other taxes.

Exam use: Raising tax revenue.

Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain why an infant industry may need protection. (Feb/Mar 2022, P22)
ReminderNew, small, high costs, no economies of scale; foreign firms cheaper; protection gives time; jobs.
An infant industry is new and small, so its average costs are high. Established foreign firms produce on a large scale and have lower costs, so they can sell more cheaply and could drive the new firms out of business. Protection gives the industry time to grow, gain skills and economies of scale, so its costs fall and it can compete. It could then create jobs and contribute to growth.
Q2 Explain 2 marks Explain one way that import tariffs could improve the US economy. (Feb/Mar 2023, P22)
ReminderCurrent account, output, or tax revenue, with an explanation.
They could improve the current account, by raising the price of imports and so reducing them.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse the reasons for imposing tariffs on imports. (May/June 2023, P22; Oct/Nov 2022, P23)
ReminderSeveral reasons, each with a link: reduce imports and the deficit, protect infant, declining and strategic industries, revenue, dumping.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A country may impose tariffs to raise the price of imports. Demand for imports could fall, reducing import spending and improving the current account, while demand for domestic firms rises, which could reduce unemployment and raise output. Tariffs can protect infant industries, giving them time to gain economies of scale so they are not driven out by larger foreign firms. They can protect declining industries, preventing unemployment from rising too quickly, and strategic industries, to ensure self-sufficiency. They also raise revenue for the government, and anti-dumping tariffs stop foreign firms from selling below cost.
Q4 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not imposing tariffs on imports will increase a country's output. (Oct/Nov 2023, P23)
ReminderFor: higher demand for domestic goods, protected firms grow, jobs. Against: retaliation, higher costs for firms using imports, inefficiency, consumer demand falls.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A tariff is a tax on imports, and output is the total production of goods and services. Tariffs raise the price of imports, so consumers may switch to domestic goods, and domestic firms can sell more, expand and employ more workers, so output could rise. Protected infant industries may grow. This is more likely if domestic firms can supply the goods and import demand is elastic. However, firms that use imported raw materials face higher costs and may cut output, and other countries may retaliate, reducing exports. Protected firms may become inefficient, and higher prices reduce consumers' spending power. Overall, tariffs could raise output in the protected industries but are unlikely to raise total output if there is retaliation and higher costs.
Q5 Discuss 6 marks Discuss whether or not an increase in Nigeria's import tariff on rice would increase the output of Nigerian rice. (May/June 2021, P21)
ReminderFor: dearer imports so demand for domestic rice rises; protects farmers. Against: farmers' capacity and costs, smuggling, higher prices, other constraints.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A tariff on imported rice raises its price. Consumers could switch to Nigerian rice, so demand for it rises, and farmers may produce and invest more, increasing output. This is more likely if farmers can expand quickly. However, output may not rise much if farmers lack land, machinery or finance, or if prices of inputs rise, and smuggling could limit the effect. Overall, a tariff could raise rice output in the long run if farmers can respond, but it depends on supply constraints.
Q6 Explain 4 marks Explain what is meant by dumping and why a government may take action against it.
ReminderDefine dumping (below cost), then the harm and the response (anti-dumping duty).
Dumping is when a foreign firm sells goods in another country at a price below their cost of production. Domestic firms cannot match the price, so they lose sales and some may close, causing unemployment. Once rivals have gone, the foreign firm could raise prices. A government may impose an anti-dumping tariff to raise the price of the goods and keep competition fair.

Top tips

  • Eight reasons: infant, declining, strategic, dumping, current account, revenue, demerit goods, environment.
  • Explain the aim and how the restriction helps. For infant: lower costs through scale.
  • Dumping = selling below cost. Not just selling cheaply.
  • Say it should be temporary for infant industries. Otherwise firms may stay inefficient.
  • Evaluate: retaliation, elasticity, higher costs. A tariff may not cut the deficit.
