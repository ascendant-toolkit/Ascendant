Exchange Rates: Systems and Determination

6.4.1 to 6.4.4: floating, fixed and managed exchange rates and what moves them, with currency market diagrams. Animations, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. In a floating system it is determined by the demand for and supply of the currency: demand comes from overseas buyers of the country's exports, inward investment, deposits attracted by higher interest rates and speculators; supply comes from residents buying imports, investing abroad and moving savings to higher interest rates elsewhere. A depreciation (a fall) is caused by a fall in demand or a rise in supply; an appreciation by a rise in demand or a fall in supply. In a fixed system the government or central bank sets the rate and defends it by buying or selling the currency using reserves or by changing interest rates, while a managed float lets the rate move but with occasional intervention.

1 · Floating exchange rates

ChangeEffect on the currencyExamples
Demand for the currency risesAppreciationHigher export demand, inward investment, higher interest rates, speculation that it will rise
Demand fallsDepreciationLower export demand, a fall in interest rates, loss of confidence
Supply risesDepreciationMore imports, investment abroad, higher interest rates abroad (savers move funds)
Supply fallsAppreciationFewer imports, less investment abroad
  • Relative inflation: if a country's inflation is above that of its trading partners, its exports become less competitive and imports more attractive, so demand falls, supply rises and the currency may depreciate.
  • Relative interest rates: higher rates may attract "hot money", raising demand for the currency, but the effect depends on confidence and expectations.
  • Trade-weighted exchange rate: an average of exchange rates against several currencies, weighted by the amount of trade with each.

2 · Fixed and managed rates

  • How a fixed rate is defended: if the currency is falling, the central bank buys it using reserves (raising demand) and/or raises interest rates; if rising, it sells it and/or lowers interest rates.
  • Devaluation and revaluation: an official lowering or raising of a fixed rate.
  • Advantages: certainty for traders and investors, and may help control inflation.
  • Disadvantages: needs large reserves; interest rates may have to be set to defend the rate rather than for domestic aims; may become misaligned with the rate the market would set.
  • Floating: adjusts automatically and needs fewer reserves, but is more volatile and uncertain for traders.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Rising interest rates

When a central bank raises interest rates, overseas savers may move money into that currency, raising demand and the exchange rate. The effect depends on confidence and what is expected next.

Exam use: interest rates and the demand for a currency.

A boom in imports

Households and firms buy more foreign goods and need foreign currency, so supply of the domestic currency rises and the exchange rate may fall.

Exam use: imports and the supply of a currency.

A central bank defends a peg

A country that fixes its currency to the dollar must buy its own currency when it is under pressure. If reserves run low, it may have to devalue or raise interest rates sharply.

Exam use: fixed exchange rates and reserves.

Speculation

If traders expect a currency to fall, they sell it, which can push the rate down further and faster than the fundamentals alone would suggest.

Exam use: speculation and exchange rates.

Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/21 · Q4(a) 8 marks With the aid of a diagram(s), explain one demand factor and one supply factor that would cause the value of a currency in a floating exchange rate system to depreciate.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): a correctly labelled diagram with the exchange rate (the price of one currency in terms of another) on the vertical axis and the quantity of currency on the horizontal axis (1), with market equilibrium shown (1).
  • Application (up to 6): an explanation of one reason for a fall in demand for the currency, such as less demand for the country's goods and services or a fall in its interest rate (1 for identification and up to 2 for explanation); and one reason for an increase in supply of the currency, such as higher interest rates in other countries or more demand for imports (1 + up to 2).
PlanDiagram: axes, D, S, equilibrium. Demand factor: less demand for exports or a lower interest rate. Supply factor: more imports or higher foreign rates.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

In a floating system the exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another, set by demand and supply. The diagram shows the exchange rate on the vertical axis, the quantity of the currency on the horizontal axis, a downward-sloping demand curve and an upward-sloping supply curve meeting at the equilibrium rate.

A fall in demand for the currency could come from lower foreign demand for the country's exports. Fewer overseas buyers need the currency, so demand shifts left and the rate falls. A rise in supply could come from higher interest rates in other countries, as savers sell the domestic currency to move funds abroad, or from more spending on imports, so supply shifts right and the rate falls further.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/21 · Q4(a) 8 marks With the aid of a diagram, explain how a central bank intervenes to maintain a fixed exchange rate when the economy is experiencing a current account deficit on its balance of payments.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): a correct diagram with the axes labelled (exchange rate or price of one currency in terms of another, and quantity of currency) (1), and demand and supply curves labelled (1).
  • Application (up to 6): an explanation that a current account deficit puts downward pressure on the exchange rate (up to 3): demand for imports exceeds foreign demand for exports, so domestic demand for foreign currency exceeds foreign demand for the domestic currency; and an explanation of how the central bank intervenes (up to 3): by buying the domestic currency using its foreign currency reserves and/or by raising interest rates.
PlanDiagram with the fixed rate above the market rate. Deficit: supply of currency exceeds demand. Intervention: buy the currency with reserves, raise interest rates.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The diagram shows the exchange rate against the quantity of the currency, with demand and supply curves. The fixed rate is held above the rate the market would set, so at the fixed rate the supply of the currency exceeds the demand for it.

A current account deficit means spending on imports is greater than receipts from exports, so more of the domestic currency is supplied to buy foreign currency than is demanded by foreigners. This puts downward pressure on the exchange rate. To maintain the rate, the central bank may buy the domestic currency using its reserves of foreign currency, which raises demand, and may raise interest rates to attract foreign savings, which also raises demand. Both have costs, as reserves are limited and higher interest rates may slow the economy.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/21 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain why fixed exchange rates could cause difficulties for a country that has one.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): a fixed exchange rate is determined by the government or central bank (1) and is therefore not determined by demand and supply (1).
  • Application (up to 6): one difficulty applied to a country with a fixed rate (1 for identification and up to 2 for explanation), such as the need for sufficient reserves to intervene, and another difficulty (1 + up to 2), such as being reluctant to change interest rates because it conflicts with other economic aims.
PlanDefine a fixed rate. Difficulties: needs reserves; interest rates tied to the peg; the rate may become misaligned.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A fixed exchange rate is a rate set by the government or central bank rather than by the forces of demand and supply in the foreign exchange market.

A country may need large reserves of foreign currency to intervene by buying its own currency when it comes under downward pressure, and if these run out it may be forced to devalue. The central bank may also have to use interest rates to defend the rate. For example, it may need to raise rates to attract funds even when the economy is weak and lower rates would be better for growth and employment, so domestic aims could conflict with the exchange rate target.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/22 · Q5(a) 8 marks With the help of a diagram, explain two ways in which a fall in the balance of trade in goods may affect the value of a floating exchange rate and consider the extent to which a change in the relative rate of interest between two countries may have a greater impact on the exchange rate.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): a fully labelled diagram (1), showing that an increase in demand for imports may increase the supply of the currency and lower its price (1), and that a fall in demand for exports may reduce the demand for the currency and lower its price (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): the impact of a change in relative interest rates on the demand and supply of the currency through hot money flows, for example a higher relative rate attracting savings, raising demand for the currency (up to 2); and that the extent depends on confidence in the country and other factors (up to 2).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a judgement on whether interest rates could have a greater impact, with a conclusion.
PlanImports up: supply of currency up. Exports down: demand down. Both lower the rate. Interest rates: hot money, but depends on confidence and other factors. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The diagram shows demand and supply of the currency. A rise in imports increases the supply of the domestic currency, as more is sold to buy foreign currency, and a fall in exports reduces demand for it, as fewer foreigners need it. Both lower the equilibrium exchange rate.

A higher interest rate relative to another country may attract savings from abroad, raising demand for the currency and its value. But this depends on confidence in the economy and on expectations about the currency, and other factors such as a large trade deficit may offset it.

Relative interest rates could have a greater impact in the short run, because funds can move quickly, while trade effects may matter more over time. The extent depends on how large the interest rate difference is and how confident investors are.

Top tips

  • Axes right. Exchange rate vertical; quantity of currency horizontal.
  • Label D and S. Demand and supply of the currency, with equilibrium.
  • Name the shift. Which curve moves and why, then the new rate.
  • Demand and supply factors. Exports, investment, interest rates; imports, investing abroad.
  • Fixed rate. Reserves and interest rates.
  • Costs of a fixed rate. Reserves run out; interest rates conflict.
  • Hedge. Use may and could for effects.
