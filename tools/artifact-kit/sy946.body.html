Policies to Reduce Inflation and Their Effectiveness

9.4.6: monetary, fiscal, supply-side, exchange rate and incomes policies to reduce inflation, the transmission mechanism, and how to judge effectiveness. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

The best policy to reduce inflation depends on its cause. Demand-pull inflation is tackled by cutting aggregate demand: higher interest rates and a tighter money supply (monetary policy), and lower government spending or higher taxes (fiscal policy). Cost-push inflation needs policies that cut costs or raise supply: supply-side policy, an appreciation of the exchange rate and incomes policy. Every policy has costs, especially lost output and jobs, time lags and uncertainty, so effectiveness depends on the cause, the credibility of the policy and expectations.

2 · Monetary policy

  • Raise the interest rate. Borrowing is dearer, saving more rewarding, so consumption and investment fall and AD shifts left. A higher rate may attract hot money, raising the exchange rate, which lowers import prices and exports.
  • Control the money supply: sell government bonds, raise reserve requirements, reverse quantitative easing.
  • Inflation targeting and an independent central bank: a clear target (such as 2%) and independence from politicians increase credibility, which lowers expected inflation.

The monetary transmission mechanism

A rise in the central bank's rate works through several channels: the borrowing channel (loan and mortgage rates rise), the saving channel (saving is more attractive), the asset price and wealth channel (bond, share and house prices fall), the exchange rate channel (the currency rises, so import prices fall and exports are less competitive) and the expectations channel (people expect lower inflation). The effect takes 12 to 24 months.

Why it may not work in practice: time lags; consumers and firms may be insensitive to rates (interest inelastic) or already heavily in debt; banks may not pass on the change; low confidence or a liquidity trap when cutting rates; the change may affect cost-push inflation little; the exchange rate may not respond as expected.

3 · Fiscal policy

  • Lower government spending or higher taxes reduce AD (contractionary or deflationary fiscal policy), helped by the multiplier.
  • Strengths: direct effect on demand; it can be targeted.
  • Limits: politically difficult (unpopular), time lags in decisions, can harm growth and jobs, and does not tackle cost-push inflation. Higher indirect taxes can raise prices directly.

4 · Supply-side policy, the exchange rate and incomes policy

  • Supply-side: training, competition, infrastructure and flexible labour markets shift SRAS and LRAS right, which cuts cost pressure and raises output at the same time. It is the most sustainable but the slowest.
  • Exchange rate appreciation: lowers the price of imported goods and raw materials, so it reduces cost-push and imported inflation, but it harms exporters and the current account.
  • Incomes policy and wage and price controls: limits on pay rises (especially in the public sector) cut wage-push pressure and expectations, but are hard to enforce and can distort markets or lead to strikes.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

The Volcker disinflation, 1979 to 1982

The US Federal Reserve raised its interest rate to about 20% to end high inflation. Inflation fell from over 13% to under 4%, but unemployment rose to nearly 11% and the economy had a deep recession.

Exam use: monetary policy working but at a heavy cost in output and jobs. A good example for the sacrifice ratio and credibility.

Inflation targeting in the UK, 1992 to now

The UK adopted an inflation target after leaving the exchange rate mechanism, and gave the Bank of England independence in 1997. Inflation was close to target for many years until global energy and supply shocks in 2021 to 2022.

Exam use: credibility and an independent central bank. Evaluate with the 2021 to 2022 supply-driven inflation, which interest rates could not fix quickly.

The 2022 inflation surge

Inflation reached 9% or more in many economies, driven partly by supply shocks (energy prices, supply chains) and partly by strong demand. Central banks raised interest rates rapidly, while some governments also used price caps and subsidies.

Exam use: the cause matters: rate rises work on the demand side but cannot cut the world price of gas.

Wage restraint and incomes policy in the 1970s

Several governments used wage and price controls to fight inflation. The controls held down prices for a time, but when they were removed pay and prices jumped, and the underlying causes were not addressed.

Exam use: incomes policy as a short-term cure for cost-push inflation, with the risk of distortion and a rebound.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/41 and 43 · Q6(a) 12 marks Explain what is meant by a transmission mechanism of monetary policy and consider why it might not work in practice.
What the mark scheme wants
  • The mechanism is the chain of effects from a change in the policy interest rate to spending, output and inflation: loan and deposit rates, saving and borrowing, asset prices and wealth, the exchange rate and expectations, leading to a change in AD and prices.
  • Why it might not work: time lags; consumers and firms with low interest elasticity of demand; banks not passing on rate changes; confidence and expectations; liquidity trap; saving behaviour; global factors that dominate (the exchange rate or world prices).
  • Level 4 (9 to 12) needs a clear explanation of the chain and at least two reasons it might fail.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/41 · Q6(b) 13 marks How far would you agree that monetary policy is the most effective way to solve the problem of an inflationary gap?
What the mark scheme wants
  • Define the inflationary gap (actual output above potential, AD too high) and show it on a diagram.
  • Monetary policy: higher interest rates, slower money growth, a stronger currency; works through the transmission mechanism to cut AD.
  • Alternatives: contractionary fiscal policy (immediate effect on spending, politically difficult), supply-side policy (raises potential output, slow).
  • Compare effectiveness: speed, cost in output and jobs, ease of use and credibility, and conclude on which is best for this problem.

Practice (not a past paper question) 12 marks Explain why policies that reduce aggregate demand may be an inefficient way to tackle cost-push inflation, and suggest better alternatives.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Cost-push inflation comes from a leftward shift of SRAS (higher costs), not from excess demand. On an AD and AS diagram it gives a higher price level and lower output.
  • Cutting AD reduces prices a little but cuts output and jobs further: a large cost for a small gain in prices (a worse Phillips curve trade-off).
  • Alternatives: supply-side policy to cut costs, an appreciating exchange rate (for imported costs), wage restraint or competition policy; lowering expectations by credible policy.
  • Evaluation: some demand tightening may be needed if expectations are rising; supply-side policy is slow; appreciation harms exports.

Cost-push inflation occurs when firms' costs rise, for example because of dearer oil or higher wages, which shifts the short-run aggregate supply curve to the left. This raises the price level and reduces output, so the economy has higher inflation and a negative output gap at the same time (stagflation). Policies that reduce aggregate demand, such as higher interest rates, would lower the price level a little but also reduce output and employment still further. Because the problem is on the supply side, a large rise in unemployment would be needed to achieve a small fall in inflation, so it is an inefficient policy.

Better alternatives would deal with costs directly. Supply-side policies such as training and competition could raise productivity and shift the supply curves back to the right; an appreciating exchange rate would reduce the cost of imported inputs; and wage restraint or a credible inflation target could reduce wage pressure and expectations. However, supply-side policies take years to work and an appreciation would hurt exporters, and if workers have begun to expect higher inflation, some tightening of demand may also be needed to stop a wage-price spiral.

Top tips

  • Start by diagnosing the cause. Demand-pull or cost-push? The answer decides which policy is appropriate.
  • Draw AD and AS. AD shifting left for demand-pull cures; SRAS shifting left for cost-push, which demand cuts cannot fix cheaply.
  • Explain the transmission mechanism step by step. Rate up, borrowing and saving, spending down, AD down, price level down.
  • Mention the lag. 12 to 24 months for monetary policy.
  • State the cost. Lost output and jobs. Link to the Phillips curve.
  • Credibility lowers the cost. An independent central bank and a clear target reduce expectations.
  • Compare policies. Monetary: fast to change but lagged. Fiscal: direct but political. Supply-side: slow but lasting.
