Effects of Changes in the Size and Structure of Populations

The optimum population, the effects of rising and falling population, and of ageing, young and unbalanced populations. Syllabus 5.3.2.

The big idea

The optimum population is the size of population that produces the highest output (income) per head given a country's resources, capital and technology. Below it the country is underpopulated; above it, overpopulated. Changes in population size and in the age and gender structure affect output, the labour force, public spending and living standards. Always say whether the effect is good or bad, who it affects and what it depends on.

Optimum, under- and over-population

SituationWhat it meansEffect of more people
UnderpopulationToo few people to use the resources fullyOutput per head rises (more workers, specialisation, bigger market)
Optimum populationOutput per head is highestNeither more nor fewer people raises output per head
OverpopulationToo many people for the resources and capital availableOutput per head falls (pressure on land, housing, jobs, services)

The optimum is not fixed: new technology, more capital and better education raise it, so a country can support more people at a high income. Using up resources or climate change can lower it.

Effects of an increase in population size

Possible benefits

  • Larger labour force and a bigger market for firms (more demand)
  • Economies of scale and more specialisation
  • More tax revenue and a younger, adaptable workforce
  • Higher growth if the country was underpopulated; more entrepreneurs and ideas

Possible costs

  • Pressure on resources, food, housing, water and land; more pollution
  • More spending needed on schools, hospitals and infrastructure
  • Unemployment if jobs do not grow; lower wages
  • Lower output per head if the country is overpopulated

On diagrams: a bigger population increases demand in markets (housing, food, clothing, toys: demand shifts right, price rises), increases the supply of labour (wages may fall) and could shift the PPC outwards as more labour is available.

Effects of a decrease in population size

Possible benefits

  • Less pressure on resources, housing, congestion and pollution
  • Higher output per head if the country was overpopulated
  • Fewer dependants to support if the cause is a fall in children

Possible costs

  • Smaller labour force, labour shortages and less demand
  • Lower tax revenue and slower growth; PPC shifts inwards
  • An ageing population if caused by a lower birth rate or emigration of the young
  • Pressure to cut services, empty areas

Age structure

StructurePossible advantagesPossible disadvantages
Young population (large share under 15)Future labour force; adaptable and innovative workers; demand for goods; low pension costsHigh dependency ratio now; spending on schools and childcare; need jobs for a large number of young people
Ageing population (large share aged 65+)Experienced workers; demand for leisure, healthcare and related industries; volunteers and savingsPensions and healthcare costs; higher taxes or borrowing; labour shortages; slower growth; less spending on durable goods; fewer people save as they retire
Dependency ratio = (population aged 0 to 14 + aged 65 and over) ÷ population aged 15 to 64

Spending and saving: older people usually have lower incomes and spend more of it on healthcare and essentials and less on new goods, and many draw down their savings, so national saving falls as the share of older people rises, while working-age people may save more for retirement.

Gender structure

A population with more women than men (or the reverse) can affect the labour force, birth rates and demand for goods and services. A shortage of men of working age (through war or emigration) can reduce the labour force; a shortage of women could reduce future birth rates.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Japan's shrinking population

Japan's population has been falling since around 2010 and a large share is aged over 65. Spending on pensions and healthcare is rising and some regions face labour shortages.

Exam use: Ageing and a falling population: costs and some adjustments such as robots and later retirement.

China's one-child policy

China limited most families to one child for decades. The birth rate fell, and now the population is ageing quickly with a shrinking labour force, though there is a larger share of men than women.

Exam use: Effects of changes in size, age and gender structure.

Nigeria's young population

Nigeria has a very young population, with around 40% aged under 15. This promises a large future workforce but requires many jobs and schools.

Exam use: A young population: advantages and disadvantages.

Migrants in Gulf states

Gulf countries such as the UAE rely on migrant workers, who make up most of the labour force, boosting construction and services, while the countries have a high share of men.

Exam use: Immigration, labour force and gender structure.

Overcrowded cities

Fast-growing cities such as Lagos and Dhaka face pressure on housing, transport and water as the population increases faster than infrastructure.

Exam use: Overpopulation and pressure on services.

The optimum population is the size of population that gives the highest output or income per head given the country's resources, capital and technology.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain two benefits a firm may gain from an increase in the size of a country's population. (May/June 2024, P21)
ReminderBigger market and sales; larger labour force and lower wages; economies of scale.
A larger population means more potential customers. Demand for the firm's product could rise, so sales and revenue may increase. It also means a larger pool of workers. The firm may find it easier to recruit and wage costs could be lower, and higher output could bring economies of scale.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain the effects of an ageing population on spending and saving levels. (Oct/Nov 2024, P21)
ReminderRetired people have lower incomes, spend more on healthcare, may spend less on durables; saving falls as people draw on savings.
Older people tend to have lower incomes after retiring, so they may spend less on consumer goods and more on healthcare and essentials, so the pattern of spending changes. Saving is likely to fall. Retired people draw on their savings, so a larger share of older people may reduce total saving in the economy, although workers may save more for retirement.
Q4 Analyse 6 marks Analyse the disadvantages of a rapidly growing population. (Feb/Mar 2020, P22)
ReminderResources, overpopulation, dependants, pressure on services, depends on cause.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A rapidly growing population may use resources more quickly, so the country's ability to grow in future and its sustainability fall, and pollution may rise. It could take the population beyond the optimum level, so output per head falls and there may be overcrowding. If the cause is a high birth rate, more resources have to be spent on schools and healthcare for dependants, and if it is immigration, there may be pressure on housing and services. These costs are smaller if the economy grows quickly enough to provide jobs.
Q5 Analyse 6 marks Analyse the advantages to a country of having a young population. (Oct/Nov 2023, P23)
ReminderFuture workforce, adaptable, demand, lower pension costs, tax revenue.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A young population means a large share of people will join the labour force. A growing workforce can increase output and economic growth, and young workers are often adaptable and open to new technology. They may spend on housing and consumer goods, raising demand and profits. Government spending on pensions and healthcare is lower, and as they enter jobs, they pay taxes that fund services. However, this depends on there being enough jobs and education.
Q6 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not an ageing population is a benefit to an economy. (May/June 2020, P22)
ReminderCosts: pensions, healthcare, labour shortages. Benefits: experience, demand for services, volunteers; depends on health and retirement age.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
An ageing population means a rising share of people aged 65 and over. It could harm the economy because more people need pensions and healthcare, so government spending rises while the labour force shrinks, which may mean higher taxes or borrowing and slower growth. It could also reduce productivity if older workers adapt less to new technology and cut saving. These problems are larger if people retire early and have poor health. However, older workers bring experience and can work longer, and older people create demand for healthcare, leisure and care services, and can volunteer. Governments can raise the retirement age or encourage immigration of young workers. Overall, an ageing population is more likely to be a problem than a benefit, but its effect depends on health, the retirement age and productivity.
Q7 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not a very low birth rate would be a cause for concern for a government. (May/June 2022, P22)
ReminderConcern: ageing, smaller labour force, dependency, tax revenue, below optimum. Not: less overcrowding, pollution, resource depletion.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A very low birth rate means few babies are born, so the population may fall and age. It could be a concern because the labour force may shrink in future, the dependency ratio rises and tax revenue falls while pension and healthcare spending rise. If the population falls below the optimum level, output per head could fall. The effects take time to appear. However, a low birth rate could reduce overcrowding, pollution and depletion of non-renewable resources, and parents may stay in the labour force in the short run. Overall, a very low birth rate is likely to be a concern in the long run, especially if the population is already ageing, but it could be a benefit in an overpopulated country.
Q8 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not having a large population is beneficial for the economy. (May/June 2023, P23)
ReminderFor: labour, markets, scale; against: resources, overpopulation, dependants, unemployment. Depends on the optimum.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A large population means many people live in the country. It could be beneficial because it provides a large labour force and a big market, so firms can benefit from economies of scale and demand is high, which could raise growth and tax revenue. A diverse population can also produce more entrepreneurs and ideas. This is more likely if the country is underpopulated. However, a large population may put pressure on resources, land and services, and could cause overcrowding, pollution and unemployment, and may lower output per head if the country is overpopulated. Overall, whether a large population is beneficial depends on whether it is above or below the optimum, and on the level of capital, technology and education.

Top tips

  • Optimum = highest output per head. Not the biggest or the smallest population.
  • Give both sides for every population change. Benefits and costs, then 'it depends on the optimum, age structure and jobs'.
  • Know the dependency ratio. (0-14 + 65+) ÷ (15-64).
  • Use diagrams where asked. PPC shifts out or in; demand shifts right for a larger population.
  • Ageing: pensions, healthcare, labour shortage, saving falls. Add that retirement age and technology can help.
