Market, Planned and Mixed Economies

1.4.1 to 1.4.2: how decisions about what, how and for whom are made in market, planned and mixed economies, the functions of price, and the strengths and weaknesses of each system. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

An economic system is the way a society answers the three basic questions of what, how and for whom to produce. In a market economy private individuals and firms decide, guided by prices that ration scarce goods, signal what consumers want and give an incentive to producers; in a planned (command) economy the government decides through central planning and usually owns the factors of production; and in a mixed economy both the price mechanism and the government allocate resources, which is true of nearly every real economy. Each system has strengths and weaknesses, and what matters in practice is how mixed the economy is.

1 · Market economies (1.4.1 and 1.4.2)

  • Who decides: consumers and firms, in markets. The price mechanism brings demand and supply into balance. Firms are mostly privately owned and aim to make profit.
  • The three functions of price: rationing (a higher price limits demand to the amount available), signalling (prices transmit consumer preferences to producers, so a rise tells firms that more is wanted) and incentivising (higher prices and profits encourage firms to produce more, and lower ones to leave).
  • What, how, for whom: what is produced depends on what consumers will pay for; how depends on which method gives the lowest cost (competition rewards efficiency); for whom depends on ability to pay, not need.
  • Strengths: efficient use of resources through competition, consumer choice, incentives to innovate and to take risks, little bureaucracy, and quick response to changes in preferences.
  • Weaknesses (market failure): public goods are not provided; merit goods are under-consumed and demerit goods over-consumed; external costs and benefits are ignored; monopoly power; inequality and little help for those without income; factors may be immobile, causing unemployment; prices do not always reflect all costs and benefits.

2 · Planned economies

  • Who decides: the government or a central planning authority, which sets output targets, prices and the use of resources. Factors of production are usually state-owned, and most goods and services are produced by the state.
  • What, how, for whom: planners decide which goods are produced, which methods are used and how output is shared, often aiming to share it according to need and equity.
  • Strengths: provision of public and merit goods for all; more equal distribution; the ability to direct resources to long-term projects and to avoid unemployment; no wasteful duplication in some cases.
  • Weaknesses: without price signals planners lack information, so there are shortages and surpluses; weak incentives for workers and managers lead to low efficiency and little innovation; limited consumer choice; bureaucracy and slow decisions; possible lack of freedom.

3 · Mixed economies

  • Who decides: the price mechanism allocates most goods and services, but the government intervenes to correct market failure and to pursue equity: it provides public goods and some merit goods (such as health and education), regulates markets, taxes and subsidises, and sometimes owns industries.
  • Economies lie on a spectrum. How mixed an economy is depends on the share of government spending, the amount of state ownership and the amount of regulation and price control.
  • Advantages: keeps the efficiency and choice of markets while the government supplies what markets fail to provide and reduces extreme inequality.
  • Disadvantages: government intervention can itself cause problems (taxes and subsidies distort prices, government failure, high taxes may reduce incentives, state firms may be inefficient), and there is no agreement on the right balance.

4 · Changing system

  • From planned to market (as in the former Soviet bloc and China's reforms): prices are freed, firms are privatised and competition grows. Benefits can be more efficiency, choice and growth; costs include rising prices, structural unemployment, widening inequality and under-provision of some merit and public goods.
  • From market towards mixed: governments add provision of public goods and regulation when market failure is serious.
  • Whether a change is beneficial depends on how quickly it is done, how well institutions work and who gains and loses.
Exam tipDo not write that a planned economy has "no" markets or that a market economy has "no" government. Say who makes the main decisions and what the consequences are, then judge using the degree of mixing.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Central planning and queues

In some planned economies of the twentieth century, prices of basic goods were fixed by the state, often below the level that matched demand and supply. Shops ran out of goods and people queued, which showed how hard it is for planners to know what consumers want without price signals.

Exam use: the information problem of planning and the role of the price mechanism.

A mixed economy in action

Most countries today let markets set the price of food, clothes and phones, but governments provide defence and street lighting, run or fund schools and hospitals, tax tobacco and regulate banks. Countries differ in how much they do, so some are more market-based than others.

Exam use: how mixed economies combine markets and government and why the balance varies.

Moving to a market economy

When former planned economies introduced market reforms in the 1990s, prices rose, many state firms closed and unemployment rose in the short run. Over time, new private firms, more choice and higher productivity brought gains, though some groups lost out.

Exam use: the costs and benefits of a transition from a planned to a market economy.

A surge in demand

When a new fashion makes a product popular, its price rises. This rationing effect limits the amount that each buyer can afford. It also signals producers to make more, and the higher profit gives them the incentive to do so. Resources move into the industry without any government order.

Exam use: the three functions of price.

What the mark scheme wants

  • Application to a planned economy (up to 3): central planning and government allocation of resources, perhaps with an emphasis on welfare.
  • Application to a market economy (up to 3): the price mechanism, with rationing, signalling and the interaction of demand and supply.
PlanScarcity. Planned: government decides what, how and for whom. Market: price mechanism and profit.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The fundamental economic problem is that resources are scarce but wants are unlimited, so choices must be made about what, how and for whom to produce.

In a planned economy the government decides. A central authority sets output targets, directs resources to chosen industries and decides how output is shared, often aiming for a fairer distribution.

In a market economy the price mechanism decides. Prices ration scarce goods to those willing and able to pay, signal consumer preferences to firms and give firms an incentive to supply what is profitable, so demand and supply allocate resources.

Based on Feb/March 2023 · 9708/22 · Q2(a) 8 marks Explain the functions of price in resource allocation and consider the importance of these functions in relation to the potential effectiveness of a market economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): at least two functions of price: rationing (1), signalling or transmitting preferences (1), incentivising (1); two functions well explained can gain 3.
  • Analysis (up to 3): the importance of rationing, signalling and incentivising to the operation of a market economy, in the context of a market economy.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a judgement on the importance of at least two functions for the effectiveness of a market economy, and a justified conclusion.
PlanRationing. Signalling. Incentive. Why each matters for a market economy. Judge which matters most.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Price has three functions in allocating resources. Rationing means a higher price reduces demand to match the limited supply. Signalling means prices pass information about consumers' preferences to producers, so that a rise in price shows that more of the good is wanted. Incentivising means higher prices and profits encourage firms to produce more, while lower prices push them to leave. Together they let a market economy work without central direction: scarce goods go to those who value them most, firms produce what is wanted, and resources move into growing industries automatically.

The functions are important for effectiveness, but they only work well if prices reflect all costs and benefits and if consumers and producers can respond. Signalling and incentives are probably the most important for long-run efficiency, while rationing is the most immediate. Therefore the market may be effective but not perfect.

Based on Feb/March 2023 · 9708/22 · Q2(b) 12 marks Assess whether all market economies should become mixed economies.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): for staying a market economy: little government intervention, decisions based on demand and supply, competition makes production more efficient, consumers and producers act in their own interests.
  • For becoming mixed: market economies fail to provide public goods, under-consume merit goods, over-consume demerit goods and may have prices that are too low, too high or too unstable.
  • A one-sided response can only reach Level 2.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): whether all market economies should become mixed, leading to a justified conclusion; a one-sided answer cannot gain evaluation marks.
PlanCase for staying market. Case for mixed (market failure). Judge: how much government is needed.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A market economy relies on the price mechanism and private firms. The argument for staying as it is rests on competition, which encourages productive efficiency and innovation, and on consumers' freedom to choose, with little bureaucracy. The argument for becoming mixed is that markets fail. They do not provide public goods because of free riders, they under-consume merit goods such as education and over-consume demerit goods such as tobacco, and prices of essentials may be too high or unstable. Government provision, taxes, subsidies and regulation can correct these problems.

Most economies are already mixed, so the real question is how mixed. If a market economy already has strong institutions and few failures, it may need only limited intervention. But because public goods will not be provided in a pure market, some government role is probably needed everywhere, so most market economies should be at least partly mixed.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/21 · Q2(b) 12 marks Assess whether a planned economic system should always switch to a mixed economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): advantages and disadvantages of a planned system, including its emphasis on welfare and the provision of public and merit goods, but the lack of competition may mean inefficient allocation.
  • Advantages and disadvantages of a mixed economy: it may contain the best of both systems, but this depends on how mixed it is; the ease of the switch can also be assessed.
  • Balanced analysis reaches the top level; one-sided answers are limited. Evaluation (up to 4): a valid conclusion.
PlanPlanned: pros and cons. Mixed: pros and cons. Judge: depends on the starting point and the speed of the switch.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A planned economy has strengths: the state can provide public and merit goods for everyone, aim for equity and direct resources to long-term goals. Its weaknesses are the lack of price signals and competition, which can cause shortages, inefficiency and limited choice. A mixed economy keeps the efficiency and choice of markets, while the government provides public goods and corrects market failure. Its disadvantages are possible government failure, and that the outcome depends on how the balance is set.

A switch is probably beneficial if the planned economy suffers from inefficiency and shortages, but it may bring unemployment and inequality in the short run, and the benefit depends on how quickly it is done. So a planned economy should not always switch, but a move towards mixed is likely to help where the planning system is failing.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/21 · Q2(b) 12 marks Discuss whether the advantages of a transition from a planned economy to a market economy always outweigh the disadvantages.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): advantages of a market economy: the price mechanism, prices as signals of consumer preferences, private ownership and decision-making with little government intervention, competition leading to efficiency.
  • Disadvantages: some products under-provided and under-consumed (merit goods), some over-provided (demerit goods), some not provided at all (public goods), and further consequences of the transition.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): judgement on whether the advantages always outweigh the disadvantages; reserve 1 mark for a conclusion.
PlanAdvantages (prices, competition). Disadvantages (market failure, unemployment, inequality). Judge: depends on speed and institutions.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A transition to a market economy introduces a price mechanism that signals what consumers want. Private ownership and competition give firms an incentive to be efficient and to innovate, and resources can move quickly to where they are most valued.

But a market economy may under-provide merit goods, over-provide demerit goods and fail to provide public goods. In the transition, state firms may close and cause structural unemployment, prices may rise and inequality may widen.

The advantages do not always outweigh the disadvantages. Gains in efficiency may take years, while the costs fall quickly on workers and the poor. A gradual change with strong institutions and a government that still provides public and merit goods is likely to give the best result.

Top tips

  • Say who decides. Market: consumers and firms through prices. Planned: the government. Mixed: both.
  • Name the three functions of price. Rationing, signalling and incentivising.
  • Use the three basic questions. What, how and for whom, for each system.
  • Always give both sides. Each system has strengths and weaknesses.
  • Mention market failure. Public goods, merit and demerit goods, external effects, inequality.
  • Say 'how mixed'. Economies are on a spectrum; the balance matters.
  • Judge with 'depends on'. The speed of change, institutions and who gains or loses.
