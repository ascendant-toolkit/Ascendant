Private Costs and Benefits, Externalities and Social Costs and Benefits

When a decision hurts or helps people who are not part of the deal. Private versus external versus social, the four kinds of externality, the welfare loss diagrams, how a tax or subsidy "internalises" the externality, and cost-benefit analysis. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

The chapter in four sentences

A private cost or benefit falls on the buyer or seller. An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on a third party, and the market price ignores it. Social cost = private cost + external cost and social benefit = private benefit + external benefit. When externalities exist, the free market output is not the socially best output, so there is market failure and a welfare loss; a tax, subsidy or other policy can internalise the externality. Cost-benefit analysis counts all the private and external costs and benefits of a project to decide whether it is worthwhile.

1 · Private, external and social

TermMeaningExample (a factory that pollutes)
Private costCost paid by the producer or consumerWages, materials, energy
External costCost imposed on a third party and not paid by the decision-makerPollution harming nearby residents
Social costPrivate cost + external costThe cost to society of the factory's output
Private benefitBenefit gained by the buyer or sellerThe satisfaction of a consumer
External benefitBenefit received by a third party who did not payNeighbours protected by someone's vaccination
Social benefitPrivate benefit + external benefitThe total gain to society
MSC = MPC + MEC   ·   MSB = MPB + MEB   ·   Net social benefit = social benefit − social cost

The "M" stands for marginal: the extra cost or benefit of one more unit. Efficiency needs MSB = MSC, not just MPB = MPC.

2 · The four types of externality

Negative production externality

The producer's activity harms others. Examples: factory pollution, noise from an airport, a quarry's dust. MSC > MPC.

Negative consumption externality

A consumer's activity harms others. Examples: passive smoking, litter from chewing gum, loud music, traffic congestion. MSB < MPB.

Positive production externality

A producer's activity helps others. Examples: a firm training workers who later move on, research that spills over, a firm rebuilding a local road. MSC < MPC.

Positive consumption externality

A consumer's activity helps others. Examples: vaccination, education, a well-kept garden. MSB > MPB.

Production or consumption?Ask who creates the effect. A factory producing causes pollution: production. A driver using a car causes congestion: consumption. Examples in the papers: the aircraft noise (production), chewing gum litter (consumption), vaccination (consumption), a firm rebuilding a road (production).

3 · The diagram: a negative production externality

The firm's supply curve is the private marginal cost (MPC). The social cost curve lies above it by the external cost per unit. The free market produces where demand meets MPC, which is more than the socially best output.

  • Market output Qm is where MPB = MPC. The price is too low because it leaves out the external cost.
  • Social optimum Qs is where MSB = MSC, which is lower. Between Qs and Qm each unit costs society more than it is worth: overproduction.
  • The welfare loss is the triangle between MSC and MSB from Qs to Qm.

Positive externality (consumption)

MSB lies above MPB. The market produces too little (Qm < Qs), because buyers ignore the external benefit. The welfare loss is the triangle of benefits not realised.

Internalising the externality

Make the decision-maker face the full social cost or benefit. A tax equal to the external cost shifts MPC up to MSC. A subsidy equal to the external benefit shifts MPC down to the social level. Policies are covered in syllabus 8.1.

4 · Cost-benefit analysis (CBA)

A way for governments to decide whether a project (a new airport, hospital or road) is worthwhile by counting all the costs and benefits to society, in money terms.

  1. Identify the private costs and benefits, then the external ones (pollution, congestion, jobs, time saved).
  2. Give them money values. Hard for things such as a lost view or a human life.
  3. Discount future costs and benefits to present values, because $100 now is worth more than $100 in ten years. Present value = future value ÷ (1 + r)t.
  4. Decide: go ahead if the net present value (benefits minus costs, discounted) is positive, or compare projects.

Benefits of CBA

  • Takes account of externalities and wider effects
  • Helps to compare projects and ration public money
  • Makes the decision more open and consistent

Problems

  • Valuing external costs and benefits is difficult and may be biased
  • Uncertain future outcomes
  • The choice of discount rate can change the answer
  • Does not show who gains and who loses (distribution)

5 · Evaluating externality arguments

  • Measuring the externality is hard (information failure), so a tax or subsidy may be set too high or too low.
  • Property rights and bargaining could solve some problems without government, if the parties are few and clear.
  • Government failure: intervention has its own costs and time lags.
  • Magnitude: if an externality is small, intervention may cost more than it gains.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. The numbers are simple on purpose, to show the idea.

Air travel

Flights produce emissions and noise that the airline and passenger do not pay for. The cost to society is higher than the ticket price covers, so more flying is likely than is socially best. A tax on flights could raise the price towards the social cost.

Exam use: the Feb/March 2023 20-mark question. Draw MPC, MSC and demand, mark the welfare loss, then explain a tax, negative advertising or regulation.

Traffic congestion and congestion charges

Each driver adds to the delay of everyone else, which is a negative consumption externality. Singapore uses a quota system and electronic road pricing to raise the cost of driving into busy areas.

Exam use: data response questions on congestion asked for a diagram of a negative externality and for the effectiveness of policies. Compare charges (price mechanism) with quotas (quantity limits).

Smoking and second-hand smoke

The smoker bears some of the cost, but others breathe the smoke and public health services bear the cost of illness. Governments use taxes, bans in public places and health warnings.

Exam use: the Feb/March 2021 25-mark question. Separate the negative externality from the information failure (smokers underestimating the risk), and discuss alternatives to government intervention.

Vaccination

When one person is vaccinated, others are less likely to catch the disease. The individual considers only their own benefit, so too few people may be vaccinated without a subsidy or campaign.

Exam use: a positive consumption externality, where MSB > MPB. A Paper 3 question asked why this is a positive externality.

A firm that trains its workers

If trained workers later move to rival firms, the rivals benefit from training they did not pay for. The training firm may train too few workers.

Exam use: an example of a positive production externality, with a subsidy for training as a response.

Building a hospital

A new hospital has large private costs. It also creates negative externalities (traffic, effects on wildlife) and positive ones (health, jobs, skills). A cost-benefit analysis counts them all and discounts benefits that arrive many years later.

Exam use: the Oct/Nov 2022 25-mark question. Explain CBA, apply to the hospital, then evaluate the difficulties of valuing and discounting.

Feb/March 2023 · 9708/42 · Q2 20 marks The use of air travel leads to market failure caused by negative externalities. With the help of a diagram, assess the extent to which a government can intervene to correct this market failure.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (14): market failure as allocative inefficiency: define allocative efficiency and link to maximising consumer satisfaction; a diagram of output where AR = MC consistent with efficiency. Define a negative externality (cost to society greater than that borne by the individual: a "spill-over"). A labelled diagram showing the market equilibrium, the allocatively efficient output and the welfare loss. Forms of intervention: taxation (raises the cost of flying, lowers demand and the number of flights), negative advertising, direct regulation.
  • Evaluation (6): how well each form of intervention works and what limits it (information about the size of the externality, effects on employment and tourism, whether the tax is set at the right level).
Diagram to drawDemand (MPB = MSB), MPC and MSC above it. Mark Qm and Qs, the prices, and the welfare loss triangle. Show the tax shifting MPC up to MSC.
KANE

A negative externality occurs when the production or consumption of a good imposes a cost on a third party that is not paid by the producer or consumer, so the marginal social cost is greater than the marginal private cost. Allocative efficiency occurs where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost, so that resources are allocated to maximise society's satisfaction.

Air travel is likely to lead to market failure because airlines and passengers ignore the cost of emissions and noise. On a diagram the market equilibrium is where demand (MPB) meets the airlines' marginal private cost (MPC), at output Qm. The marginal social cost curve lies above MPC by the external cost per flight, and the socially efficient output Qs is where MSB equals MSC. Because Qm is greater than Qs, the market over-produces and there is a welfare loss, shown by the triangle between MSC and MSB. The size of the welfare loss depends on how large the external cost is, which is difficult to measure.

A government could use a tax to correct the failure. A tax per flight equal to the external cost shifts the supply curve up to MSC, raises the price paid by passengers and reduces the number of flights to Qs, so allocative efficiency is achieved and the externality is internalised. A government could also use negative advertising to reduce demand, or regulation such as limits on night flights or emissions standards. A tax may not be fully effective if it is set at the wrong level because the external cost is hard to estimate, or if demand for flights is inelastic so that the number of flights falls little. It could also reduce employment and tourism income.

Regulation may give more precise control over noise or emissions but could be costly to monitor, and negative advertising may have a small effect on behaviour. Intervention may also suffer from government failure, such as time lags and lobbying by the industry. Overall, a government is likely to be able to reduce the market failure substantially through a combination of a tax and regulation, but it is unlikely to eliminate it because the external cost is difficult to measure and each policy has its own costs.

Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/42 · Q2(a) 12 marks Using diagrams, explain with examples the meaning of a positive externality for a consumer and a negative externality for a producer.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Externalities are spill-over costs or benefits not borne by the originator. A positive externality for a consumer: MSB > MPB. A negative externality for a producer: MSC > MPC. Examples for both.
  • Level 4 (9 to 12): defines both, analyses their link to social and private costs and benefits, appropriate diagrams referred to in the text, examples linked to both types.
KANE

An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on a third party who is not part of the transaction and is not reflected in the market price. A positive externality in consumption occurs when the consumption of a good benefits others, so that the marginal social benefit (MSB) is greater than the marginal private benefit (MPB). A negative externality in production occurs when production imposes a cost on others, so that the marginal social cost (MSC) is greater than the marginal private cost (MPC).

Vaccination is an example of a positive consumption externality. A person who is vaccinated gains protection, but others in the community are also less likely to catch the disease. On a diagram the MSB curve lies above the MPB curve by the external benefit per vaccination. The market output, where MPB equals marginal cost, is lower than the socially optimal output where MSB equals marginal cost, so too few people are vaccinated and there is a welfare loss, shown by the triangle between the two outputs.

Pollution from a chemical factory is an example of a negative production externality. The factory pays its private costs of materials and labour but not the cost of the pollution to local residents. On a diagram the MSC curve lies above the MPC curve by the external cost per unit. The market output, where demand equals MPC, is higher than the socially optimal output where MSB equals MSC, so there is overproduction and a welfare loss. The price is too low because it leaves out the external cost. The size of both welfare losses depends on how large the external effects are, which could be hard to measure.

Top tips

  • Define an externality as a cost or benefit to a third party that is not reflected in the price. Add "spill-over" and "marginal social cost greater than marginal private cost".
  • State the identities: MSC = MPC + MEC, MSB = MPB + MEB. Use MSB = MSC for efficiency.
  • Draw the diagram, and label it. Qm, Qs, MPC, MSC, MPB, MSB, the welfare loss triangle and any tax or subsidy. Several 20-mark answers are capped at Level 2 without one.
  • Say which way the market goes wrong: negative externality, overproduction and price too low; positive externality, underproduction.
  • Match the policy to the failure: a tax equal to the external cost for negative externalities, a subsidy for positive ones. Use the words "internalise the externality".
  • For CBA, say all costs and benefits, money values and discounting. Then give the difficulties.
  • Evaluate with measurement problems: the external cost is hard to value, so a tax may be too high or too low. Add government failure, elasticity and alternatives such as bargaining.
  • Test strong words: "only", "all forms", "necessarily". Show other causes of market failure and other solutions.
