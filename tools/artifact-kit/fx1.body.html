Definition of Foreign Exchange Rate

What a foreign exchange rate is, how to read and convert it, and what a rise or fall means. Syllabus 6.3.1.

The big idea

The foreign exchange rate is the price (or value) of one currency in terms of another currency. It tells you how much of one currency you need to buy one unit of another. For example, £1 = $1.50 means one pound costs one and a half US dollars. The rate matters because it decides what foreign goods cost, what exports earn abroad, and what holidays, loans and investments abroad cost.

Quoting and reading an exchange rate

An exchange rate can be written two ways. If £1 = $1.50, then $1 = £0.67 (1 ÷ 1.50). The same rate is just viewed from the other side.

RateMeaningConversion
£1 = $1.50One pound buys 1.50 dollarsMultiply pounds by 1.50 to get dollars; divide dollars by 1.50 to get pounds
$1 = £0.67One dollar buys 0.67 poundsMultiply dollars by 0.67 to get pounds

A rise or a fall in a currency?

A currency rises if one unit buys more of another currency, and falls if it buys less. Look at the currency whose value you are measuring.

£1 = $1.50 → £1 = $1.20

One pound now buys fewer dollars: the pound has fallen. It takes more pounds to buy a dollar ($1 = £0.67 becomes $1 = £0.83), so the dollar has risen against the pound.

1 US dollar = 15,000 dong → 23,000 dong

More dong are needed to buy a dollar, so the dong has fallen and the dollar has risen. (This is the Vietnam question from a past paper.)

Using exchange rates

  • Price of an export abroad: price in home currency × exchange rate (foreign currency per unit of home currency).
  • Price of an import at home: price in foreign currency ÷ exchange rate.
  • Holidays and investment abroad are cheaper when your currency is strong.
Price in $ = price in £ × ($ per £)  ·  Price in £ = price in $ ÷ ($ per £)

Check which currency is 'one unit'. If the question says €1 = $1.25, multiply euros by 1.25 to get dollars.

Why it matters

  • The exchange rate affects exports and imports and so the current account, jobs and inflation (see 6.3.4).
  • It is set by supply and demand for a currency in a floating system, or by the government in a fixed system (6.3.3).

Q2 Explain 2 marks In 2010 one US dollar bought 18,000 Vietnamese dong. In 2017 it bought 22,700 dong. Explain what happened to the foreign exchange rate of the dong. (May/June 2020, P21 style)
ReminderThe dong fell: more dong needed for a dollar.
The dong fell (depreciated) against the dollar because more dong had to be given to buy one dollar.
Q3 Calculate 2 marks £1 = $1.50. Calculate the price in dollars of a UK car costing £20,000, and the price in pounds of a US phone costing $450.
ReminderMultiply pounds by the rate; divide dollars by the rate.
20,000 × 1.50 = $30,000. 450 ÷ 1.50 = £300.
Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain what is meant by the exchange rate £1 = $1.25 and how a change to £1 = $1.00 affects a US importer of UK goods.
ReminderMeaning, then effect: pound falls so UK goods cost fewer dollars.
It means that one pound can be exchanged for 1.25 US dollars. If it changes to £1 = $1.00, the pound has fallen. A UK good priced at £100 falls in price for the US importer from $125 to $100, so UK goods become cheaper for US buyers and they may buy more.

Top tips

  • Define it as 'the price of one currency in terms of another'. Two ideas, two marks.
  • Check what is 'one unit'. £1 = $1.50: multiply pounds, divide dollars.
  • Rise or fall: look at the first currency. If one unit buys less, it has fallen.
  • Invert by dividing 1 by the rate. £1 = $1.25 is $1 = £0.80.
  • Link to prices. A falling currency makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer.
