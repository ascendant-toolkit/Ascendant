Policies to Reduce Unemployment

The range of demand-side and supply-side policies to reduce unemployment, matched to the type of unemployment, and how effective each is. Syllabus 4.6.5.

The big idea

There is a range of policies to reduce unemployment, and which works best depends on the type of unemployment. Demand-side policies (fiscal and monetary) raise AD and reduce cyclical unemployment. Supply-side policies such as training and job information reduce structural and frictional unemployment. Evaluate with time lags, cost, inflation and conflicts with other aims.

How effective are they?

Demand-side

  • Pros: relatively quick when there is spare capacity.
  • Cons: risk of inflation (a conflict between full employment and stable prices); bigger deficit; does little for structural unemployment.

Supply-side

  • Pros: tackles the cause of structural and frictional unemployment; raises productivity.
  • Cons: slow and costly; training may not match jobs; cuts to benefits can hurt the poor and cut demand.

Minimum wages

A minimum wage helps low-paid workers but, if set above the market wage, firms may hire fewer workers, so unemployment could rise, especially among young and unskilled workers.

Tariffs

Tariffs may protect some jobs but raise prices, invite retaliation and reduce exports, so overall employment may not rise.

Evaluating

  • Identify the type of unemployment first: the same policy may work for one type and not another.
  • Time lags: training takes years; interest rate cuts take months.
  • Cost and deficit: spending and subsidies need funding.
  • Inflation: reducing unemployment may raise inflation near full capacity.
  • Side effects such as inequality or damage to other aims.

Germany's short-time work

During recessions Germany subsidised firms to keep workers on reduced hours rather than lay them off, which kept unemployment low.

Exam use: A policy to keep cyclical unemployment down.

Retraining after coal closures

Several governments have funded retraining and regional investment for areas that lost coal mining, with mixed success.

Exam use: Structural policies and their slow results.

Tunisia and frictional unemployment

A past paper asks about two ways the Tunisian government tried to reduce frictional unemployment.

Exam use: Policies matched to the type of unemployment.

Minimum wage debates

Many countries have raised minimum wages in recent years. Research finds that effects on employment are small for modest rises but can be larger when it is set very high.

Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain how training could reduce structural unemployment.
ReminderMismatch is the cause; training fixes skills and mobility; then jobs.
Structural unemployment is caused by a mismatch between workers' skills and the jobs available. Training and retraining give workers the skills that employers need, so they can fill vacancies in growing industries. Better qualifications can also make workers more mobile between industries, so the mismatch narrows and structural unemployment could fall. However, it can take years and the skills must match what firms need.
Q2 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how an increase in investment may affect unemployment. (May/June 2023, P22)
ReminderCapital spending raises AD and demand for labour (cyclical); investment in education or infrastructure reduces structural unemployment.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Investment is spending on capital goods such as machinery and buildings. It adds to total demand and firms may expand output, so they might need more workers to use the new equipment, reducing cyclical unemployment. Investment in education and training could raise skills and mobility, reducing structural unemployment. Investment in infrastructure could make it easier for workers to travel to jobs and for firms to set up, creating jobs. However, new machines could replace some workers, so unemployment may rise in some jobs.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how the introduction of a minimum wage could affect unemployment. (May/June 2024, P22)
ReminderBoth effects: higher wages could mean fewer jobs; but also more spending. Hedge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A minimum wage is a legal lowest wage that firms can pay. If it is set above the market wage, firms' labour costs rise and the quantity of labour demanded may fall, so some low-skilled workers could lose their jobs or not be hired. At the same time more people may want to work at the higher wage, so the supply of labour could rise, increasing unemployment. Firms might also replace workers with machines. However, higher incomes for low-paid workers could raise spending and demand for labour, and if the minimum is below the market wage it might have no effect.
Q4 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not an increase in government spending will reduce unemployment. (May/June 2020, P21)
ReminderFor: raises AD and the demand for labour. Against: time lags, inflation, deficit, wrong type of unemployment.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Government spending is a part of aggregate demand. If it rises, for example on building projects, firms are likely to hire more workers and the multiplier effect may create further jobs, so cyclical unemployment could fall. It can also be targeted at regions with high unemployment. This is most effective when there is spare capacity. However, it may take time to plan projects, and the extra spending could widen the budget deficit. If the economy is near full capacity, it may cause inflation, and it does little for structural unemployment, where workers lack the skills for the jobs. Overall, an increase in spending is likely to help when unemployment is cyclical, but its success depends on the type of unemployment and the state of the economy and the budget.
Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not a government can reduce unemployment without increasing inflation. (Feb/Mar 2021, P22)
ReminderFor: supply-side policies; spare capacity. Against: demand-side policies near capacity; wage pressure.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A government can try to reduce unemployment using demand-side or supply-side policy. Supply-side policies such as training and job information raise productive capacity, so output and jobs can rise without pushing up prices, and they could even lower costs. If there is spare capacity, a rise in demand could also create jobs with little inflation. However, supply-side policies are slow. Demand-side policies such as spending and lower interest rates are quicker but, if the economy is near full capacity, additional demand could mostly raise prices, and a tight labour market may push up wages, increasing costs. Overall, a government is more likely to reduce unemployment without inflation if there is spare capacity or it uses supply-side measures, but the choice depends on the type of unemployment and the economy's capacity.
Q6 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not high tariffs can reduce unemployment. (Oct/Nov 2024, P21)
ReminderFor: protect jobs in domestic industries; against: retaliation, higher prices, less competition, lost export jobs.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A tariff is a tax on imports. High tariffs make imports dearer, so consumers may buy more domestic goods, and domestic firms could expand and employ more workers, reducing unemployment in protected industries. This may be most effective for industries facing unfair competition from abroad. However, other countries may retaliate with tariffs on this country's exports, so jobs in export industries could be lost. Higher prices reduce consumers' real incomes, and without competition domestic firms may become inefficient. Firms that rely on imported inputs also face higher costs. Overall, high tariffs may protect some jobs in the short term but are unlikely to reduce unemployment across the economy, particularly if other countries retaliate. The outcome depends on the size of the tariffs and the industries protected.

Top tips

  • Identify the type first. Cyclical: demand-side. Structural and frictional: supply-side.
  • Link each policy to a mechanism. 'Training improves skills so workers can fill vacancies.'
  • Conflict: unemployment and inflation. Demand-side policy near full capacity raises prices.
  • Evaluate with time lag, cost and side effects. Deficit, inequality, retaliation, minimum wage unemployment.
  • End with 'it depends on the type and the state of the economy'. That is Level 3 reasoning.
