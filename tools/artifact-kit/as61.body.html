Exports, Imports and the Terms of Trade

6.1.3: measuring the terms of trade, the causes of changes and their effects. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

The terms of trade measure the relationship between the prices a country receives for its exports and the prices it pays for its imports: terms of trade = (index of export prices ÷ index of import prices) × 100. An improvement (a rise in the index) means export prices have risen relative to import prices, so each unit of exports buys more imports; a deterioration (a fall) means the opposite. The terms of trade can change because of the exchange rate, relative inflation, changes in world demand and supply for the goods traded (especially commodity prices), productivity and protectionism by trading partners. An improvement is not always beneficial: it can make exports less competitive and worsen the balance of trade, while a deterioration can help export volumes but means a country must sell more to buy the same imports.

1 · Measuring the terms of trade (6.1.3)

  • Formula: terms of trade = (average export price index ÷ average import price index) × 100. The index has no units because it is a ratio of two indices.
  • Base year = 100. Above 100 means export prices have risen more than import prices (improved); below 100 the reverse (deteriorated).
  • Not the same as the balance of trade. The terms of trade compare prices; the balance of trade compares the value (price times quantity) of exports and imports of goods.
  • The terms of trade must change when the average price of exports changes relative to the average price of imports. They need not change when only the volume of trade changes.

2 · Causes and effects of changes (6.1.3)

  • Low-income countries often export primary products whose prices are volatile and have low income elasticity of demand, and import manufactures, so their terms of trade can deteriorate over time.
  • An improvement: higher living standards (more imports per unit exported), lower import costs and inflation. But export prices rise, exports may fall in volume and the current account may worsen if demand is price elastic.
  • A deterioration: more exports needed to buy the same imports; but it may make exports more competitive. The effect on the trade balance depends on price elasticities of demand for exports and imports.

Calculating the index. In the base year the index of export prices and the index of import prices are both 100, so the terms of trade are 100.

  • In year 2 export prices rose and import prices fell, so the index rose. In year 3 the opposite happened. In year 4 both prices fell by the same percentage, so the ratio did not change.
  • An index of 87.5 means that export prices relative to import prices are 12.5% lower than in the base year.

What it means for purchasing power. A country exports coffee at $100 a tonne and imports tractors at $500 each, so it needs 5 tonnes of coffee to buy a tractor. If the price of coffee falls to $80 a tonne while tractors stay at $500, the country needs 6.25 tonnes for each tractor: the terms of trade have deteriorated (80 ÷ 500 compared with 100 ÷ 500, a 20% fall) and it must sell more exports to buy the same imports.

4 · Terms of trade and the balance of trade

  • An improvement in the terms of trade does not necessarily improve the balance of trade. If export prices rise and demand is price elastic, the quantity of exports falls by more, and export revenue falls. If demand is inelastic, revenue rises.
  • A country should be concerned about both: the terms of trade show the purchasing power of exports, the balance of trade shows whether the country is earning enough to pay for its imports.

5 · Effects of a change on the macroeconomy

Improvement (rise)Deterioration (fall)
Living standardsEach unit of exports buys more imports: higher real incomeFewer imports for each unit exported: lower real income
InflationCheaper imports, especially raw materials, reduce cost-push pressureDearer imports raise costs; may add to inflation if imports are price inelastic
Exports and growthExport prices rise: volumes may fall if demand is elastic, reducing growth and jobs in exporting industriesExports cheaper abroad: export volumes and output may rise
Current accountMay worsen if export volumes fall and imports riseMay improve if demand for exports and imports is elastic enough
Open, trade-dependent economyEffects on income, inflation and jobs are largeEffects are large and spread widely through the economy
  • Primary producers: many low-income countries export agricultural goods and minerals whose demand grows slowly as incomes rise (low income elasticity), while their imports of manufactures have higher income elasticity, so their terms of trade may deteriorate over time. But countries exporting goods that are in high demand, such as oil or key minerals, may see their terms of trade improve.
  • Volatile prices: commodity prices swing with harvests and world demand, so the terms of trade of commodity exporters are unstable, which makes planning and government revenue uncertain.

6 · Is a rise always better than a fall?

  • A rise is better for living standards and inflation, but may reduce competitiveness and the current account.
  • A fall may boost exports and output but means lower real income, and if it comes from weak demand for exports it signals a weak economy.
  • The answer depends on the cause (higher demand for exports, or higher inflation, or a currency change), the elasticities, the economy's dependence on trade and the time horizon.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A coffee exporter

A country exports coffee and imports machinery. When coffee prices on world markets halve while machinery prices stay the same, the terms of trade deteriorate: the country must sell twice as much coffee to buy the same machine.

Exam use: a fall in export prices worsening the terms of trade.

An oil importer

When world oil prices rise sharply, an oil-importing country's import price index rises, so its terms of trade deteriorate even if its export prices are unchanged.

Exam use: rising import prices.

A stronger currency

If the currency appreciates, exports become dearer in foreign currency and imports cheaper in home currency. The terms of trade improve, but exporters may lose sales.

Exam use: an appreciation improving the terms of trade but hurting competitiveness.

A primary product exporter

Many low-income countries rely on exporting raw materials. Demand for these grows slowly as world incomes rise, while manufactured imports rise in price, so their terms of trade can deteriorate over time.

Exam use: why low-income countries may suffer.

A commodity price slump

A country that exports mainly copper sees the world price fall 30% while the prices of its imported machinery are unchanged. Its terms of trade deteriorate sharply and it must export much more copper to pay for the same imports.

Exam use: a deterioration for a primary producer.

Cheaper imported oil

A country that imports most of its fuel benefits when world oil prices fall. Import prices fall relative to export prices, so the terms of trade improve and cost-push pressure eases.

Exam use: an improvement caused by lower import prices.

Based on Feb/March 2025 · 9708/22 · Q4(a) 8 marks With the help of a formula, explain two reasons for an improvement in the terms of trade and consider the extent to which an improvement in the terms of trade will benefit an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): an accurate definition of the terms of trade or an accurate formula (1); an improvement is likely if export prices rise (1) relative to import prices (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): an improvement means a unit of exports buys relatively more imports (1), which may improve living standards (1) as imported goods appear cheaper (1), but it may lead to a current account deficit (1). Maximum 2 if only benefits are considered.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): an assessment of the advantages and disadvantages, for example in terms of unemployment and price elasticity values, with a valid conclusion.
PlanFormula: export price index ÷ import price index × 100. Two reasons: export prices rise, import prices fall. Benefit: more imports per export. Risk: less competitive, current account deficit. Judge using elasticity.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The terms of trade are the ratio of an index of export prices to an index of import prices, multiplied by 100. An improvement occurs when export prices rise relative to import prices. This could be because world demand for the country's exports rises, pushing up their price, or because the price of imported raw materials falls.

An improvement means each unit of exports buys more imports, which may raise living standards because imported goods become relatively cheaper. But higher export prices can reduce export sales if foreign demand is price elastic, which could lead to a current account deficit.

Whether an improvement is beneficial depends on the price elasticity of demand for exports and on the effect on employment in export industries. If demand is inelastic, revenue from exports rises, so it is likely to be of overall benefit; if elastic, the loss of competitiveness may outweigh the gain.

If a country's inflation is higher than its trading partners', its export prices rise faster than the prices of its imports, so the index rises and the terms of trade improve. If the exchange rate declines, exports become cheaper in foreign currency and imports dearer in domestic currency, so export prices fall relative to import prices and the terms of trade deteriorate.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/22 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain the difference between the terms of trade and the balance of trade in goods and consider whether an economy should be more concerned about its terms of trade than its balance of trade in goods.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): the terms of trade is a numerical measure of the ratio between export and import prices (1) with an accurate formula showing it as an index (1); the balance of trade in goods is the revenue from exports of goods minus expenditure on imports of goods (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): both measures analysed: effects of changes in the terms of trade (relative competitiveness, cost-push inflation, effects on the trade balance) up to 2; effects of changes in the balance of trade (employment, growth, inflation) up to 2. Maximum 3.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a judgement on which the economy should be more concerned about, with a conclusion.
PlanToT: ratio of prices. BoT: value of exports minus imports. Compare effects. Judge: BoT matters more for jobs and growth; ToT matters for living standards.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The terms of trade is the ratio of export prices to import prices: (export price index ÷ import price index) × 100. The balance of trade in goods is the value of exports of goods minus the value of imports of goods.

A deterioration in the terms of trade means more exports are needed to buy the same imports, and rising import prices can add to cost-push inflation. A deficit on the balance of trade in goods means more is spent on imports than earned from exports, which may reduce AD and employment and may put pressure on the exchange rate.

An economy is probably more concerned about its trade balance, because a persistent deficit affects output, jobs and the exchange rate, while the terms of trade affect purchasing power and matter most to countries that depend on a few commodities. The importance depends on the economy.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/23 · Q5(a) 8 marks Explain two possible causes of a change in the terms of trade in an economy and consider which of these causes is likely to be more important for low-income countries.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): what is meant by the terms of trade, the ratio of the export price index to the import price index (1); that it measures the number of exports that have to be sold to buy a given number of imports (1); that the terms of trade improve when the ratio increases and deteriorate when it falls (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): two main causes of changes, for example a rise in export prices and a fall in import prices, often the result of changes in demand for exports and imports (1); low-income countries are more likely to export primary goods and import manufactured goods, and the demand for primary goods may be lower than for manufactures when incomes rise, so import prices may fall and the terms of trade deteriorate (1); but commodity prices such as oil may have inelastic demand and prices may rise, improving the terms of trade (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): considers the significance of changes in the terms of trade for low-income countries that produce foodstuffs compared with those that export minerals in high demand, and a justified conclusion.
PlanToT formula. Two causes: export price changes (world demand), import price changes (oil). Low-income: primary goods, low income elasticity. Judge which matters more.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The terms of trade are the index of export prices divided by the index of import prices, times 100. It shows how many exports must be sold to buy a given amount of imports. A rise in the index is an improvement and a fall is a deterioration.

One cause is a change in world demand for the country's exports, which changes export prices. Another is a change in the price of imports, such as oil. Low-income countries typically export primary goods and import manufactures. As world incomes rise, demand for primary goods grows slowly while demand for manufactures grows faster, so export prices may fall relative to import prices and the terms of trade may deteriorate.

For a low-income country exporting food, the export-price cause is probably more important because demand for these goods is slow-growing and prices volatile. A country exporting a mineral in high demand could see its terms of trade improve, so the importance depends on what it exports.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess whether a rise or a fall in the terms of trade will benefit the macroeconomic performance of an open economy that is heavily dependent on international trade.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): better answers examine the effects on the wider economy: employment, price stability, economic growth and the current account, with reference to the dependency on international trade. For a rise: it may lower inflation if raw material import prices are lower; depending on price elasticity of demand it may lead to growth if income from exports increases and boost employment; it may lead to lower growth if it causes a current account deficit. For a fall: exports may be boosted and growth may rise; it may reduce a current account deficit; it may be a sign of a weak economy; if imports are price inelastic it may cause import cost-push inflation if the economy depends on imports. Answers that do not refer to heavy dependence on international trade cannot gain more than Level 2; answers that refer to only a rise or only a fall cannot gain more than Level 2; at least one negative effect in addition to the positives should be considered to reach lower Level 3.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether a rise or a fall benefits the economy, with a conclusion.
PlanRise: lower import costs, higher real income; but dearer exports, current account. Fall: exports up, CA better; but lower real income, import inflation. Link to trade dependence. Judge by elasticities and cause.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

In an open economy that depends heavily on trade, a rise in the terms of trade lowers the price of imported raw materials relative to exports, which eases cost-push inflation and raises real income. If demand for exports is inelastic, export revenue also rises, boosting growth and employment. But if demand is elastic, dearer exports may sell less, and a current account deficit may reduce growth. A fall boosts exports and may narrow a deficit, helping output and jobs in exporting industries, but a heavily trade-dependent economy would face dearer imports, so inflation could rise if import demand is inelastic, and real incomes fall.

Neither is clearly better. A rise benefits an economy that wants lower inflation and higher real incomes, while a fall helps one that needs to boost exports. The result depends on the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports and on the reason for the change, so I would judge that the benefit depends mainly on whether demand for exports is elastic.

Top tips

  • Write the formula. Export price index ÷ import price index × 100.
  • Say which prices move. Export prices up or import prices down is an improvement.
  • It is about prices, not values. Do not mix with the balance of trade.
  • Exchange rate falls: ToT deteriorate. Exports cheaper, imports dearer.
  • Higher relative inflation raises export prices. ToT improve as measured.
  • An improvement is not always good. Exports may become uncompetitive.
  • Use elasticity. It decides the effect on the trade balance.
