Causes and Consequences of Economic Growth

4.4.4 to 4.4.5: demand-side and supply-side causes of economic growth, and its possible benefits and costs. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Economic growth has demand-side causes, a rise in aggregate demand (consumption, investment, government spending or net exports) that uses up spare capacity, and supply-side causes, an increase in productive capacity from more or better labour, capital, technology, enterprise and infrastructure, which shifts LRAS right and allows sustained growth. The consequences can be good: higher living standards, lower unemployment, higher tax revenue to fund public services, and more resources for investment and development. They can also be bad: inflation if demand outruns capacity, a current account deficit as imports rise, environmental damage and resource depletion, widening inequality, and congestion. Whether the consequences are positive depends on the source and speed of the growth and on who gains.

1 · Causes of economic growth (4.4.4)

TypeCausesEffect on the diagram
Demand-side (short run)Lower interest rates, tax cuts, rising confidence, higher government spending, export growth, a weaker currencyAD shifts right; real output rises, most when there is spare capacity
Supply-side (long run)More workers (population, immigration, participation); better education and training; net investment in capital; technology and R&D; enterprise; infrastructure; new resources; mobility of factorsLRAS (and SRAS) shift right; potential output rises; the PPC moves outward
  • Sustained growth needs supply-side improvements. Demand-led growth in an economy at full capacity just raises prices.
  • Measuring the impact of each cause is difficult: the informal economy hides output, causes interact, and time lags are long.
  • Internal causes (investment, education, technology) and external causes (export demand, commodity prices, foreign investment, world growth) both matter.

Possible benefitsPossible costs

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Investing in education and technology

A country spends heavily on schools and research. Workers become more skilled and firms adopt better methods, so potential output rises. This is supply-side growth, which can last without causing inflation.

Exam use: supply-side causes and LRAS shifting right.

A consumer boom

Low interest rates and confidence lead households to borrow and spend. If the economy has spare capacity, output and jobs rise. If it does not, prices rise and the extra spending sucks in imports.

Exam use: demand-led growth and its costs.

Rapid industrial growth

A fast-growing economy raises incomes and cuts poverty, but factories and traffic raise air pollution and use up natural resources. The benefits are real, but so are the environmental costs.

Exam use: positive and negative consequences.

Growth in a few sectors

If growth is concentrated in mining or finance, a small group may gain most. Average income rises, but inequality widens and workers in declining industries may become unemployed.

Exam use: growth and the distribution of income.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/23 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain two causes of economic growth and consider the extent to which their impact can be measured.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): economic growth is the increase in the real value of goods and services produced in an economy over time (1); an explanation of two causes, such as an increase in AD (higher real wages, direct tax cuts, lower interest rates) or increases in LRAS (increased investment, increased productivity) (1 + 1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): an explanation of how each cause increases economic growth (1 + 1), for example reductions in income tax raise disposable income, AD and growth, though if job insecurity is high the increase may be less; or alternatively that the ability to measure the impact is affected by the size of the hidden or informal economy (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): the extent to which the causes can be measured, such as the size of the informal economy, with a justified conclusion (1).
PlanDefine growth. Two causes, each explained: one demand-side (lower interest rates), one supply-side (investment). Measurement: informal economy, lags.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Economic growth is an increase in the real value of goods and services produced in an economy over time. Lower interest rates are one cause, because they make borrowing cheaper and so raise consumption and investment, shifting AD to the right. Increased investment in machinery and technology is another, because it raises productivity and shifts LRAS to the right.

Lower interest rates raise AD, so real GDP rises if there is spare capacity. Investment raises productive capacity, so potential output grows. Each increases real GDP, but the extent can be hard to measure: causes work together, there are time lags, and output in the informal economy is not recorded.

The impact of each cause can be estimated, for example from changes in investment and in GDP, but it cannot be measured exactly because of the hidden economy and because of other influences. So the measurement is approximate.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/21 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain two possible causes of economic growth and consider whether the consequences of economic growth for an economy will always be positive.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): what is meant by economic growth (1) and two causes, such as an increase in the number of workers, better education and training, research and development, technology, investment in capital stock, mobility of factors or new export markets (up to 2).
  • Analysis (up to 3): the possible positive and negative consequences of growth, such as a higher standard of living and lower unemployment, but also inflation, a worse current account, environmental damage and inequality.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a judgement on whether the consequences will always be positive, with a conclusion.
PlanGrowth defined. Two causes. Consequences: higher living standards, jobs, but inflation, imports, environment, inequality. Judge: not always.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Economic growth is an increase in real GDP over time. It could be caused by investment in capital stock, which raises productive capacity, and by improved education and training, which raises the quality of labour.

Growth raises living standards, may cut unemployment, and raises tax revenue for health and education. But if AD grows faster than capacity there may be inflation, higher incomes may raise imports and worsen the current account, and higher output may damage the environment.

The consequences are not always positive. Growth driven by investment and productivity is more likely to be sustainable and beneficial, while rapid demand-led growth is more likely to be inflationary, and the gains may not be shared equally.

Based on Feb/March 2023 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess whether the consequences of economic growth for an economy are always positive.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the positive consequences, such as higher living standards, lower unemployment, higher tax revenue and more resources for public services (up to 4); the negative consequences, such as inflation, a current account deficit, environmental damage and inequality (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether the consequences are always positive, depending on the type of growth, the economy's spare capacity and who benefits, with a conclusion.
PlanPositives: living standards, jobs, tax revenue. Negatives: inflation, imports, environment, inequality. Judge: depends on source, capacity and distribution.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Economic growth raises real incomes and living standards, creates jobs and raises tax revenue, which the government can spend on health and education. But growth that comes from rising AD in an economy with little spare capacity pushes up prices, higher incomes raise spending on imports and may widen the current account deficit, and more output can mean pollution and depletion of resources. The benefits may also go mostly to higher earners, so inequality could rise.

Growth is not always positive. It is most beneficial when it comes from higher productive capacity, is sustainable and is widely shared. It is more likely to cause problems when it is driven by demand in a fully employed economy or by activities that damage the environment.

Top tips

  • Split causes into demand and supply. AD shifts versus LRAS shifts.
  • Name two causes and explain each. Do not just list factors.
  • Actual versus potential growth. Using spare capacity versus raising capacity.
  • Give both sides of consequences. Benefits and costs.
  • Say when growth is beneficial. Supply-led, sustainable, shared.
  • Mention measurement. The informal economy, lags, interacting causes.
  • Use GDP per head and distribution. Who gains?
