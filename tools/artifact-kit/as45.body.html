AD/AS Equilibrium and the Effects of Shifts

4.3.11 to 4.3.12: macroeconomic equilibrium, output, the price level and employment, and the effect of shifts in AD and AS. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Macroeconomic equilibrium is where aggregate demand equals short-run aggregate supply, and it determines the level of real output (and so employment) and the price level; long-run equilibrium is where AD, SRAS and LRAS all meet, so output is at potential. A shift of AD to the right raises real output, employment and the price level (and left does the opposite); a shift of SRAS to the left raises the price level and lowers output and employment (stagflation); a shift of SRAS or LRAS to the right raises output and lowers the price level. How the effect splits between output and prices depends on the shape of AS and on the spare capacity: with much spare capacity extra demand mainly raises output, near full capacity it mainly raises prices. When output is below potential there is a negative output gap and unemployment; above it, an inflationary gap.

1 · Equilibrium (4.3.11)

  • Short-run equilibrium: AD = SRAS gives the price level and real output. More output means more labour demanded, so employment is higher where output is higher.
  • Long-run equilibrium: AD = SRAS = LRAS, with output at potential (full employment).
  • If equilibrium output is below potential (a negative output gap, recessionary gap) there is spare capacity and cyclical unemployment; if above potential (an inflationary gap), resources are overstretched and prices rise.

  • Shape matters: on the flat part of AS an AD rise increases output only; on the sloping part both rise; on the vertical part only prices rise (pure demand-pull inflation).
  • Employment is not shown on the diagram directly: infer it from output.

3 · Worked example: effects of shifts in numbers

Take AD as P = 120 − Y and SRAS as P = 20 + Y. Equilibrium is Y = 50, P = 70 and suppose potential output is 70.

ShiftNew curveNew equilibriumChange in output and prices
AD right by 20P = 140 − YY = 60, P = 80Output +10, prices +10
AD left by 20P = 100 − YY = 40, P = 60Output −10, prices −10
SRAS left by 20P = 40 + YY = 40, P = 80Output −10, prices +10
SRAS right by 20P = YY = 60, P = 60Output +10, prices −10
  • With equilibrium output of 50 and potential output of 70, there is a negative output gap of 20 (28.6% of potential), so spare capacity and cyclical unemployment exist.
  • After AD shifts right, output of 60 is closer to potential: the output gap narrows to 10.

4 · Types of gap and what to do about them

SituationOutput compared with potentialFeaturesPolicy response
Negative (recessionary) output gapBelow potentialSpare capacity, high unemployment, low inflationRaise AD (expansionary fiscal or monetary policy)
At potential outputEqualFull employment, stable pricesMaintain; use supply-side policy for growth
Positive (inflationary) output gapAbove potentialOverheating, rising inflation, labour shortagesReduce AD (contractionary policy)

5 · Using AD/AS analysis: a method

  • Step 1: draw the original AD, SRAS (and LRAS if relevant) and label the equilibrium, price level and real output.
  • Step 2: identify the event and say which curve it moves and why (for example, a rise in oil costs moves SRAS).
  • Step 3: draw the new curve and equilibrium, and describe the change in real output, the price level and employment.
  • Step 4: evaluate: how big the effect is depends on spare capacity, the slope of AS, and whether other curves also move; also consider the time period and what policies might respond.

6 · Combining shifts

  • AD right and SRAS right (for example, higher confidence and lower oil prices): output rises; the price level may rise, stay the same or fall, depending on which shift is larger.
  • AD left and SRAS left (for example, a world recession and higher costs): output falls; the price effect is uncertain.
  • AD right and SRAS left: the price level rises sharply; the effect on output is uncertain.
  • Real examples nearly always have more than one change at once, so exam questions often ask for the net effect.
Do not write thisDo not say that a rise in AD always causes inflation: with spare capacity it mainly raises output. Do not treat stagflation as demand-pull: it comes from SRAS shifting left. Do not forget to label the axes: the vertical axis is the price level and the horizontal axis is real output, not price and quantity.
Exam tipAlways label the axes (price level, real output), the original and new curves and the new equilibrium, and state the change in output, prices and employment. Mention the shape of AS or spare capacity when judging how big each change is.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Recovery from a recession

After a recession many workers and factories are idle. When AD recovers, output and employment rise strongly but prices rise only a little, because there is spare capacity.

Exam use: an AD increase on the flat part of AS.

An economy at full capacity

In a boom, factories are running flat out and unemployment is very low. More spending cannot raise output much, so it mostly raises prices.

Exam use: an AD increase on the vertical part of AS.

Stagflation after an energy shock

A sharp rise in energy prices raises costs. SRAS shifts left: prices rise while output and employment fall, a combination called stagflation.

Exam use: SRAS shifting left.

Technology boosts supply

New technology lowers costs and raises potential output. SRAS and LRAS shift right, so output rises and the price level can fall, even while AD is growing.

Exam use: SRAS and LRAS shifting right.

A fall in oil prices

Lower fuel costs reduce firms' costs, so SRAS shifts right. Output rises and the price level falls, a positive supply shock.

Exam use: a rightward shift of SRAS.

A recession after a financial crisis

Banks cut lending and households lose confidence. AD shifts left, so real output falls, unemployment rises and price rises slow or reverse.

Exam use: a leftward shift of AD and a negative output gap.

Based on Feb/March 2024 · 9708/22 · Q5(a) 8 marks With the help of an aggregate demand and aggregate supply diagram, explain how a decrease in interest rates could cause economic growth and consider whether economic growth will always result in inflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): a clear understanding of economic growth (1), inflation (1) and interest rates (1); an accurate diagram with labelled axes and equilibrium price level and real GDP (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): using the diagram to explain how a decrease in interest rates affects saving and borrowing (1), which causes an increase in consumption, investment or net exports (1), and so an increase in economic growth or real GDP (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a rise in inflation assumes that the economy is operating at full capacity; growth may be focused on capital intensive industries or its source may be long term (1), and a justified conclusion (1).
PlanDefine growth, inflation, interest rates. Diagram: lower rates raise C and I, AD right, output up. Inflation only if near full capacity.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Economic growth is an increase in real GDP, inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, and the interest rate is the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving. On an AD/AS diagram the equilibrium is where AD meets SRAS at the price level P1 and real GDP Y1.

A lower interest rate reduces the reward for saving and the cost of borrowing, so households borrow and spend more and firms invest more. Consumption and investment are components of AD, so the AD curve shifts to the right and real GDP rises to Y2, which is economic growth.

Growth will not always result in inflation. If the economy has spare capacity, output rises with little change in prices, and if the growth comes from investment that raises capacity, AS also rises. Inflation is more likely when the economy is near full capacity.

Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/21 · Q1(e) 6 marks Using aggregate demand and aggregate supply analysis, discuss the possible impact on an economy of a sustained increase in its net exports.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 3): an explanation of the potential advantages of a sustained increase in net exports using AD/AS analysis, such as a rise in AD, a rise in real output, incomes and employment, and a boost to the current account balance.
  • Analysis (up to 3): an explanation of the potential disadvantages using AD/AS analysis, such as less being available for the domestic market and a rise in the rate of domestic inflation.
  • Judgement (1): reserve 1 mark for a conclusion.
PlanNet exports up: AD right, output and employment up, current account improves. But prices may rise, goods diverted from home market. Conclude.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A sustained rise in net exports shifts AD to the right, because net exports are a component of AD. Real output and employment rise, and incomes increase, as well as the current account balance improving. But if the economy is near full capacity, the extra demand mainly pulls up the price level, so inflation could rise, and more output sold abroad may mean less available at home.

Overall the effect is likely to be beneficial if there is spare capacity, because output and jobs rise with little inflation. It may become a problem if capacity is limited, when prices rise.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/23 · Q4(a) 8 marks With the help of a diagram, explain how increases in aggregate demand affect the level of real output and the price level in an economy and consider when such increases may become a problem.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): the components of AD, C + G + I + X − M (1); an accurately labelled AD/AS diagram showing the initial equilibrium (1); a shift or shifts of the AD curve to the right and the resulting changes in the price level and real GDP (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): an explanation of why AD might increase (1); why this increase may not cause problems such as inflation, for example because of the shape of the AS curve or because AS may increase in line with AD if the increase is due to investment (1); why it may cause problems, for example if the economy is at full capacity and AS cannot increase (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a recognition that increases in AD may cause an inflationary rise in the price level beyond the full employment level of output, and a justified conclusion.
PlanComponents and diagram. AD right: output and price level up. No problem with spare capacity; problem at full capacity: inflation. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Aggregate demand is C + I + G + (X − M). On an AD/AS diagram with price level and real output on the axes, a rise in AD shifts the AD curve to the right, so the equilibrium moves to a higher price level and higher real GDP.

AD might rise because of lower interest rates, which raise consumption and investment. If there is spare capacity, output can increase with little rise in prices, and if the increase is due to investment, AS may also rise. But if the economy is at full capacity, AS cannot rise, so the extra demand only raises the price level.

Increases in AD become a problem when they push output beyond the full employment level, creating inflation. Below full capacity they are likely to be beneficial.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/22 · Q1(d) 6 marks Use aggregate demand and aggregate supply analysis to explain how recent interest rate rises might help a country to reduce its annual rate of inflation and achieve its target.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis: an explanation of how the interest rate rise might help to reduce inflation caused by demand-pull factors (up to 4 marks) and by cost-push factors (up to 4 marks); 2 marks are allowed for evaluative comment that the rise might exacerbate inflation. 6 marks maximum.
PlanDemand-pull: rate rise, C and I fall, AD left, price level falls. Cost-push: higher exchange rate, cheaper imports, SRAS right. Evaluate: higher rates raise firms' costs.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Higher interest rates make borrowing dearer and saving more attractive, so consumption and investment fall and AD shifts left, which reduces the price level and so demand-pull inflation. Higher rates may also raise the exchange rate, making imported raw materials cheaper, which lowers firms' costs, shifting SRAS right and reducing cost-push inflation. However, higher interest rates raise firms' borrowing costs, which could shift SRAS left and worsen cost-push inflation, so the policy might exacerbate inflation.

Top tips

  • Equilibrium is AD = SRAS. Add LRAS for long-run equilibrium.
  • Label everything. Axes, curves, old and new equilibrium.
  • State output, prices and employment. All three changes.
  • Use the shape of AS. Flat: output. Sloping: both. Vertical: prices.
  • SRAS left is stagflation. Prices up, output and employment down.
  • Use the output gap. Spare capacity means growth with low inflation.
  • Judge with capacity. Will growth always cause inflation? Not if capacity exists.
