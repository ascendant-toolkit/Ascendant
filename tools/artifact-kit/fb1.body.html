Government Budget

What a government budget is, how to calculate a deficit or surplus, and how borrowing builds the national debt. Syllabus 4.2.1.

The big idea

A government plans each year how much it will spend and how much it expects to raise, mainly through taxes. That plan is the government budget. The gap between the two is the budget deficit or budget surplus, and it tells you whether the government has to borrow. Everything else in fiscal policy shows up here first.

The budget

A government publishes a budget: how much it plans to spend and how much it expects to raise. The result is one of three positions.

Budget deficit

Spending is higher than revenue. The government has to borrow the gap.

Budget surplus

Revenue is higher than spending. The extra can repay debt or be saved.

Balanced budget

Spending and revenue are equal. This happens less often.

Budget balance = government revenue − government spending

A negative answer is a deficit. It can be shown in dollars or as a percentage of GDP. The national debt is different: it is the total the government has borrowed over time. Deficits add to it each year.

Why a budget changes without a new policy

Automatic stabilisers are spending and taxes that move with the economy. In a slump, incomes fall so tax revenue falls, and more people claim unemployment benefit, so spending rises: the deficit widens with no new decision. In a boom the opposite happens. They cushion the swings in demand.

Why deficits matter

  • A deficit has to be borrowed, so it adds to the national debt.
  • Debt means interest payments in future years, which is spending that does not buy new services (an opportunity cost).
  • A deficit during a slump can support spending and jobs; a deficit that persists in a boom may be harder to justify.
  • A surplus can repay debt, but raising taxes or cutting spending to get one may slow the economy.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Tourism and the Maldives budget

The Maldives gets government revenue from taxes and from leasing islands for resorts, fisheries and agriculture, and a big share of spending goes on public sector wages. When tourism falls, revenue falls while wage bills do not, so the deficit widens.

Exam use: a budget-position question. Calculate spending as revenue plus the deficit, then explain how a fall in tourist numbers cuts tax and leasing income.

Recessions and automatic stabilisers

When the economy slumps, firms lay off workers. Income tax revenue falls and spending on unemployment benefit rises automatically, so the deficit widens without any new policy and support for spending is maintained.

Exam use: a clean example of automatic stabilisers. Explain the mechanism, then evaluate: it limits the damage but does not remove the recession.

Austerity and the cost of tightening

Some countries with large deficits raised taxes and cut spending to reduce borrowing. Public finances improved, but critics argued the cuts slowed growth and raised unemployment, which cut tax revenue.

Exam use: contractionary fiscal policy, and a standard evaluation point: reducing the deficit may take longer or cost more output than hoped.

Stimulus after a crisis

After the 2008 financial crisis and during the COVID-19 pandemic, many governments raised spending (support payments, wage subsidies, infrastructure) and some cut taxes temporarily such as lowering VAT rates. Deficits and national debt rose sharply as a result.

Exam use: expansionary fiscal policy in action. Cover AD, growth and jobs, then the cost: bigger deficit, higher debt and interest payments.

Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A balanced budget is where government spending equals revenue, so no new borrowing is needed. Aiming for one could help to keep the national debt under control, which limits interest payments and leaves more of future tax revenue for services. Lenders may also trust a government that does not keep borrowing, which could keep its borrowing costs low. However, these benefits may be smaller if the debt is already small compared with GDP. On the other hand, an insistence on balance could force spending cuts or tax rises in a recession, which are likely to reduce aggregate demand, raise unemployment and cut tax revenue further. Borrowing to build roads, schools or hospitals may raise productivity and output, so that future revenue could rise. Overall, a government is more likely to benefit from balancing the budget over the economic cycle, running deficits in slumps and surpluses in booms, rather than every year. The best approach depends on the size of the existing debt, the state of the economy and what the borrowing is used for.

Top tips

  • Budget balance = revenue − spending. A negative answer is a deficit; a positive answer is a surplus.
  • Deficit is a yearly flow; national debt is the running total. Deficits add to the debt.
  • Show your totals. Add the revenue column and the spending column before you subtract.
  • A recession widens the deficit by itself. Tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises: automatic stabilisers.
  • Give a cost of borrowing. Interest payments are an opportunity cost: money not spent on services.
  • Use hedged words. A deficit 'could' raise the debt; spending 'may' support jobs.
