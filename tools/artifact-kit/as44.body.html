Aggregate Supply

4.3.6 to 4.3.10: aggregate supply, the shape of the short-run and long-run AS curves, and the causes of shifts in SRAS and LRAS. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Aggregate supply (AS) is the total output that firms in an economy are willing and able to supply at each price level. The short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve slopes upward because input prices such as wages are slow to adjust, so a higher price level raises profit and output; it shifts when costs of production change (wages, raw materials, energy, taxes, subsidies, the exchange rate). The long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) curve is either vertical at the economy's potential output (full employment of resources), or has three sections: highly elastic when there is much spare capacity, upward sloping as capacity tightens, and vertical at full capacity; it shifts when the quantity or quality of factors of production or technology change. A change in the price level causes a movement along AS, and any other determinant shifts it.

1 · Short-run aggregate supply (4.3.6 to 4.3.9)

  • Why SRAS slopes up: in the short run some costs, especially wages, are fixed by contracts. When prices rise, revenue rises faster than costs, so firms raise output; as output approaches capacity costs rise, so the curve gets steeper.
  • Shifts of SRAS (costs): a fall in wages, raw material or energy prices, lower business taxes or higher subsidies, a stronger exchange rate (cheaper imported inputs), higher productivity shift SRAS to the right; the opposite moves it left.
  • A leftward shift of SRAS raises the price level and lowers output, called cost-push inflation with falling output (stagflation).
  • A change in the price level is a movement along SRAS.

2 · Long-run aggregate supply (4.3.7 to 4.3.9)

  • Shifts of LRAS (capacity): more or better labour (population, immigration, education, training), more capital (net investment), technology, discovery of resources, better infrastructure, deregulation and competition. Shifting LRAS right is economic growth in potential output.
  • The LRAS shifts left if capacity falls: war damage, emigration, resource depletion, or a lasting loss of skills.
  • Short-run shifts (costs) can be reversed quickly; long-run shifts change potential output.

4 · Explaining the shapes

  • SRAS upward-sloping: input prices (especially wages) are fixed in the short run, so a higher price level raises profit per unit, and firms raise output. It gets steeper near capacity because firms must pay more for scarce inputs (overtime, less efficient machines).
  • Three-section LRAS: (1) spare capacity: output can rise with little pressure on costs, so the curve is flat; (2) nearing capacity: bottlenecks push up costs, so output rises less and prices more; (3) full capacity: no more can be produced, so the curve is vertical.
  • Vertical LRAS: the view that in the long run output depends on productive capacity, not on the price level, because wages and other costs adjust.
  • Potential output is the maximum output the economy can sustain with full employment of resources and without rising inflation.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

An oil price shock

Oil is an input for transport and many products. When its price jumps, firms' costs rise at every level of output, so SRAS shifts left. Prices rise while output falls, which is cost-push inflation with weaker growth.

Exam use: SRAS shifting left from higher costs.

A stronger currency

A higher exchange rate makes imported raw materials and components cheaper, lowering costs. SRAS shifts right, helping to lower prices and raise output in the short run.

Exam use: the exchange rate as a cost determinant of SRAS.

Investing in education

Better education and training raise the skills and productivity of workers, so the economy can produce more even at full employment. LRAS shifts right over time.

Exam use: a long-run supply-side shift in LRAS.

A recession with spare capacity

After a deep recession many factories and workers are idle. Output can rise a lot without much pressure on prices, the flat part of the three-section LRAS. Later, as spare capacity disappears, extra demand mostly raises prices.

Exam use: the three-section LRAS.

An oil price shock

A sharp rise in oil prices raises transport and production costs for firms across the economy. SRAS shifts left, so output falls and the price level rises at the same time.

Exam use: a leftward shift of SRAS and cost-push inflation.

A new technology

Widespread broadband and automation allow firms to produce more with the same inputs. Productivity rises and both SRAS and LRAS shift right, raising potential output.

Exam use: capacity growth and LRAS.

Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/23 · Q3(a) 8 marks Explain what is meant by aggregate supply and explain one reason why the aggregate supply curve of an economy would shift outwards in (i) the short run and (ii) the long run.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): an explanation of aggregate supply, such as total output in an economy (1), that distinguishes between the short run and the long run (1).
  • Application (up to 3): a reason for an outward shift of SRAS (1) plus up to 2 marks for explaining why this moves SRAS outwards.
  • Application (up to 3): a reason for an outward shift of LRAS (1) plus up to 2 marks for explaining why. Indicative reasons include an increase in the supply of labour, net investment, other factor inputs, devaluation of the exchange rate, government regulations, discovery of new resources, better education and training, and advances in technology.
PlanDefine AS (total output; short vs long run). Short run: a cost fall shifts SRAS. Long run: more or better resources shifts LRAS.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Aggregate supply is the total output firms in an economy are willing and able to supply at each price level. In the short run, some costs such as wages are fixed, while in the long run all inputs can change, so output depends on the economy's capacity.

In the short run, a fall in the price of raw materials or energy lowers firms' costs, so they supply more at every price level and SRAS shifts to the right. In the long run, better education and training raise the skills and productivity of the workforce, which increases the economy's productive capacity, so LRAS shifts to the right.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/22 · Q3(a) 8 marks Explain how the factor of production enterprise contributes to aggregate supply in a modern economy and how it differs from the other factors of production in terms of the reward for its services.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): enterprise organises the other factors of production (1) and bears the risk of production (1).
  • Application (up to 6): profit is the reward to enterprise (1); labour is rewarded with wages (1), land with rent (1) and capital with interest (1); enterprise uses the other factors to produce goods and services that increase aggregate supply (1), through an increase in productive potential or a fall in unused resources (1).
PlanEnterprise organises and takes risk. Reward: profit (which may be a loss), unlike wages, rent, interest. It raises AS by using resources.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Enterprise organises the other factors of production, bringing together land, labour and capital, and bears the risk that costs will exceed revenue.

Profit is the reward for enterprise, and it is not guaranteed, whereas labour earns wages, land earns rent and capital earns interest, which are paid whether or not the firm makes a profit. By using the other factors to produce goods and services, enterprise raises aggregate supply, either by increasing productive potential, through new products and methods, or by reducing unused resources.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/24 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain why expenditure on education and training is a supply-side policy and consider why its impact on the general price level may differ between the short run and the long run.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): supply-side policy consists of government tools designed to increase aggregate supply (1); the effect is to shift the AS curve to the right (1); spending on education and training increases worker skills and productivity, so raises productive capacity and shifts LRAS to the right (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): in the short run, increased government expenditure increases AD, which may raise inflation and the price level (up to 2); in the long run, higher productivity and capacity shift LRAS and should lower the price level (up to 2).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): an overall assessment of whether the impact differs between the short and long run, for example if the spending is ineffective, or time lags mean the short-run impact is minimal; a justified conclusion (1).
PlanSupply-side: raises AS. Short run: spending raises AD, prices up. Long run: LRAS shifts right, prices lower. Evaluate: time lags, effectiveness.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Supply-side policies aim to raise aggregate supply. Spending on education and training raises workers' skills and productivity, which increases the economy's productive capacity and shifts LRAS to the right.

In the short run, the extra government spending adds to AD, which may raise the price level, since the benefits to supply take time. In the long run, higher productivity and capacity shift LRAS right, which should lower the price level and raise output.

The long-run effect depends on the spending being effective and on time lags, so the short-run effect may be small. If it does not raise productivity, the policy may simply push up prices.

Top tips

  • Costs move SRAS; capacity moves LRAS. State which curve before you describe the shift.
  • Say why SRAS slopes up. Sticky wages mean higher prices raise profit.
  • Left shift: price up, output down. Cost-push inflation.
  • LRAS is potential output. Full employment of resources.
  • Know both LRAS shapes. Vertical, or three sections.
  • Shifting LRAS right is growth. More potential output.
  • Movement along is price level only. Do not draw it as a shift.
