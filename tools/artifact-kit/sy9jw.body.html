The Injections and Withdrawals Diagram Explained

A step-by-step explainer of the injections and withdrawals (J and W) graph: building J and W, why W starts below zero, equilibrium and stability, rises in injections and the multiplier, shifts and rotations of W, the gaps at full employment and how it compares with the 45° diagram. With animations, a build-your-own lab, drills and exam questions.

This part in four sentences

The injections and withdrawals (J and W) diagram plots injections J = I + G + X and withdrawals W = S + T + M against national income. J is a horizontal line because injections are assumed not to depend on domestic income, and W slopes upwards because saving, taxes and imports all rise with income. Equilibrium is where J = W: if J is greater than W, spending is being added faster than it leaks out, so income rises, and if W is greater than J, income falls. A change in injections shifts the J line, and national income changes by the multiplier times the shift, which is why the horizontal distance between the two equilibria is larger than the vertical shift.

1 · What the diagram shows

  • Horizontal axis: national income, Y. The same income, output and expenditure measure as on the 45° diagram.
  • Vertical axis: injections and withdrawals (J and W). Both are flows of money per year, in the same units as income.
  • J is the horizontal line. J1 in the textbook figure is a higher horizontal line after a rise in injections.
  • W is the upward-sloping line. Notice that it starts below the horizontal axis. The next two sections explain why.
  • Y and Y1 are the equilibrium incomes where W crosses J and J1.
Where this fitsThis is the leakages and injections approach to national income. The 45° (Keynesian cross) diagram gives the same equilibrium from the expenditure side. Both are in the syllabus under 9.1.1, and a question may name either one, so you need to be able to draw both.

2 · Building the lines

Injections: J = I + G + X

  • I investment by firms in capital goods
  • G government spending on goods and services
  • X exports, spending by foreigners on the country's output

Each is assumed to be decided by factors other than domestic income: interest rates and expected profit (I), government policy (G), and foreign incomes and competitiveness (X). So J is autonomous, which is why the line is flat.

Withdrawals: W = S + T + M

  • S saving, income not spent
  • T taxes, which take income out of the flow (net of government transfers)
  • M imports, spending that leaves the domestic economy

All three rise as income rises, so W slopes upward. Its slope is the marginal propensity to withdraw: MPW = MPS + MRT + MPM.

Throughout this page the numbers are: I = 100, G = 70, X = 30, so J = 200. S = −50 + 0.1Y, T = 0.25Y, M = 0.15Y, so W = −50 + 0.5Y. The MPW is 0.1 + 0.25 + 0.15 = 0.5.

3 · Why does W start below zero?

This is the feature of the textbook figure that is easy to miss. At zero income there are no taxes and no imports, because both are a share of income. But households still need to spend, so they draw on past savings or borrow. That is dissaving, negative saving. It is the autonomous part of the saving function, S = −a + sY (the mirror image of the consumption function C = a + cY).

  • With a = 50, W is −50 where the line meets the vertical axis.
  • The line crosses the horizontal axis at Y = 100 (where 0.5Y = 50). Below that income, withdrawals are negative: households dissave by more than taxes and imports take out.
  • A W line drawn from the origin assumes there is no autonomous dissaving, which is a common simplification. A negative intercept is more accurate and matches the consumption function.

4 · Equilibrium, and why it is stable

Equilibrium national income is where J = W. With J = 200: 200 = −50 + 0.5Y, so Y = 500.

  • Below equilibrium (Y = 300): J = 200 but W = 100. There is a net injection of 100: more money is being put into the circular flow than leaves it. Firms sell more than they planned, stocks fall, output and incomes rise, and as income rises W rises with it, until it reaches J.
  • Above equilibrium (Y = 700): W = 300 is greater than J = 200, a net withdrawal of 100. Spending falls short of output, stocks build up, firms cut production and income falls until W comes back to J.

Notice that planned injections and withdrawals are only equal at equilibrium. In the national accounts, after the event, they are always equal because any difference shows up as unplanned changes in stocks.

5 · A rise in injections: J to J1

Suppose the government raises spending by 50, so J rises from 200 to 250. J1 is a higher horizontal line.

  • At the old equilibrium (500) the new J1 is 250 but W is still 200, so there is a net injection of 50.
  • Income rises. For every 1 of extra income, W rises by 0.5 (the MPW), so W only catches up with J1 when income has risen by 50 ÷ 0.5 = 100.
  • The new equilibrium is Y1 = 600, where −50 + 0.5Y = 250.
ΔY = ΔJ ÷ MPW = ΔJ × k, where k = 1 ÷ MPW = 1 ÷ (MPS + MRT + MPM)

Here k = 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2, so 50 × 2 = 100. On the diagram the vertical shift of J is 50, and the horizontal distance between Y and Y1 is 100. The slope of W tells you the multiplier: a flatter W means a larger multiplier, because withdrawals take longer to catch up with the new injections. A fall in injections is the same in reverse: J shifts down and income falls by a multiple. The negative intercept does not change the multiplier, only the level of income.

6 · Changes in withdrawals

A shift of W (a change in the intercept)

A change in autonomous saving, taxes or imports moves the line up or down in parallel. For example, if households become more cautious, autonomous dissaving falls from 50 to 30, so W shifts up by 20 (it is now −30 + 0.5Y). Equilibrium falls to (200 + 30) ÷ 0.5 = 460, a fall of 40, which is 20 × 2.

A shift down would raise income.

A rotation of W (a change in the slope)

A change in the marginal propensities changes the steepness. For example, a rise in the marginal rate of income tax from 0.25 to 0.35 raises MPW from 0.5 to 0.6, so W is steeper, pivoting around its intercept (−50). Equilibrium falls to 250 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 417, and the multiplier falls from 2 to 1.67.

A flatter W (for instance a lower MPM after import substitution) raises both income and the multiplier.

7 · Full employment and the gaps on this diagram

Mark the full employment level of income, YF, on the horizontal axis and compare J and W at YF.

  • W is above J at YF: a deflationary gap. Equilibrium is below YF. The vertical distance between W and J at YF is the shortfall in injections. With YF = 600, W = 250 and J = 200, so the gap is 50, which is exactly the rise in J that moves equilibrium to 600.
  • J is above W at YF: an inflationary gap. Equilibrium would be above YF, but real output cannot exceed YF, so the excess demand pulls up prices. With YF = 400, W = 150 and J = 200, the gap is 50 and a fall in J of 50 would close it.
Same gap as on the 45° diagramThe vertical gap between W and J at YF has exactly the same size as the vertical gap between the AE line and the 45° line at YF. The two diagrams are two views of the same economy. The required change in injections is the gap, and the change in income is the gap times the multiplier.

9 · Related ideas

The paradox of thrift

If households try to save more, W shifts up. In a closed economy without a government, equilibrium needs saving to equal investment (S = I), and J (= I) has not changed. So income falls until saving is back to the original level: everyone tries to save more, but total saving does not rise and income is lower. This is a Keynesian result and it depends on there being spare capacity.

Net injection and the multiplier rounds

The net injection at the old equilibrium (50 in section 5) is the first round of extra spending. Each round of income creates a smaller net injection, until the net injection is zero. The multiplier is the total of all the rounds.

Taxes and transfers

Strictly, taxes are net of transfer payments (pensions, benefits), which add to income. Benefits that rise in a recession make W lower than it would otherwise be, which is part of why automatic stabilisers help.

Opening the economy

Adding imports (M) makes W steeper and the multiplier smaller. Adding exports (X) raises J. This is why a country that opens to trade has a smaller multiplier but gains a new injection.

10 · What the model assumes

  • Injections are autonomous, so J is flat. In reality, investment also depends on income (the accelerator), so J could slope upwards.
  • The price level is fixed. Near full employment, extra demand may raise prices instead of output.
  • Withdrawals are a stable share of income, so the slope of W is constant. The propensities change over time, so the multiplier is only an estimate.
  • The adjustment takes time, which the diagram does not show.

12 · How to draw it in an exam

  1. Label the axes: J and W (vertical) and national income (horizontal).
  2. Draw W sloping up (start it below the axis if you want to show dissaving) and label it.
  3. Draw J as a horizontal line and label it.
  4. Mark the equilibrium Y where they cross, with a dashed line to the axis.
  5. For a change in injections, draw J1 and mark Y1. For a change in W, show the new W1.
  6. For employment or gap questions, mark YF and show the vertical gap, and write the chain in words: J up, net injection, income and output up, employment up, multiplier.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A lower interest rate and employment

When a central bank cuts interest rates, borrowing becomes cheaper, so firms may raise investment and households may spend more and save less. J shifts up (and W may shift down), income and output rise by a multiple, and firms may hire more workers.

Exam use: a question on the effect of an interest rate cut on employment. Draw the J/W graph, then say that the effect depends on how sensitive investment is to interest rates and on the multiplier.

A recession abroad

If a country's main trading partners fall into recession, they buy fewer of its exports. J shifts down, equilibrium income falls by a multiple, and unemployment may rise in export industries.

Exam use: showing how foreign incomes feed into national income. Mention the multiplier and the share of exports in GDP.

Rising saving after a crisis

In a financial crisis, households and firms may try to save more to rebuild their finances. W shifts up, income falls, and total saving may not rise as much as people planned because lower income cuts saving too.

Exam use: the paradox of thrift. Link to a deflationary gap and to why governments may raise spending in a downturn.

Benefits and automatic stabilisers

In a downturn tax revenue falls and benefit payments rise automatically, so net taxes fall. W is lower than it would otherwise be, which means income falls by less than it would otherwise do.

Exam use: automatic stabilisers. Say that they lower the slope of W and therefore the multiplier.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/42 · Q4 20 marks With the help of an injections and withdrawals graph, assess the impact of a decrease in interest rates on the level of employment in an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge and analysis (14): explain injections, withdrawals and employment, describe the circular flow, and analyse with an accurately labelled injections and withdrawals graph how a fall in interest rates affects employment through components of aggregate demand. A J/W graph can show full employment equilibrium and the links between expenditure, income, output and employment. Credit for the multiplier and accelerator.
  • Caps: no J/W graph, maximum Level 2 (8 marks). A general monetary policy answer is also capped at Level 2. No credit for alternative policies such as supply-side or fiscal policy.
  • Evaluation (6): inflation if the economy is near full employment; depreciation raising import costs (cost-push); house price inflation; the type of unemployment (less effective for structural); the MPS and MPC and the size of the multiplier and accelerator; time lags.
PlanDefine injections, withdrawals and employment. Draw the J/W graph first, with J and J1, W, Y and Y1. Chain: lower rate, cheaper borrowing, I up (and C up, S down), J shifts up (and W may shift down), net injection, income and output up, employment up, multiplier. Then evaluate and judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Injections (J = I + G + X) are spending added to the circular flow of income and withdrawals (W = S + T + M) are income taken out of it, and national income is in equilibrium where J = W. Employment depends on output, because firms need labour to produce. A fall in the central bank's interest rate makes borrowing cheaper, so firms are more likely to invest in new machinery and households are more likely to borrow to spend, and the reward for saving is lower.

On the graph, higher investment shifts J up to J1, and lower saving may shift W down. At the old level of income, J1 is greater than W, so there is a net injection. Firms sell more than they planned and raise output, income rises, and as income rises W rises, until W equals J1 at a higher level Y1. Because the increase in income is a multiple of the change in injections, employment could rise by more than the initial boost to investment. Higher output leads firms to employ more workers, and extra income and demand may encourage more investment through the accelerator.

However, the effect depends on how sensitive investment is to interest rates. If firms are pessimistic, they may not borrow even at low rates and J may shift by only a little. The multiplier depends on the MPW: if households save a large share of extra income, or buy many imports, the rise in income is smaller. Interest rate changes take time to work, and if unemployment is structural, extra demand cannot create jobs for workers without the right skills. If the economy is close to full employment, extra demand may mainly raise prices, and a lower exchange rate may raise import costs. Overall, a cut in interest rates is likely to raise employment when there is spare capacity and cyclical unemployment, but the size depends on the interest elasticity of investment, the multiplier and the cause of unemployment.

Practice (not a past paper question) 8 marks Explain, using an injections and withdrawals graph, why a rise in government spending of $50bn could raise national income by more than $50bn.

  • A rise in G raises J, so at the old Y injections exceed withdrawals and there is a net injection.
  • Income rises, and each extra dollar of income leads to further spending and to further withdrawals, until W equals J1 again. The rise in income is ΔJ × 1 ÷ MPW.
  • A limit: the size of the rise depends on the MPW.

B · Multiple choice questions

Choose an answer, then read the reason. Reworded from the paper and question number shown.

Top tips

  • Name the graph in the answer. Write J and W, label J1 and W1 where lines change, and label Y and Y1.
  • J is flat, W slopes up. Do not draw J as a sloping line unless the question says investment depends on income.
  • Show the net injection. At the old Y after a rise in J, say that J1 is greater than W, so income rises.
  • Use the multiplier from the slope. k = 1 ÷ MPW. A flatter W means a bigger multiplier.
  • Separate a shift from a rotation. Autonomous items shift a line. A change in a propensity rotates W.
  • Mark the gap vertically at YF. The size of the gap is the change in injections needed.
  • Evaluate with the propensities and the cause. The MPW, the type of unemployment, lags, and whether there is spare capacity.
