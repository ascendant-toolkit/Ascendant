Functions of Price in Resource Allocation

2.4.4: rationing, signalling (transmission of preferences) and incentivising, and what happens when price cannot work. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

In a market economy, price does three jobs in allocating resources. It rations scarce goods, because a higher price discourages demand until the quantity wanted matches the quantity available. It signals (transmits preferences), because a rising price tells producers that consumers want more of a good and a falling price tells them to cut back. It incentivises, because higher prices raise profit and so encourage firms to produce more, and lower prices push resources elsewhere; consumers are incentivised to buy less of dearer goods. Together these answer the questions what, how and for whom to produce without central direction, but the price mechanism works poorly where there are public goods, externalities, imperfect information or unequal incomes.

1 · The three functions (2.4.4)

FunctionWhat it meansExample
RationingA higher price reduces quantity demanded until it matches the limited supply; those unable or unwilling to pay do withoutA crop failure raises the price of wheat, so only buyers who value it most still buy
Signalling (transmission of preferences)Price changes tell producers what consumers want and tell consumers about scarcityRising demand for electric cars raises their price, signalling firms to build more
IncentivisingPrices and profits reward producers who respond and encourage consumers to switchHigher profit in solar panels attracts new firms; a dearer good pushes buyers to cheaper substitutes
  • The functions work together: demand rises, price rises (rations the limited supply), which signals to firms and incentivises them to produce more, until a new equilibrium is reached.
  • Price operates in product markets and factor markets too: high wages signal a shortage of skilled labour and attract workers.

2 · When it does not work well

  • Price controls. An effective maximum price stops price rising, so there is a shortage and rationing must be done another way (queues, coupons, black markets). The signal is lost and producers lack the incentive to supply more.
  • Public goods and externalities. Prices do not reflect social costs and benefits, so resources are misallocated (see 1.6 and 3.1).
  • Ability to pay, not need. Rationing by price favours those with income, so it may be seen as unfair.
  • Imperfect information and monopoly power distort signals; immobile factors mean resources respond slowly.
  • Direct provision (free education or health care) is financed by tax, so it is not allocated by the price mechanism. But much of the economy still uses prices.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Surge pricing for taxis

When demand jumps (rain or a concert ending), the fare rises. The high price rations seats to those who value them most, signals to drivers that demand is high and attracts more drivers onto the road.

Exam use: all three functions in one example.

Rising house prices in a city

Higher prices signal that homes are scarce there. Builders are incentivised by profit to build more, and some buyers are priced out or choose cheaper towns.

Exam use: signalling, incentivising and rationing.

Rent controls

A maximum rent below the market level cuts the signal to landlords, so fewer homes are built and let. A shortage follows, and rationing happens through waiting lists, contacts or illegal payments.

Exam use: how price controls stop the mechanism from working.

Wages and skills

A shortage of engineers raises their wages. The high pay signals to students that the career is rewarding and incentivises them to train, while firms hire fewer people at the higher cost.

Exam use: price in factor markets.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/23 · Q1(e) 6 marks A market report says a country's avocado market is not allocating resources successfully, because rising prices are encouraging farmers to clear forests. Discuss whether the price mechanism in the avocado market is working to allocate resources successfully.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 3): the market is working successfully, because price signals consumer preferences and responds to demand and supply (up to 3).
  • Analysis (up to 3): why it may not work successfully, such as environmental damage from clearing forests and strain on water supplies, a collapse in prices if new producers flood the market, or a change in health beliefs (up to 3). Maximum 5 for analysis.
  • Judgement (1): a valid conclusion about whether the price mechanism is working to allocate resources successfully.
PlanWorks: price signals demand and attracts producers. Fails: ignores environmental costs. Conclude.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The price mechanism appears to be working in one way: rising prices signal strong consumer demand and incentivise more farmers to switch to avocados, so supply responds. But prices do not capture the environmental cost of clearing forests and using scarce water, so too many resources may be directed to avocados. New producers could flood the market and cause prices to fall, which would leave farmers with losses.

Overall the mechanism is probably working for consumers and producers in the short term, but not for society, because external costs are ignored. It is not allocating resources successfully in the long run.

Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/21 · Q2(b) 12 marks Discuss whether the direct provision of goods and services by the government prevents the price mechanism from working effectively.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): how direct provision prevents the price mechanism from working effectively, for example it does not ration, signal or transmit preferences when goods are paid for by taxation (up to 4); and how it does not prevent it, for example education and health care can also be provided through prices, alongside the state (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): judgement on whether direct provision prevents the price mechanism from working effectively, with a conclusion (1).
PlanProvision financed by tax: no rationing or signal. But private provision also exists. Judge: depends on how much is provided.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

When the government provides education or health care free at the point of use, financed through taxation, price does not ration demand, signal what consumers want or incentivise producers, so the price mechanism does not operate for these goods. There may be over-consumption and long waiting lists. However, in many countries private schools and hospitals charge prices alongside state provision, so the price mechanism still rations, signals and transmits preferences in those markets. Direct provision usually covers only a small part of the economy.

Direct provision prevents the price mechanism from working for the goods the government supplies, but does not stop it elsewhere. The effect depends on how large the state sector is, so in most mixed economies the mechanism still works effectively overall.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/21 · Q2(b) 12 marks Discuss why in most mixed economies resources are mainly allocated using market forces and the price mechanism.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): market forces and the price mechanism, such as prices as signals and private decision-making (up to 4); government intervention, since 'mainly' makes clear that the market is not the only method, for example public and merit goods (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): judgement on why resources are mainly but not exclusively allocated by market forces, with a conclusion (1).
PlanWhy markets: signals, incentives, efficiency. Why state too: public and merit goods, equity. Judge: mainly, not only.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Market forces allocate most resources in a mixed economy because prices signal what consumers want, incentivise firms to produce it efficiently and ration scarce goods, without costly central planning. Private ownership gives firms a profit motive to innovate. But the government also intervenes, because markets will not provide public goods, under-provide merit goods and ignore externalities, and because incomes may be very unequal.

Resources are mainly allocated by prices because they work well for most goods, but not exclusively, because market failure and equity concerns justify state provision and regulation for some goods. The balance differs between countries.

Top tips

  • Name all three functions. Rationing, signalling (transmission of preferences) and incentivising.
  • Say who gets the message. Signals go to producers and consumers; incentives reward or discourage them.
  • Use a chain. Demand rises, price rises, firms supply more, a new equilibrium.
  • Price rations by ability to pay. That can be unfair, which is a limit of the mechanism.
  • Price caps break the mechanism. Shortage and no signal.
  • Public goods cannot be priced. The mechanism fails for non-excludable goods.
  • Evaluate with 'mainly'. Most economies use both markets and government.
