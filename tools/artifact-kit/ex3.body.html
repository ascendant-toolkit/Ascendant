Policies for Externalities: Taxes, Subsidies, Regulation and Bargaining

How a government can internalise an externality with a tax or subsidy, other policies such as regulation, permits and information, and when property rights and bargaining could work instead. Animated bargaining, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

To correct an externality the government can internalise it: a tax equal to the external cost moves MPC up to MSC, and a subsidy equal to the external benefit moves output up to the social optimum. It can also use regulation, tradable permits, information and state provision, or leave it to property rights and bargaining between the parties. Each approach has costs, and the right level is hard to find because external costs and benefits are hard to measure. A good answer chooses a tool, explains it with a diagram, and then weighs its limits.

1 · Internalising with a tax or a subsidy

  • Tax on a negative externality. With an external cost of $15 a unit, a tax of $15 shifts supply up to MSC, so the market output falls from 53 to the social optimum of 43 and the welfare loss of 75 disappears.
  • Subsidy for a positive externality. With an external benefit of $15, a subsidy of $15 shifts supply down so that output rises from 53 to the social optimum of 63.
  • Too low or too high. A tax below the external cost leaves some overproduction. A tax above it overcorrects, so output falls below the optimum and a new welfare loss appears. The Explore tab lets you test this.
Link to syllabus 8.1Tax incidence, how subsidies work and how permits cap pollution are explained in syllabus 8.1 part 2. Here the focus is on why the tax or subsidy is set equal to the external cost.

2 · Other policies

Regulation and bans

Standards, limits on emissions or night flights, and bans. Precise and quick, but costly to monitor and with no reward for doing better than the limit.

Tradable permits

A cap on total pollution with permits that firms can trade, so the cap is met at the lowest cost. The cap may be set wrongly.

Information and persuasion

Warnings, campaigns and negative advertising can change behaviour cheaply, but the effect may be small, especially for addictive goods.

Public provision and transport policy

Building public transport or providing the service directly, or congestion charges and quotas for road use. Costs fall on taxpayers.

3 · Property rights and bargaining

If property rights are clear and few people are involved, the parties may bargain without the government. Suppose a factory gains $100 from producing and the damage to a resident is $60. If the resident holds the right to clean air, the factory can pay any amount between $60 and $100 and both gain, so production goes ahead. If the damage is $150, no payment can satisfy both, and the factory stops. Either way the outcome is efficient, but it needs clear rights, few parties and low transaction costs, which is rarely true for pollution that affects thousands of people.

4 · Evaluating the policy options

  • Measuring the externality is hard (information failure), so a tax or subsidy may be set too high or too low.
  • Property rights and bargaining could solve some problems without government, if the parties are few and clear.
  • Government failure: intervention has its own costs and time lags.
  • Magnitude: if an externality is small, intervention may cost more than it gains.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. The numbers are simple on purpose, to show the idea.

Traffic congestion and congestion charges

Each driver adds to the delay of everyone else, which is a negative consumption externality. Singapore uses a quota system and electronic road pricing to raise the cost of driving into busy areas.

Exam use: data response questions on congestion asked for a diagram of a negative externality and for the effectiveness of policies. Compare charges (price mechanism) with quotas (quantity limits).

Charges for plastic bags

Several countries put a small charge on single-use plastic bags. It acts like a tax on the external cost of litter and waste, and use of bags fell sharply in many places.

Exam use: a tax on a negative consumption externality. Evaluate: the charge may be far from the true external cost, and substitutes (thicker bags) may have costs of their own.

Carbon pricing

Some governments tax carbon emissions or run cap and trade schemes, so that the price of emitting reflects the damage that emissions cause.

Exam use: internalising an external cost. Compare a tax (certain price, uncertain quantity) with permits (certain quantity, uncertain price).

Neighbours who settle by agreement

Disputes over noise or shared boundaries are sometimes settled by the parties themselves, perhaps with a payment. This works because few people are involved and rights are clear.

Exam use: an alternative to government intervention, for the "only solved by government" style of question.

Feb/March 2023 · 9708/42 · Q2 20 marks The use of air travel leads to market failure caused by negative externalities. With the help of a diagram, assess the extent to which a government can intervene to correct this market failure.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (14): market failure as allocative inefficiency: define allocative efficiency and link to maximising consumer satisfaction; a diagram of output where AR = MC consistent with efficiency. Define a negative externality (cost to society greater than that borne by the individual: a "spill-over"). A labelled diagram showing the market equilibrium, the allocatively efficient output and the welfare loss. Forms of intervention: taxation (raises the cost of flying, lowers demand and the number of flights), negative advertising, direct regulation.
  • Evaluation (6): how well each form of intervention works and what limits it (information about the size of the externality, effects on employment and tourism, whether the tax is set at the right level).
Diagram to drawDemand (MPB = MSB), MPC and MSC above it. Mark Qm and Qs, the prices, and the welfare loss triangle. Show the tax shifting MPC up to MSC.
KANE

A negative externality occurs when the production or consumption of a good imposes a cost on a third party that is not paid by the producer or consumer, so the marginal social cost is greater than the marginal private cost. Allocative efficiency occurs where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost, so that resources are allocated to maximise society's satisfaction.

Air travel is likely to lead to market failure because airlines and passengers ignore the cost of emissions and noise. On a diagram the market equilibrium is where demand (MPB) meets the airlines' marginal private cost (MPC), at output Qm. The marginal social cost curve lies above MPC by the external cost per flight, and the socially efficient output Qs is where MSB equals MSC. Because Qm is greater than Qs, the market over-produces and there is a welfare loss, shown by the triangle between MSC and MSB. The size of the welfare loss depends on how large the external cost is, which is difficult to measure.

A government could use a tax to correct the failure. A tax per flight equal to the external cost shifts the supply curve up to MSC, raises the price paid by passengers and reduces the number of flights to Qs, so allocative efficiency is achieved and the externality is internalised. A government could also use negative advertising to reduce demand, or regulation such as limits on night flights or emissions standards. A tax may not be fully effective if it is set at the wrong level because the external cost is hard to estimate, or if demand for flights is inelastic so that the number of flights falls little. It could also reduce employment and tourism income.

Regulation may give more precise control over noise or emissions but could be costly to monitor, and negative advertising may have a small effect on behaviour. Intervention may also suffer from government failure, such as time lags and lobbying by the industry. Overall, a government is likely to be able to reduce the market failure substantially through a combination of a tax and regulation, but it is unlikely to eliminate it because the external cost is difficult to measure and each policy has its own costs.

Top tips

  • Match the policy to the failure: a tax equal to the external cost for negative externalities, a subsidy for positive ones. Use the words "internalise the externality".
  • Draw the diagram with the tax or subsidy on it: shift MPC up to MSC (tax) or down (subsidy) and show the new output at the social optimum.
  • Always evaluate with measurement problems: the external cost is hard to value, so a tax may be too high or too low. Add elasticity, government failure and alternatives.
  • Consider non-government solutions. Property rights and bargaining, private action and persuasion are needed for "only solved by government" questions.
  • Compare two or more policies when asked for two. State which you think is better and why.
  • Test strong words: "only", "necessarily", "all". Show other causes and other solutions.
