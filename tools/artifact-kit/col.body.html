Types of Collusion

Formal and tacit collusion: cartels and their methods, price leadership and other tacit forms, why firms want to collude, why agreements so often collapse, and how the law responds. Animated diagrams, labs, drills, real cases and exam practice.

The whole idea in five sentences

Collusion is firms co-operating to limit competition instead of competing on price or output. It is tempting because a few firms acting together can behave like a monopolist and share a larger joint profit. Collusion takes two broad forms: formal (overt) collusion, an explicit agreement such as a cartel, and tacit (informal) collusion, where firms move in step with no agreement, for example by following a price leader. Formal collusion is illegal in most countries, and both forms tend to be unstable because each firm could gain by cheating. How long collusion lasts could depend on the number of firms, barriers to entry, how easy cheating is to spot, and the penalties.

1 · Why firms want to collude

In an oligopoly a few firms share the market, and each one's profit depends on what the others do. Competing on price tends to push price towards cost and profit down. If the firms instead agree to restrict output and keep price high, they could share monopoly-type profit.

Competing

Firms compete on price and output until price is close to MC. Here that means 16 units at $4, and profit near zero. Consumers gain.

Colluding

Firms act as if they were one monopolist: they produce where MR = MC, 8 units, and sell at $12. Joint profit is $64. Consumers pay more and buy less, and there is a deadweight loss.

3 · Methods of formal collusion

  • Price fixing. Members agree a common price, or a minimum price, to charge.
  • Output quotas. Each member agrees to limit output to a set share, so total output falls and the price rises. This is the usual method of a cartel such as OPEC.
  • Market sharing. Firms divide the market by region, customer type or product line so that they do not compete with each other.
  • Bid rigging. Firms agree in advance who will win a contract and the others put in higher bids, which keeps prices up in tenders for public or large projects.

4 · Forms of tacit collusion

  • Dominant-firm price leadership. The largest firm sets the price and smaller firms follow it, accepting it as the market price.
  • Barometric price leadership. A firm that is seen as best at judging the market, not necessarily the largest, changes price first and the others follow.
  • Focal-point pricing. Firms settle at an obvious price, such as a round number or last year's price, which each expects the others to charge.
  • Price signalling and information sharing. Firms announce intended price changes, or publish prices openly, so rivals can respond. This could shade into illegal collusion if it is used to agree prices.
Parallel prices are not proofRivals charging similar prices does not show collusion. Firms in competitive markets with similar costs and a homogeneous product could also charge similar prices. Regulators look for evidence of an agreement or a pattern that competition alone is unlikely to explain.

5 · Why collusion tends to break down

Collusion is easier when there are few firms, high barriers to entry, similar costs, similar products, transparent prices, stable demand and firms that expect to meet again and again. The reverse conditions make it harder.

  • The incentive to cheat. If the others keep to their quotas, a firm that secretly produces more or cuts price could win extra profit. Every member faces the same temptation, which is the prisoner's dilemma.
  • Entry. Outsiders can sell at a lower price and take the cartel's customers, or join the market attracted by high profit.
  • Different costs and aims. Low-cost firms would prefer a lower price than high-cost ones, so it is hard to agree on one price or quota.
  • Falling demand. In a downturn each member is tempted to cut price to fill spare capacity.
  • The law. Fines, criminal penalties and leniency programmes (the first member to confess is treated more leniently) raise the cost of collusion and encourage members to break ranks.

6 · Evaluating collusion

Likely harm to consumers

Higher prices and lower output, less incentive to innovate or cut costs, deadweight loss and a risk of X-inefficiency. Consumers may also face less choice.

Possible benefits

Stable prices could help planning and encourage investment in R&D from profits. In some cases co-operation can raise standards or spread costs, and legal forms (such as research joint ventures) could help consumers. Much depends on whether any gains are passed on.

6 · Multiple choice

Cases are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting a specific fine, date or company in an exam answer.

OPEC: output quotas

The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, with partner producers, agrees production targets for its members to influence the world oil price. Cutting output tends to raise prices, and members have often exceeded their targets, which shows the temptation to cheat.

Exam use: a standard example of output quotas, and of how cheating and non-members (such as shale producers, which supply more when the price rises) weaken a cartel. Note that OPEC is a group of governments, so ordinary competition law does not apply to it in the same way.

The vitamins cartel

In the 1990s several large chemical firms, including Roche and BASF, were found to have fixed prices and shared out volumes of vitamins sold around the world. Competition authorities in the US and the EU imposed large fines and some executives faced prosecution.

Exam use: price fixing and market sharing in a market with few producers and a homogeneous product, with a regulator uncovering it, often through a member that cooperated.

Lysine

In the mid-1990s a small group of producers of the feed additive lysine, including the US firm ADM, agreed on prices. The case became well known because an insider co-operated with the authorities and secretly recorded meetings, and several executives were convicted.

Exam use: evidence that cartels need meetings, which leave a trail, and that insiders can be tempted by leniency to turn on the others.

UK dairy and supermarkets

In 2011 the UK's Office of Fair Trading fined several large supermarkets and dairy processors, roughly £50m in total, for sharing information on planned retail price rises on products such as milk and cheese. The firms were not accused of fixing the price directly but of passing each other information about future prices.

Exam use: a case on the border between tacit and formal collusion: sharing intended price changes could remove the uncertainty that normally drives competition.

Price leadership and similar timing

In markets such as energy supply and fuel retail, rivals have sometimes raised prices within days of each other. In the UK the regulator's market investigation into energy found weak competition but not proof of collusion.

Exam use: an example of tacit collusion or price leadership, and of why parallel behaviour alone is not proof: it may simply reflect a common cost increase such as higher wholesale prices.

Airlines and price signalling

In the 1990s US airlines were investigated for using a shared fares database to signal planned fare changes to each other, with fares reversed if rivals did not follow. Airline fuel surcharges and air cargo prices have also drawn large fines in Europe and elsewhere.

Exam use: price signalling as a way to co-ordinate without ever agreeing, and a reminder that competition authorities pursue even indirect communication.

How the law responds

Agreements between firms to fix prices or share markets are illegal in the UK under the Competition Act 1998 (the Chapter I prohibition) and in the EU under its competition rules. The Competition and Markets Authority and the European Commission may fine firms up to 10% of their worldwide turnover. In the UK, individuals involved in cartels could face prison under the Enterprise Act 2002 and directors could be disqualified. Leniency programmes offer immunity or reduced fines to the first firm to report the cartel, which makes members distrust each other. Customers harmed by a cartel could also seek damages.

Q1 Distinguish 4 marks Distinguish between formal and tacit collusion.
Reminder"Distinguish" means say how the two differ. Define each, then give the key difference and an example of each. A short comparison is enough.

Formal (overt) collusion is an explicit agreement between firms to limit competition, for example a cartel that fixes prices or sets output quotas. Tacit (informal) collusion is co-ordination without any agreement, where firms follow each other's behaviour, for example by following a price leader.

The key difference is whether there is an agreement: formal collusion is usually illegal and easier to prove, whereas tacit collusion is harder to detect because similar prices could also result from competition. OPEC's output quotas are an example of formal collusion, while rival fuel retailers raising prices soon after a dominant firm does could be tacit.

Common mistakeDefining only one type, or describing both without saying what separates them.
Q2 Explain 6 marks Explain why a cartel may find it difficult to keep to its agreement.
ReminderGive the incentive to cheat (with a number if you can), then at least one more cause such as entry or differing costs, and say what happens to the cartel as a result.

A cartel is an agreement between firms to restrict output or fix price so that they earn a higher joint profit. Each member has an incentive to cheat: if the others keep to their quotas and price stays high, a firm that secretly sells more could earn extra profit at the expense of the others. Because every member faces the same temptation, the situation resembles a prisoner's dilemma, and if all of them cheat, total output rises, price falls and profit could end up lower than under the agreement.

Other factors may also make the agreement unstable: members with different costs could prefer different prices or quotas, and high profits could attract new entrants who sell outside the cartel at a lower price. For example, OPEC members have often produced more than their quotas, and non-members have supplied more when prices rise. The more firms there are, the harder it is to monitor everyone and to punish cheats.

Common mistakeSaying a cartel is illegal and stopping there. The question is about why the agreement is hard to keep, so the answer must focus on incentives and conditions.
Q3 Evaluate 12 marks Evaluate whether collusion between firms in an oligopoly always harms consumers.
ReminderThe word "always" is the hook: a strong claim. Explain how collusion could harm consumers, then give a developed case where it might not, then judge which is more convincing and why. Use "likely" and "could".

Collusion is firms co-operating to limit competition, for example by fixing price or output quotas. It is likely to harm consumers because the colluding firms behave like a monopolist: output is restricted, price rises towards the monopoly level, and a deadweight loss arises. Without competitive pressure, firms may also have less incentive to cut costs or innovate, so X-inefficiency could grow and choice may fall. The vitamins cartel of the 1990s, for instance, raised prices to buyers of a product with few substitutes.

However, the harm may not be certain. Some collusion is unstable, so higher prices could be short-lived, and entry or the threat of entry could limit how far prices rise. Stable prices and supernormal profit might also fund research and development, which could benefit consumers over time, and some forms of co-operation, such as sharing research costs, may be legal and raise product quality. Competition authorities can also punish collusion with fines, reducing how long consumers are harmed.

Overall, collusion that fixes price or output is likely to harm consumers, particularly where demand is inelastic and barriers to entry are high; the claim that it always does is too strong, because the size of the harm depends on how long the agreement lasts, whether entry is possible, and whether any extra profit is reinvested in ways that benefit consumers.

Common mistakeAccepting "always" without testing it, or listing the harms of monopoly with no discussion of when collusion might not harm consumers.

Top tips

  • Say which type you mean. Overt or formal (cartel) versus tacit or informal (price leadership). The two are often confused in answers.
  • Name the method. Price fixing, output quotas, market sharing, bid rigging, or price leadership. Methods are the application marks.
  • Explain the motive with monopoly. Firms collude to act like a monopolist and share the higher joint profit.
  • Explain instability with cheating. If the others keep to the agreement, one firm can gain by secretly producing more or cutting price. Mention the prisoner's dilemma.
  • Give the conditions. Few firms, high barriers, similar costs, similar products, transparent prices and stable demand make collusion more likely.
  • Parallel pricing is not proof. Evaluate tacit collusion by saying similar prices could result from competition or common costs.
  • Mention the law. Fines up to 10% of turnover, leniency programmes and criminal penalties raise the cost of collusion.
