Free Trade

What free trade is, its advantages and disadvantages, who gains and loses, and how to move towards it. Syllabus 6.1.2.

The big idea

Free trade is when countries buy and sell goods and services with each other without restrictions such as tariffs, quotas, subsidies or embargoes. It lets countries specialise and gain from trade, but it also exposes domestic firms to competition and makes economies dependent on others. Good answers weigh the advantages (lower prices, choice, competition, markets, growth) against the disadvantages (job losses, unfair competition, dependence) and say who gains and who loses.

Advantages of free trade

For consumers

  • Lower prices from competition and cheaper imports
  • Greater choice of goods and services
  • Higher quality and new products

For producers

  • Larger markets for exports: higher sales and profit
  • Economies of scale from higher output
  • Access to cheaper and better raw materials and technology
  • Competition raises efficiency

For the economy

  • Specialisation and more efficient use of resources
  • Higher output, economic growth and jobs in export industries
  • Lower costs and inflation pressure
  • Better international relations

For the government

  • More tax revenue as output and incomes rise
  • Pressure on firms to be efficient without subsidies

Disadvantages of free trade

  • Domestic firms and jobs may be lost when cheaper imports arrive, especially in infant industries and declining industries: structural unemployment.
  • Unfair competition such as dumping (selling below cost), or imports made with lower wages, environmental or safety standards.
  • Over-dependence on other countries for essentials such as food and fuel, and on exports.
  • Vulnerable to global shocks and price changes.
  • Loss of tariff revenue for the government; risk of a larger current account deficit.
  • Winners and losers: inequality may widen between workers who gain and those who lose jobs; poorer countries may find it hard to compete with richer, bigger ones.
  • Environmental costs from more transport and production in countries with weak regulations.

Who gains and who loses?

Moving towards free trade

A government can reduce protectionism by removing or cutting tariffs, raising or removing quotas, ending embargoes, removing subsidies and cutting red tape, and by joining trade agreements or trading blocs (for example free trade areas or the World Trade Organization). Other countries may then cut their own restrictions, opening export markets.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

The EU single market

Within the European Union goods, services, capital and people can move freely between member countries. Trade among members has grown strongly, though some industries have lost out.

Exam use: Free trade among countries, with benefits and costs.

China and the WTO

China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001 and its exports expanded rapidly. Consumers elsewhere benefited from cheaper goods, but some manufacturing industries and workers in other countries lost jobs.

Exam use: Winners and losers from free trade.

Vietnam's trade agreements

Vietnam signed free trade agreements with many countries and its exports of electronics and clothing grew quickly, helping growth and jobs.

Exam use: Free trade raising exports and growth.

Farmers and imports

Farmers in some countries have protested against imports of cheaper food from abroad, arguing that they cannot compete with large, subsidised producers.

Exam use: A disadvantage of free trade for particular groups.

Brexit and trade barriers

After the UK left the EU's single market, new customs checks added costs and paperwork for exporters, showing the benefits of low trade barriers.

Exam use: Benefits of reducing barriers.

Q1 Define 2 marks Define free trade. (May/June 2022, P23)
ReminderExports and imports between countries without restrictions such as tariffs or quotas.
Free trade is when countries export and import goods and services between each other without restrictions or protection such as tariffs or quotas.
Q2 Identify 2 marks State two benefits of free trade. (May/June 2020, P23)
ReminderChoice, competition, lower prices, specialisation.
Lower prices (or more choice). More competition (or more specialisation).
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain two benefits producers may gain from free trade. (Oct/Nov 2020, P22)
ReminderLarger markets; economies of scale; cheaper inputs; technology; competition.
Producers gain access to a larger global market. They can sell more, so revenue and profit may increase. They can produce on a larger scale. Higher output lets them benefit from economies of scale, lowering average costs, and they may specialise.
Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain two disadvantages of free trade for a country.
ReminderJob losses; infant industries; dependence; dumping.
Domestic firms may be unable to compete with cheaper imports. Some may close and workers lose their jobs, causing structural unemployment in those industries. The country may become dependent on other countries. If it relies on imports of food or fuel, supplies could be disrupted or prices could rise, harming consumers.
Q5 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a government could reduce protectionism and move towards free international trade. (Oct/Nov 2023, P22)
ReminderRemove tariffs, quotas, embargoes, subsidies; other countries may follow. Each with an effect.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A government could remove tariffs on imports. Imports become cheaper, so consumers pay less and firms that use imported inputs have lower costs. It could remove or raise import quotas, increasing the quantity of imports allowed, and end bans or embargoes. It could remove subsidies so firms' products are not artificially cheap. If other countries respond by removing their restrictions, the country's exports could be sold without barriers.
Q6 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not free trade between countries increases economic growth. (Oct/Nov 2024, P21)
ReminderFor: markets, scale, specialisation, competition, technology. Against: closures, unemployment, dependence, import surge, unequal gains.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Free trade is trade without restrictions and economic growth is a rise in real GDP. It could increase growth because firms have access to larger markets, so exports and output rise and they gain economies of scale. Specialisation and competition raise efficiency, and imports of cheaper inputs and technology raise productivity. These effects are larger for countries with strong export industries. However, domestic firms that cannot compete may close, reducing output and raising unemployment. Higher imports could create a current account deficit, and a country dependent on other markets may be hit by global shocks. Overall, free trade is likely to raise growth in the long run, particularly for efficient producers, but the benefits depend on a country's industries and how it supports workers who lose out.
Q7 Discuss 6 marks Discuss whether or not free trade is beneficial for a city such as Cambridge. (Oct/Nov 2023, P21)
ReminderApply to the city: firms, universities and students, tourists, consumers; but possible local job losses.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Free trade means there are no restrictions on imports and exports. Cambridge firms, such as hi-tech and education providers, may gain access to larger markets, increasing sales and jobs, and residents can buy cheaper and more varied goods. Universities may attract more international students and tourism may grow. These benefits are greater for the city's export-oriented industries. However, some local firms may face competition from cheaper imports and may close, and rising demand may increase housing costs. Overall, free trade is likely to benefit a city with strong export industries such as Cambridge, although some businesses and workers could lose.
Q8 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not a reduction in a country's trade protection will reduce its current account deficit. (Oct/Nov 2020, P23)
ReminderFor: other countries cut barriers, exports rise; efficiency. Against: imports rise faster; import surge; domestic firms close.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A current account deficit occurs when the value of imports is greater than the value of exports and other flows; reducing protection means lowering barriers to imports. It could reduce the deficit if other countries respond by reducing their barriers, so that exports rise, and if competition makes domestic firms more efficient and competitive. Cheaper imported inputs could also lower costs and help exports. This is more likely in the longer run. However, in the short run lower tariffs and quotas make imports cheaper, so imports could rise by more than exports, increasing the deficit, and domestic firms that close may reduce exports. Overall, removing protection may widen the deficit in the short run but could help over time if the country's firms become more competitive.

Top tips

  • Free trade = no tariffs, quotas, subsidies, embargoes. Name some.
  • Give benefits for specific groups. Consumers, producers, workers, government, economy.
  • Give a disadvantage for import-competing firms. Closures, structural unemployment, dependence.
  • Move towards free trade by removing restrictions. Tariffs, quotas, embargoes, subsidies, red tape; trade agreements.
  • Short run versus long run. Imports may rise first; competitiveness takes time.
