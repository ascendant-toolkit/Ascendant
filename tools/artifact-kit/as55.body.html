Supply-Side Policy

5.4: the meaning, objectives and tools of supply-side policy, and its effect on LRAS in AD/AS analysis. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Supply-side policy consists of government measures designed to increase aggregate supply, mainly by shifting the long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) curve to the right so the economy can produce more at full employment. Its objectives are to increase productivity and productive capacity, which supports economic growth, lower unemployment and lower inflation. Tools include spending on education and training, infrastructure, support for technology and research, tax incentives for work and investment, deregulation, privatisation and reducing labour market rigidities. The effects are mostly long term, uncertain and costly: in the short run extra government spending also raises AD, and policies can take years to raise productivity.

1 · Meaning and objectives (5.4.1 to 5.4.2)

  • Supply-side policies aim to shift the LRAS curve to the right, raising potential output. They are also called aggregate supply policies.
  • Objectives: raise productivity (output per worker); increase productive capacity (more and better factors of production); increase competition and efficiency; reduce structural unemployment.
  • Benefits if successful: higher real output, lower unemployment, lower price level (or lower inflation), better international competitiveness and a stronger current account.

2 · Tools (5.4.3)

TypeExamplesHow it raises supply
Investment in human capitalEducation, training and apprenticeships, health careMore skilled, productive, healthy workers; fewer skill mismatches
Infrastructure and technologyRoads, ports, broadband, research grants, tax relief for R&DLower firms' costs, raise productivity, speed innovation

3 · AD/AS analysis and evaluation (5.4.4)

  • Effect on the diagram: LRAS (and SRAS) shift right: real output rises, the price level falls, employment rises. If AD also rises, the price level may stay stable while output grows.
  • Short run: spending on education or roads is also government spending, so it raises AD first, which may raise prices before supply improves.
  • Limits: time lags (years before training raises productivity); high cost and opportunity cost; uncertain results (training may not match skills needed); incentive policies may widen inequality; deregulation can harm consumers or the environment; privatised firms may exploit market power.
Exam tipLink each tool to a shift of LRAS and explain how it raises productivity or capacity. Always separate short-run AD effects from long-run supply effects, and evaluate with time lags, cost and uncertainty.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Apprenticeships and training

A government funds training programmes so workers gain skills that employers need. Productivity rises and structural unemployment falls, shifting LRAS right, but the benefits take years to appear.

Exam use: human capital as a supply-side tool.

Broadband and transport links

New rail and fibre networks lower firms' costs and open new markets. Capital spending adds to AD at once and to productive capacity over the long run.

Exam use: infrastructure raising AS.

Cutting red tape

Simplifying the rules for starting a business makes it quicker and cheaper to set up firms, encouraging enterprise and competition. But less regulation might reduce consumer protection or environmental standards.

Exam use: deregulation and its drawbacks.

Privatisation

Selling a state-owned industry to private owners gives managers a profit motive and exposes the industry to competition, which may cut costs. It can also create a private monopoly that raises prices.

Exam use: market-based supply-side policy and its risks.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/23 · Q4(a) 8 marks A government embarks on a supply-side policy involving substantial additional funding for new road schemes and for new training schemes designed to raise labour productivity. Use an AD/AS diagram to explain the effects of this funding on the level of output in the economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): an accurate AD/AS diagram showing national output in equilibrium (2).
  • Application (up to 6): a clear understanding of aggregate demand (1), with the diagram showing that the AD curve shifts right because of the increase in government spending (1) and that this increases national output (1); a clear understanding of aggregate supply (1), with the diagram showing that the AS curve shifts right because of the increase in productivity (1) and that this increases national output (1).
PlanAD/AS diagram. Short run: spending shifts AD right, output up. Long run: productivity shifts AS right, output up again.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

On an AD/AS diagram with the price level and real output on the axes, the initial equilibrium is where AD meets SRAS.

Funding for roads and training is government spending, a component of AD, so AD shifts to the right and national output rises in the short run. Over the longer term, the training raises labour productivity and the roads lower firms' costs, so AS shifts to the right as well, raising national output further and holding down the price level.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/23 · Q4(b) 12 marks Discuss the extent to which a supply-side policy involving new road schemes and new training schemes will increase the rate of inflation in the economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the short-run effect: spending raises AD, which may raise prices if there is no spare capacity (up to 4); the long-run effect: higher productivity and capacity raise AS, which reduces inflationary pressure (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on the extent to which the policy increases inflation, depending on spare capacity, time lags and the size of the AS effect, with a conclusion.
PlanShort run: AD up, inflation risk if at capacity. Long run: AS up, price level falls. Judge: depends on spare capacity and time lags.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

In the short run, spending on roads and training is injected into the economy as government spending and raises AD. If the economy has no spare capacity, the rise in AD mainly raises the price level, so inflation could increase. Over time, better roads and a more productive workforce lower costs and raise capacity, so AS shifts right, which reduces inflationary pressure and may lower the price level.

The policy is more likely to raise inflation in the short run, particularly in an economy near full capacity, but the long-run effect is likely to reduce it. Overall the extent of any inflation depends on spare capacity and on how quickly supply improves.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess whether supply-side policy is the most effective way to achieve long-run economic growth.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): how supply-side policies, such as education, training, infrastructure and technology, raise productive capacity and shift LRAS right, giving sustainable growth (up to 4); the limits of supply-side policy (cost, time lags, uncertainty) and alternatives such as expansionary fiscal or monetary policy and their limits (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether supply-side policy is the most effective way to achieve long-run growth, with a conclusion.
PlanSupply-side: LRAS shifts right, sustainable. Limits: lags, cost. Demand-side: raises growth only if spare capacity. Judge: best for long-run growth, with demand support.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Supply-side policies raise productivity and productive capacity, shifting LRAS to the right, so output can grow without inflation. Education, training and infrastructure all do this. They take years to work, cost money and may not be well targeted, so they are uncertain. Expansionary fiscal and monetary policy raise AD and can raise output in the short run, but without spare capacity they only raise prices, so they cannot achieve long-run growth by themselves.

Supply-side policy is the most effective way to achieve long-run growth, because only increases in capacity can sustain higher output. But it needs sufficient demand to use the extra capacity, so a combination of policies is likely to work best.

Based on Feb/March 2025 · 9708/22 · Q5(b) 12 marks Assess the extent to which supply-side policy is the best method to reduce the rate of inflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): how supply-side policy reduces inflation by shifting AS to the right, lowering costs and the price level (up to 4); at least one alternative, such as contractionary monetary or fiscal policy, analysed, with the limits of each (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement depending on the cause of inflation, time lags and the costs of each policy, with a conclusion.
PlanSupply-side: lowers costs, price level falls, long run. Alternatives: monetary, fiscal: quicker but cut output. Judge by cause of inflation and time horizon.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Supply-side policies such as training and investment in technology raise productivity, lower unit costs and shift AS to the right, which reduces the price level without cutting output. They are particularly suited to cost-push inflation. However, they take years to work and are costly. Contractionary monetary policy, through higher interest rates, reduces AD more quickly but cuts output and employment, and fiscal policy has similar effects.

Supply-side policy is likely to be the best long-run method, because it reduces inflation while raising output, but it is too slow to deal with high inflation in the short run, when monetary policy is usually needed. The best choice depends on the cause of inflation and the time available.

Top tips

  • Define it. Policies to increase aggregate supply, shifting LRAS right.
  • Name tools and explain each. Education, infrastructure, technology, deregulation, incentives.
  • Draw LRAS shifting right. Output up, price level down.
  • Separate short run from long run. Spending raises AD first; supply improves later.
  • Mention time lags and cost. Years to work; opportunity cost.
  • Say which problems it suits. Structural unemployment, cost-push inflation.
  • Judge with a mix of policies. Supply needs demand to be used.
