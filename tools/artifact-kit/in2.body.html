Measurement of Inflation

How the Consumer Prices Index is built, how to calculate the inflation rate from it, and its limitations. Syllabus 4.7.2.

The big idea

Inflation is measured using the Consumer Prices Index (CPI): a measure of the average change in the prices of a basket of goods and services bought by a typical household, with each item weighted by its importance in household spending. The inflation rate is the percentage change in the CPI over a year.

How a CPI is built

  1. Choose a basket. Goods and services typical households buy (food, housing, transport, clothes, leisure...), based on household spending surveys.
  2. Give weights. Each item is weighted by the share of household spending it takes. Housing or food has a larger weight than a rarely bought item.
  3. Collect prices regularly and compare them with the prices in the base year, when the index is set to 100.
  4. Calculate the index as a weighted average of the price changes.
  5. Calculate the inflation rate from the change in the index.
Inflation rate = (CPI this year − CPI last year) ÷ CPI last year × 100

Weighted example: food (weight 40%) prices +5%, housing (30%) +2%, transport (20%) +10%, leisure (10%) −5%. Inflation = 0.4 × 5 + 0.3 × 2 + 0.2 × 10 + 0.1 × (−5) = 2.0 + 0.6 + 2.0 − 0.5 = 4.1%.

Reading index numbers

If the CPI is 100 in the base year and 108 later, prices are on average 8% higher than in the base year. If the CPI goes from 108 to 111.24 the next year, inflation was (111.24 − 108) ÷ 108 × 100 = 3%.

Limitations of the CPI

  • No household is typical. A pensioner or a student spends differently from the average, so their personal inflation rate may differ.
  • The basket changes over time (new products, changing tastes), and weights may be out of date.
  • Quality changes: a more expensive phone may be better, so not all of the rise is inflation.
  • Substitution: people switch to cheaper goods when prices rise.
  • Regional and data differences and the informal economy make measurement harder.

Why people disagree with the official figure

Official inflation can feel different from lived experience: if the items you buy most often (such as food and fuel) rise faster than the average, your own inflation is higher.

Exam use: A limitation of the CPI.

Updating the basket

Statistical offices review the basket regularly: new items such as streaming services and smartphones are added and old items removed.

Exam use: The basket is not fixed.

Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain two reasons why the CPI may not accurately show the inflation experienced by every household.
ReminderDifferent spending patterns; quality changes; substitution; weights out of date.
Households spend their income differently. A pensioner may spend a larger share on housing and heating than the typical household, so if those prices rise fast their personal inflation could be higher than the CPI. The basket and weights may be out of date. If consumers switch to cheaper goods or new products appear, the index may not reflect what households actually buy.

Top tips

  • CPI = weighted basket. Two words that earn the definition marks: basket and weights.
  • Inflation rate = (new CPI − old CPI) ÷ old CPI × 100. Divide by the earlier figure.
  • Weights matter. Larger share of spending means more effect.
  • Limitations. No household is typical; quality; substitution; outdated basket.
