Actual and Potential Growth, and Output Gaps

9.2.1 and 9.2.2: what actual and potential growth are, how output gaps are measured, and what a positive or negative gap means for inflation and unemployment. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Actual growth is the percentage rise in real GDP from one period to the next. Potential growth is the rise in the economy's productive capacity, shown by the LRAS curve or the PPC shifting outwards. The output gap is actual output minus potential output: a positive gap means the economy is running above capacity (inflation pressure), a negative gap means spare capacity (unemployment). Governments try to keep the gap near zero while raising potential growth over time.

1 · Actual growth and potential growth

Actual growth (9.2.1)

The increase in real GDP over a period, usually a year. On a PPC it is a move from inside the curve towards it. On AD and AS it is a rise in real output at the current price-level equilibrium.

growth % = (GDP₂ − GDP₁) ÷ GDP₁ × 100

Potential growth (9.2.1)

The increase in the maximum output the economy can sustain. It comes from more or better factors of production and from technology. On a PPC the curve moves out. On AD and AS the LRAS shifts right.

Short run and long runActual growth can be fast for a while by using up spare capacity, but it cannot be kept up beyond potential. Only potential growth raises the speed limit. Actual growth can also be negative in a recession; potential growth is rarely negative (a war or disaster that destroys capital is an exception).

Two ways to say the same thingThe output gap is measured in output (real GDP). The inflationary and deflationary gaps from the circular flow (9.1.3) are measured in expenditure: how far aggregate demand is above or below the level that gives full employment. They are linked, but if a question asks for a deflationary gap on a Keynesian cross, measure the vertical gap in spending, not output.

3 · What shifts potential output?

  • More factors: a larger labour force (migration, higher participation), more capital (investment), more land and resources.
  • Better factors: education and training, better health, new technology and research.
  • Better use of factors: competition, flexible labour markets, better infrastructure, lower barriers to enterprise.

These are the supply-side policies covered in 9.2.4. Anything that damages capacity (war, natural disaster, loss of skills) shifts potential output left.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Japan after 1990: a long negative gap

After an asset-price bubble burst, Japan's actual output stayed below potential for much of the 1990s and 2000s. Firms and banks held back, demand was weak and prices barely rose, and policy makers kept interest rates near zero for years.

Exam use: an example of a persistent negative output gap with low inflation. Say that spare capacity can last a long time if demand does not recover.

The post-pandemic rebound and inflation

In 2021 and 2022 many economies reopened quickly. Spending rose faster than supply chains and workforces could recover, firms struggled to hire, and inflation rose sharply in many countries.

Exam use: a positive output gap and demand-pull pressure combined with supply bottlenecks. Evaluate by noting that part of the inflation was cost-push, not only a gap.

A new motorway and a skills programme

A government that builds transport links and trains workers is trying to shift potential output. Over time firms may be able to move goods faster and fill skilled jobs, so the LRAS shifts right.

Exam use: potential growth comes from the supply side. Name the policy, link it to a factor of production, and say it takes time.

A storm destroys a region's factories

Capital and housing are lost, so the economy's capacity falls for a time. Actual output drops as well, but the gap may be small because potential has fallen too.

Exam use: potential output can fall, not only rise. Separate a loss of capacity from a fall in demand.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/43 · Q1(a) and (b) 3 + 4 marks (a) What is meant by economic growth? (b) Explain how economic growth is linked to a country's level of economic development.
What the mark scheme wants
  • (a) A rise in real national output (real GDP) over time, usually a year. Mention that it is measured as a percentage change, and that real means adjusted for inflation.
  • (b) Growth raises income per head and can fund health, education and infrastructure, which raise living standards. But development is wider: income distribution, life expectancy, schooling and the environment can lag behind growth.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/42 · Q1(b) 4 marks A government starts a large programme of ports, roads and railways. Explain, with the help of a diagram, the likely effect on potential growth and on actual growth.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Actual growth: higher real GDP using existing resources, as construction spending and jobs raise output (1 to 2 marks).
  • Potential growth: better transport lowers firms' costs and widens the market, so productive capacity rises. The PPC moves outwards, or the LRAS shifts right (1 to 2 marks).
  • Diagram: a PPC with a move towards the curve and an outward shift of the curve, or AD and AS with LRAS.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/43 · Q1(b) 5 marks Define actual economic growth. The table shows a country's real GDP growth and its unemployment rate over six years. Analyse the relationship between growth and unemployment.

Year: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. GDP growth (%): 3.1, 2.4, −1.8, 0.5, 2.9, 3.4. Unemployment (%): 5.2, 5.0, 7.9, 8.1, 7.0, 6.1.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Definition: the percentage change in real GDP over a period (2 marks).
  • Inverse relationship: when growth falls or turns negative (year 3), unemployment rises sharply, because firms cut output and jobs.
  • Use the data: unemployment peaked at 8.1% in year 4 after the fall in year 3, then eased as growth returned. Unemployment lags behind growth.
  • Credit a note that growth of only 0.5% in year 4 was too weak to cut unemployment.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/41 and 43 · Q6(a) 12 marks Explain what economists mean by an inflationary gap and discuss why it is considered an economic problem. Use a diagram or diagrams to support your answer.
What the mark scheme wants
  • A clear definition and an accurate, labelled diagram showing the gap (Keynesian cross, or AD and AS with LRAS).
  • At least three negative effects, with a view on which matters most: rising inflation expectations, higher wage and cost pressure, loss of export competitiveness and a worse current account, redistribution from savers and lenders to borrowers, uncertainty that holds back investment.
  • Level 4 (9 to 12) needs the effects analysed and their relative importance judged, not just listed.
PlanDefine. Draw. Three effects in order of importance with cause and effect. Short judgement.
Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/41 · Q7 25 marks A government's choice to run a larger budget deficit as a share of GDP depends largely on whether the output gap is positive or negative. Explain and evaluate this statement, with the help of a diagram.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Define the output gap and show a negative gap on AD and AS with LRAS. Expansionary fiscal policy (higher spending or lower taxes) raises AD, so a bigger deficit may close a negative gap, helped by the multiplier.
  • With a positive gap the opposite applies: a smaller deficit or a surplus reduces AD and eases inflation.
  • Evaluation: the deficit also depends on other factors (debt level, interest rates and crowding out, bond market confidence, automatic stabilisers, political goals, the size of the multiplier), the output gap is hard to measure, and time lags.
  • A conclusion that weighs the output gap against these other influences.
PlanDefine gap. Diagram with a negative gap and AD shift. Positive gap case. Then at least three evaluation points and a judgement.
Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/43 · Q4 20 marks With the help of a diagram, assess how effective fiscal policy is in closing a negative output gap.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge and analysis (14): fiscal policy as changes in government spending and taxes; negative gap as spare capacity caused by weak demand; a budget deficit adds to AD, magnified by the multiplier, raising output and employment. Diagram: AD shifting right towards LRAS.
  • Evaluation (6): higher borrowing may raise interest rates and crowd out private investment; the size of the gap is hard to forecast; time lags; the multiplier may be small if people save the extra income or spend on imports; the debt burden; if the gap is a supply problem, demand policy will not help.
PlanDefine and draw. Spending route, tax route, multiplier. Then evaluate with crowding out, forecasting, leakages and debt. End with a clear judgement.

B · Multiple choice questions (Paper 3)

Top tips

  • Define growth with 'real'. Say the percentage change in real GDP over time, not just 'a rise in output'.
  • Keep actual and potential apart. Actual growth is a move towards or beyond the PPC. Potential growth is the PPC or LRAS shifting. Say which one the question asks for.
  • Name the sign of the gap first. Positive means actual above potential; negative means below. Then give the symptoms: inflation and low unemployment, or spare capacity and unemployment.
  • Use the formula for gap questions. (actual − potential) ÷ potential × 100. Watch the sign.
  • Draw LRAS as a vertical line at potential output. Label the actual equilibrium to its left or right. A diagram can lift an answer from Level 2.
  • Do not mix up gaps. An output gap is in output. An inflationary or deflationary gap is in expenditure on a Keynesian cross.
  • Evaluate with measurement. Potential output cannot be observed, so output gaps are estimates and are often revised.
