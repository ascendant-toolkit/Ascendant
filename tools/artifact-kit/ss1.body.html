Definition of Supply-Side Policy

What supply-side policy is, how it shifts aggregate supply and the PPC, and how it differs from demand-side policy. Syllabus 4.4.1.

The big idea

Supply-side policy is the use of government measures to increase the economy's ability to produce: its productive capacity and aggregate supply. Where fiscal and monetary policy work on demand (AD), supply-side policy works on supply (AS). A successful policy shifts AS to the right, so the economy can produce more goods and services at each price level.

Why aggregate supply matters

Aggregate supply (AS) is the total output firms are willing to supply at each price level. It depends on the quantity and quality of the factors of production (land, labour, capital, enterprise) and on how efficiently firms use them. Anything that raises these shifts AS to the right.

If AD shifts right

Output rises, but the price level rises too, and if the economy is close to full capacity most of the effect is inflation.

If AS shifts right

Output rises and the price level falls (or rises by less). Growth with less inflation: the best of both.

The same idea appears on a production possibility curve: a successful supply-side policy shifts the PPC outwards, because the economy can now produce more of everything.

What supply-side policy aims to do

  • Raise productivity and potential output, so long-run growth is higher.
  • Reduce unemployment, especially structural unemployment where workers' skills do not match vacancies.
  • Reduce firms' costs, which helps hold prices down and make exports more competitive.
  • Improve efficiency and encourage firms to compete and invest.

Demand-side compared with supply-side

Demand-side (fiscal and monetary)Supply-side
Works onAggregate demandAggregate supply
ToolsTaxes, government spending, interest rates, money supply, exchange rateEducation, infrastructure, labour market reforms, tax cuts, deregulation, incentives, privatisation
Time to workCan be quick (months)Usually slow (years)
Main effectShort-run changes in output and pricesLong-run growth in productive capacity
Main riskInflation, deficitCosts, winners and losers, uncertain results

Supply-side policies are sometimes called microeconomic policies because many work by changing how individual markets, firms and workers behave.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Germany's labour market reforms

In the early 2000s Germany reformed its labour market, making unemployment benefits conditional on job search and making it easier to offer flexible jobs. Unemployment fell over the following years, though critics say some of the new jobs were low paid.

Exam use: A supply-side measure aimed at structural unemployment, with an evaluation point about inequality.

China's market reforms

From the late 1970s China gradually deregulated markets, allowed private firms and invested heavily in infrastructure and education. Output grew very fast for decades.

Exam use: Several measures together raising productive capacity. A past paper uses China as a case.

Training schemes

Several governments, for example Singapore, fund training accounts or apprenticeships so workers can learn new skills as industries change.

Exam use: Education and training raising the quality of labour, with a long time lag.

Q1 Define 2 marks What is meant by a supply-side policy?
ReminderTwo ideas: government measures, and that they raise aggregate supply or productive capacity.
A supply-side policy is a government measure designed to increase aggregate supply, by raising the quantity or quality of resources or improving the efficiency with which they are used.
Common mistakeDescribing demand-side measures such as cutting taxes to raise spending. Supply-side is about the ability to produce.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain one difference between supply-side policy and demand-side policy.
ReminderUse 'whereas'. Compare what they shift, or how long they take.
Supply-side policy aims to increase aggregate supply and productive capacity, whereas demand-side policy, such as fiscal or monetary policy, aims to change aggregate demand. Supply-side effects, such as better skills or new roads, usually take years to appear, whereas a change in interest rates or government spending can affect spending much more quickly.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Using an AD/AS diagram, explain how a successful supply-side policy could affect real GDP and the price level.
ReminderDraw AS shifting right, label Y to Y₁ and P to P₁, then explain in words.
A successful supply-side policy increases the economy's productive capacity, so aggregate supply shifts to the right. At each price level firms can now supply more, so real GDP rises from Y to Y₁ and the price level falls from P to P₁, or rises by less than it otherwise would. For example, better training could raise workers' productivity.
Q4 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how supply-side policy could help to reduce inflationary pressure.
ReminderChain: policy, productivity or costs, AS shifts right, price level. Name one measure.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Supply-side policy aims to raise aggregate supply. For example, spending on training or infrastructure could raise productivity and lower firms' costs per unit. Lower costs could allow firms to hold or cut prices, and AS shifts to the right. Output is higher at each price level, so the price level may fall or rise more slowly, even if demand grows. This could reduce cost-push and demand-pull pressure. However, the effect could take years to appear.

Top tips

  • Supply-side = raise AS. Productive capacity, not spending.
  • Draw AS shifting right. Label Y to Y₁ and P to P₁; say output rises and prices fall.
  • Name a measure. Training, infrastructure, tax cuts, privatisation, deregulation.
  • Time lags. Supply-side effects take years: use this in evaluation.
  • Contrast with demand-side. Demand-side can be quicker but may be more inflationary.
