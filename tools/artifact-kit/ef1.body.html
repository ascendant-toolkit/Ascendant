Types of Efficiency: Productive, Dynamic, X-Inefficiency and Pareto

Productive, dynamic and Pareto efficiency and X-inefficiency: what each means, how it is measured and how the production possibility curve shows Pareto improvements. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Resources are scarce, so economists ask whether they are used efficiently, and there are several kinds of efficiency. Productive efficiency means output at the lowest possible average cost (the minimum of the AC curve, or on the production possibility curve). Dynamic efficiency means costs that fall over time through investment and innovation, while X-inefficiency means costs above the minimum because there is no pressure to control them. Pareto efficiency is the strictest test: no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.

Static versus dynamicProductive and allocative efficiency are static (a snapshot at one moment). Dynamic efficiency is about change over time. A firm can be statically inefficient yet dynamically efficient: a monopoly charging a high price now may invest in a cost-cutting innovation.

2 · Measuring X-inefficiency

A firm's lowest possible average cost is $10 but it actually produces at $12. Its X-inefficiency is $2 a unit, or 20% above the minimum. A firm with competitors has an incentive to close the gap, because rivals with lower costs could undercut it. A sole supplier protected by barriers to entry has less pressure, so costs may drift up.

3 · Pareto improvements

A Pareto improvement is a change that makes at least one person better off and nobody worse off. When no such change remains, the economy is Pareto efficient. A move from inside the production possibility curve to a point on it can be a Pareto improvement, because more of both goods is possible. Most real policies make some people worse off, so they are not strict Pareto improvements, although the gainers might compensate the losers.

Pareto and equityA Pareto efficient outcome can be very unequal. Efficiency says nothing about fairness, which is why equity is covered separately (syllabus 8.2).

4 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Pharmaceutical research

Drug companies spend heavily on research and rely on patents to earn profits. This may be dynamic efficiency: new treatments and lower costs in time. But patent protection gives monopoly power and may mean high prices today.

Exam use: a case where static inefficiency (high price now) could be traded for dynamic efficiency (innovation). A good evaluation point on monopolies.

A state-owned firm without competition

A public sector firm that is the only provider may have little pressure to keep costs down. Unit costs could rise above the minimum, which is X-inefficiency.

Exam use: an example of X-inefficiency, and a government failure point when the government itself is the producer.

A fall in unemployment

When people who want to work are idle, the economy is inside its production possibility curve. Helping them into jobs can make them better off at no cost to anyone else, which is close to a Pareto improvement.

Exam use: a move from inside the PPC to the PPC.

Feb/March 2022 · 9708/42 · Q2(a) 12 marks Explain what is meant by dynamic efficiency and consider which type of market structure is most likely to achieve dynamic efficiency.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Dynamic efficiency: profits reinvested in R&D to reduce long-run average cost. Show that the whole average cost curve shifts down.
  • Perfect competition and monopolistic competition: small firms, little profit to invest, unlikely. Monopoly and oligopoly: large profits, more likely. Monopoly is most likely because of full market control; an oligopoly may spend on non-price competition.
Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/43 · Q2(a)(b) 12 + 13 marks (a) Explain what is meant by 'Pareto optimality' and whether it is correct to say that an optimal allocation of resources would result if a good's price equalled its marginal cost. (b) Discuss why an optimal allocation of resources might fail to occur in practice and consider what could be done about this failure.
What the mark scheme wants
  • (a) Pareto criterion (nobody better off without someone worse off); P = MC gives allocative efficiency but should be MSC once externalities are included. Level 4 needs positive and negative externalities.
  • (b) Reasons: public goods, market control, externalities, imperfect information. Remedies: taxes, subsidies, regulation, persuasion, ownership. Level 4 needs four issues analysed and evaluated (any mix of causes and remedies).
Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/43 · Q2(b) 13 marks Pareto optimality can only be achieved when an economy is both productively and allocatively efficient. This will create a more equitable society. Discuss the extent to which you agree with this statement.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Productive efficiency: minimum average cost, or on the production possibility boundary. Allocative efficiency: P = MC; for the economy, the specific point on the PPC that maximises consumer satisfaction.
  • Pareto optimality: no one can be better off without making someone worse off. Points below the PPC, or at the wrong point on it, fail this.
  • Then the second half of the statement: equity is a different aim. A Pareto optimal outcome can be very unequal.
May/June 2021 · 9708/41 and 43 · Q1(c) 5 marks The article says that 'efficiency is measured by relating inputs to outputs'. Is this how economic theory states that efficiency is determined?

Answer: partly. Productive efficiency compares cost with output (inputs to outputs), but allocative efficiency is about whether resources match consumers' preferences (P = MC), not just a ratio of inputs to outputs.

Top tips

  • Define efficiency in terms of resources. The mark scheme rewards a link to the use of scarce resources for each type.
  • Give the condition for each: productive = minimum AC (MC = AC), allocative = P = MC (or MSB = MSC), dynamic = lower LRAC over time, Pareto = no one better off without someone worse off.
  • Say how each type differs: productive efficiency is about cost, allocative about what is produced, dynamic about change over time, and Pareto about any possible reallocation.
