Population Growth, Structure and Urbanisation

11.4.1: measuring population change, the causes of changes in birth rate, death rate and migration, the dependency ratio, optimum population and urbanisation. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Population change is the sum of natural increase (birth rate minus death rate) and net migration. In developing countries high birth rates and falling death rates give rapid growth and a young population with a high dependency ratio; in developed countries low birth rates and long life expectancy give an ageing population and a different dependency problem. The optimum population is the size that gives the highest income per head with the existing resources, capital and technology, and urbanisation (the share of people living in towns and cities) rises as economies develop. Each has costs and benefits for growth and living standards.

2 · Causes of changes in birth, death and migration

  • Birth rate: falls with higher incomes, better education (especially of women), access to contraception, urbanisation (children are costly in cities), pensions that remove the need for children to support old age, and lower infant mortality (parents need fewer births to ensure survivors). Higher where children are needed as farm labour, where infant mortality is high, and where culture or religion favours large families.
  • Death rate: falls with better nutrition, clean water and sanitation, health care and vaccination. It rises with disease epidemics, famine, conflict and an ageing population.
  • Infant mortality: falls with maternal health care, immunisation, clean water and education.
  • Net migration: pull factors (jobs, higher wages, services, safety) and push factors (unemployment, poverty, conflict, climate) together with immigration rules. Rural-urban migration within a country is also important in development.

The demographic transition

As an economy develops, death rates fall first (health and food improve) and birth rates fall later, so population growth speeds up and then slows: stage 1 both high; stage 2 death rate falls, population rises fast; stage 3 birth rate falls; stage 4 both low, with an ageing population.

3 · Population structure and the dependency ratio

  • Dependency ratio = (people under 15 + people over 64) ÷ (people aged 15 to 64) × 100. It shows how many dependants each 100 workers support.
  • Developing economies usually have a young population (a broad base on the age pyramid) and a high dependency ratio from children. Income per head is low, so saving, investment and spending on education are squeezed.
  • Developed economies often have an ageing population: low birth rates and long life expectancy. The old-age dependency ratio rises, so the workforce shrinks, and more is needed for pensions and health care, which could raise taxes or debt.
  • A fall in the birth rate can create a demographic dividend: a large working-age population with fewer dependants can raise savings, investment and growth, if there are jobs for them.

4 · Optimum population

  • The optimum population is the size of population that, with the available resources, capital and technology, gives the highest output (income) per head, maximising the standard of living.
  • Under-population: too few workers to use resources fully; more people would raise output per head (division of labour, bigger markets). Over-population: too many for the resources; diminishing returns lower output per head.
  • The optimum is not fixed: better technology, more capital, higher labour productivity, new resources and more education raise it, so a country can support more people. Resource depletion lowers it.
  • Note that a rise in birth rate does not raise the optimum; it changes the actual population.

5 · Urbanisation

  • Urbanisation is the growing share of the population living in cities. It rises as people move from low-productivity farming to jobs in industry and services (pull) and as rural poverty pushes them out.
  • Benefits: higher productivity (agglomeration, shared infrastructure), larger markets, easier access to health, education and services, and a lower birth rate.
  • Costs: slums and informal housing, congestion and pollution, strain on water, sanitation and transport, urban unemployment, and loss of labour in rural areas.
Evaluation pointWhether a rising or falling population is a problem depends on the structure and on whether output per head is rising. A rapid rise in a poor country may strain resources, but a younger population can supply labour and demand. A falling population in a rich country can cause a shortage of workers, but productivity growth and immigration can offset it.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

China's changing population

China's one-child policy lowered the birth rate, and later the two-child and three-child policies tried to raise it. The working-age population has started to shrink and the share of older people is rising. Economists link this to a smaller labour force, higher pension and health costs and slower growth.

Exam use: an ageing population, the dependency ratio and the optimum population.

Japan's ageing society

Japan has one of the highest shares of older people and a falling population. Responses include raising the retirement age, encouraging more women and older people to work, using robots and technology, and slowly opening up to migrant workers.

Exam use: the effects of an ageing population in a developed economy.

Sub-Saharan Africa's young population

Many countries in the region have median ages below 20 and birth rates well above the world average. The large working-age population to come could bring a demographic dividend if jobs, education and health improve, or high unemployment if they do not.

Exam use: a youthful structure, the dependency ratio and the demographic dividend.

Rapid urbanisation in Lagos and Dhaka

Cities in developing countries have grown quickly as people move from farms. The cities offer jobs and services but many residents live in informal settlements with poor sanitation and long commutes, which shows the costs of urbanisation as well as the gains.

Exam use: the costs and benefits of urbanisation.

One observation is that a smaller number of young people is entering the labour force. This means a smaller pool of potential workers, innovators and entrepreneurs, so the growth of the labour force and productivity could slow. Aggregate supply may grow more slowly and GDP could be lower.

A second observation is that the share of older people is rising. This could raise government spending on pensions, health care and social services. If funded by borrowing or higher taxes, the budget deficit may rise or private spending and investment may fall, which could reduce GDP.

PlanPart 1: developing: dependency, saving, resources. Part 2: developed: ageing, pensions, labour shortage. Compare which is greater. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

In a developing economy, a high birth rate gives a young population and a high dependency ratio. A large share of income may be spent on feeding and educating children, so saving and investment could be low, and rapid growth could put pressure on land, jobs and public services. Output per head may fall if capital does not grow as fast as the population.

In a developed economy, falling population and ageing mean fewer workers supporting more pensioners. This could raise taxes or debt, lower the labour force and slow growth, although productivity growth, higher retirement ages and immigration could offset this. The developed economy has more resources to adapt, whereas developing economies have fewer, so the problems of rapid growth may be at least as serious. Overall the statement is probably only partly true, depending on how quickly each economy adjusts.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/43 · Q6 25 marks Developing economies have a high proportion of economically inactive people relative to the labour force (a high dependency ratio). This is the main reason why the standard of living is low in developing economies. Evaluate this statement.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explain the dependency ratio and why it is high in developing economies (high birth rates, so many children).
  • Link a high dependency ratio to low living standards: insufficient wealth creation per person, less tax revenue for public and merit goods, lower saving and investment.
  • Evaluation: other factors also contribute: reliance on primary products, low investment, weak education and training, limited resources; living standards are hard to measure and GDP per head omits many factors. So it is hard to accept that the dependency ratio is the main reason.
PlanDefine. Link to income and tax. Other causes. Measurement problems. Conclusion.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The dependency ratio compares the number of people who are too young or too old to work with those of working age. In developing economies it is often high because high birth rates give a large number of children. Each worker must then support more dependants, so income per head is lower and less is available for saving and investment. The government also collects less tax from a small working population, so it may have less to spend on health and education, which could keep productivity and living standards low.

However, dependency is probably not the only reason. Many developing economies rely on primary products with volatile prices, have low levels of capital and weak access to education and training. Living standards are also hard to measure, since GDP per head ignores many factors. So a high dependency ratio is likely to be one important cause, but not clearly the main one.

Top tips

  • Define each measure. Birth rate and death rate are per 1,000 per year. Natural increase is the difference.
  • Know the dependency ratio formula. (Under 15 plus over 64) divided by (15 to 64), times 100.
  • Distinguish young and old dependency. Developing: mainly children. Developed: ageing, pensions and health costs.
  • Draw output per head against population. An inverted U with the optimum at the peak. Technology shifts it up and right.
  • Say that the optimum shifts. Capital, technology and productivity raise it. More births do not.
  • Give costs and benefits of urbanisation. Productivity and services against slums, congestion and pollution.
  • Judge, do not just describe. Population change is a problem only if output per head and structure are unfavourable.
