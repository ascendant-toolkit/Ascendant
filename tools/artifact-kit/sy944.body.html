Commercial Banks, Credit Creation and the Money Supply

9.4.4 to 9.4.5: the functions and objectives of commercial banks, the reserve and capital ratios, the credit multiplier, the central bank and the causes of changes in the money supply. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Commercial banks take deposits, make loans and provide payment services, acting as financial intermediaries between savers and borrowers. They must balance three objectives, liquidity, security and profitability, which conflict, so they hold assets of different kinds and keep a reserve ratio and a capital ratio. Because banks lend out part of every deposit, they create credit and so most of the money supply, with a bank credit multiplier of 1 ÷ reserve ratio. The money supply also changes because of the central bank, government deficit financing, quantitative easing and the balance of payments.

1 · Functions of commercial banks (9.4.4)

  • Deposit accounts: demand (current) accounts that can be withdrawn on request, and savings accounts that pay interest and may need notice.
  • Lending: overdrafts (short-term, flexible), loans and mortgages (longer term).
  • Financial intermediation: bringing savers and borrowers together, borrowing short and lending long (maturity transformation), and pooling and spreading risk.
  • Payment services: cheques, cards and transfers.
  • Credit creation: lending creates new deposits.
  • Holding assets: cash, securities, loans, deposits and equity, as shown in the bank's balance sheet.

Liabilities are what the bank owes: customer deposits (the largest part) and shareholders' funds (equity or capital).

Reserve ratio

reserve ratio = cash reserves ÷ deposits

The share of deposits kept in liquid form. Set by the bank for safety and sometimes by the central bank. A higher ratio makes the bank safer but cuts lending and profit.

Capital ratio

capital ratio = capital ÷ risk-weighted assets

How much of the bank's risky assets are funded by shareholders rather than depositors. A higher ratio helps the bank absorb losses; regulators set minimums.

The three objectives of a commercial bank

ObjectiveMeaningConflict
LiquidityAble to meet withdrawals on demandLiquid assets earn little: conflicts with profitability
SecurityLow risk of losing money lentSafe borrowers pay lower interest: conflicts with profitability
ProfitabilityEarning a return for shareholdersHigher profit comes from riskier, less liquid loans
The financial crisis linkBanks that took excessive risks, lent with too little security and held too little capital found that they could not meet withdrawals when asset values fell. Regulation of liquidity and capital ratios aims to prevent this.

2 · Credit creation and the bank credit multiplier

When a customer deposits $1,000 and the bank keeps 10% as reserves, it can lend $900. The borrower spends it, the seller deposits it in a bank, and that bank can lend 90% of it. The process continues, and total deposits expand.

credit multiplier = 1 ÷ reserve ratio   ·   maximum total deposits = initial deposit × multiplier
  • With a 10% reserve ratio the multiplier is 10, so a $1,000 deposit can support $10,000 of deposits (the original $1,000 plus $9,000 of new money).
  • If people keep some cash (a cash drain) the multiplier is smaller: 1 ÷ (reserve ratio + cash ratio).
  • If the reserve ratio rises from 5% to 10% the multiplier halves.
  • The actual amount depends on the demand for loans, banks' willingness to lend and confidence.

3 · The central bank

  • Issues notes and coins (the monopoly issuer of currency).
  • Banker to the government and banker to commercial banks: they hold reserve accounts there.
  • Lender of last resort to banks facing a shortage of liquidity.
  • Implements monetary policy: sets interest rates, controls the money supply, conducts quantitative easing.
  • Regulates banks and protects financial stability: liquidity and capital requirements, stress tests.
  • Unlike a commercial bank, it does not aim to maximise profit and does not take deposits from the general public.

4 · What changes the money supply in an open economy (9.4.5)

CauseHow the money supply changes
Commercial bank credit creationMore lending raises deposits and the money supply. Causes: a lower reserve ratio, more demand for loans, lower interest rates, more confidence.
The central bankLower interest rates, lower reserve requirements or buying securities in the open market raise the money supply; the opposite actions reduce it.
Government deficit financingBorrowing from commercial banks or the central bank (or printing money) raises the money supply. Borrowing from the non-bank public mainly transfers existing money, so there is little change.
Quantitative easingThe central bank buys assets (mostly government bonds) from the non-bank sector with newly created money. This raises bank deposits and reserves.
The balance of paymentsA surplus (exports and capital inflows) brings foreign currency that is exchanged for domestic currency, raising the money supply (unless the central bank sterilises it). A deficit has the opposite effect. The money flow between domestic and foreign residents is the total currency flow.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

The 2008 financial crisis and bank capital

Many banks held too little capital and too many illiquid assets. When mortgage losses hit, markets stopped lending to them, and governments and central banks had to rescue banks. Regulators raised capital and liquidity requirements afterwards.

Exam use: the conflict between profitability and security and liquidity, and the role of the central bank as lender of last resort.

Northern Rock, 2007

A British bank that funded mortgage lending from wholesale markets rather than deposits. When markets froze, depositors queued to withdraw their money, and the Bank of England had to lend and the government to guarantee deposits.

Exam use: a bank run and the lender of last resort in action. A good example of liquidity risk.

Quantitative easing in the 2010s

The Bank of England and other central banks created hundreds of billions in new money to buy government bonds from pension funds and banks. Bank reserves and deposits rose, but bank lending to firms rose far less than expected.

Exam use: a cause of change in the money supply (and the limits of its effect on lending).

Capital inflows and the money supply

When a country receives large foreign investment or has a big current account surplus, its central bank buys the foreign currency with domestic currency, increasing the money supply. Some central banks (such as China's) offset this by selling bonds.

Exam use: the balance of payments as a cause of money supply growth, and sterilisation.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/41 and 43 · Q6(b) 13 marks Discuss the role and importance of the commercial banks in a developed economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Key roles: safekeeping of funds, lending to consumers and firms, financial intermediation and making payments easy.
  • Link each to the economy: savings finance investment and long-run growth; loans to consumers raise AD, output and jobs; easier payments encourage specialisation and trade; credit creation provides most of the money supply.
  • Evaluation: the importance depends on stability (banking crises have large costs), regulation, competition and how much they lend to productive uses rather than asset purchases.
PlanFour roles. Link each to growth or stability. Then the risks of banking. Judge importance.
Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/41 · Q5(b) 13 marks 'The most important aim of a central bank is to control the money supply, while the most important aim of a commercial bank is to maximise profit.' Discuss the extent to which these two aims can be achieved together.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Central bank roles: monetary policy to help meet an inflation target and financial stability, using interest rates, the money supply and other instruments.
  • Commercial bank profit comes from lending, which creates money, so profit-seeking lending expands the money supply while the central bank may want to limit it. Conflict: a bank wants more loans, the central bank may want fewer.
  • Compatible: stable money and a healthy banking system support profit; regulation (reserve and capital ratios) steers banks without destroying profit; interest rate changes align incentives.
  • A conclusion on whether the two can be reconciled, and when they conflict (booms, financial risk-taking).
PlanDefine each aim. Where they conflict. Where they fit. Role of regulation. Conclude.
Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/42 · Q7(a) 12 marks Explain what is meant by quantitative easing (QE) and consider whether it is an effective policy to use in a recession.
What the mark scheme wants
  • QE: the central bank creates money electronically and uses it to buy assets, mainly long-term government bonds, from banks and other holders. This raises their price, lowers long-term interest rates, increases bank reserves and the money supply, and aims to raise lending, spending and asset prices.
  • Effectiveness: useful when interest rates are near zero and conventional policy has run out; can stabilise financial markets and boost confidence; wealth effects from higher asset prices.
  • Limits: banks may hold the reserves rather than lend; weak demand for loans; benefits asset owners (inequality); risk of inflation later; lags; may not reach the real economy.
PlanDefine QE and the mechanism. Why it is used. Three reasons it might fail. Judge.
Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/42 · Q7(b) 13 marks Discuss the impact on the economy of the introduction of quantitative easing (QE) by the central bank.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Impact on interest rates, bank reserves, lending, AD, asset prices, the exchange rate and inflation.
  • Positive: lower borrowing costs, higher asset prices raise wealth and spending, a lower exchange rate raises exports, avoids deflation.
  • Negative: little effect on lending in a recession, inequality from asset price rises, risk of asset bubbles and inflation, difficulty reversing it.
  • A reasoned conclusion on net impact.
PlanChain from QE to interest rates, lending and AD. Exchange rate effect. Costs. Judge.
Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/41 · Q6 25 marks 'The use of quantitative easing has the same effect on the economy as Keynesian fiscal demand management. Both policies create employment in the short run at the expense of inflation in the long run.' How far would you support this view?
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explain QE (monetary) and Keynesian fiscal policy (government spending or lower taxes): both raise AD.
  • Similarities: both can raise output and jobs in a recession and risk inflation if overdone; both can raise the national debt or central bank balance sheet.
  • Differences: fiscal policy works directly through spending and the multiplier; QE works indirectly through interest rates and asset prices and may not raise lending; fiscal policy affects the budget; effects on distribution differ.
  • Evaluation of 'inflation in the long run': depends on spare capacity, velocity and expectations; QE after 2008 did not cause high inflation for a decade.
PlanExplain both. Compare effects and transmission. Test the claim about inflation. Judge.
Practice (not a past paper question) 8 marks A bank receives a new deposit of $2,000 and its reserve ratio is 10%. Explain how banks can use this deposit to expand the money supply, and calculate the maximum total increase in deposits.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Calculation: multiplier 1 ÷ 0.1 = 10; maximum total deposits = 2,000 × 10 = $20,000, of which $18,000 is new money.
  • Process: the bank keeps $200 and lends $1,800; the borrower spends it and the recipient deposits it in a bank that lends 90% of it, and so on.
  • Limits: cash drain, banks holding more reserves than required, weak demand for loans, central bank controls.
PlanNumber first. Then the rounds. Then limits.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Credit creation happens because banks need to keep only part of deposits as reserves and can lend the rest. The credit multiplier is 1 divided by the reserve ratio, which is 1 ÷ 0.1 = 10. A $2,000 deposit could therefore support up to $20,000 of deposits, so $18,000 of new money could be created.

In the first round the bank keeps $200 and lends $1,800. The borrower spends this and the recipient deposits it in a bank, which keeps $180 and lends $1,620, and the process continues with each round smaller than the last, until the total reaches the maximum. In practice the increase is smaller if people keep some of the loans as cash, if banks hold more than the required reserves, or if there is little demand for loans, and the central bank can restrict the process by raising the reserve requirement or interest rates.

Top tips

  • Learn the three bank objectives and the conflict. Liquidity, security and profitability. Liquid and safe assets earn less.
  • Draw the balance sheet logic. Cash is most liquid and least profitable. Loans are the opposite.
  • Credit multiplier = 1 ÷ reserve ratio. Show the working. Allow for cash drain if told.
  • Distinguish a central bank from a commercial bank. Central bank: lender of last resort, issues notes, implements policy, no profit motive.
  • List causes of money supply change. Credit creation, central bank, deficit financing, QE, the balance of payments.
  • For QE, say who is buying what. The central bank creates money and buys bonds, which lowers long-term rates and raises reserves.
  • Evaluate QE with lending and inequality. Banks may not lend, and asset holders gain most.
