Effects of Monetary Policy on the Aims

How monetary policy measures could help a government meet its macroeconomic aims, the conflicts between them and the limits of monetary policy. Syllabus 4.3.3.

The big idea

Monetary policy can help a government meet its macroeconomic aims, but each measure helps some aims and may harm others. Expansionary policy (lower rates, more money, a lower exchange rate) tends to help growth and jobs but risks inflation and a worse current account. Contractionary policy tends to help prices but risks growth and jobs. Good answers name the aim, follow the chain, then evaluate.

Evaluating monetary policy

  • Time lags. A rate change can take a year or more to work through mortgages, loans and spending decisions, though the central bank can change rates quickly.
  • Confidence. If households and firms are worried about the future, they may not borrow even at very low rates. Cutting rates can be like pushing on a string.
  • Banks may not pass changes on. Banks may keep lending tight after a cut, or keep rates high to protect their profits.
  • Limits. Rates cannot fall much below zero, and money supply increases can be undone by banks refusing to lend.
  • Conflicts between aims. Cutting rates to cut unemployment could raise inflation and worsen the current account; raising rates to cut inflation could raise unemployment.
  • Fixed exchange rates. A country that pegs its currency (like Jordan to the US dollar) has to follow the other country's interest rates, so it loses control of its own monetary policy.
  • Winners and losers. Borrowers and exporters gain from loose policy; savers and importers gain from tight policy.
  • Compared with fiscal policy. Interest rates can be changed fast and are often set independently of politics, but they affect everyone in the economy, not a chosen group or region, and cannot directly build roads or schools.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Central banks fighting inflation, 2022–23

After prices rose quickly in many countries, central banks such as the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and the Bank of England raised interest rates in a series of steps. Mortgage payments rose and borrowing slowed.

Exam use: contractionary monetary policy against demand-pull and cost-push inflation. Evaluate: time lags, higher unemployment risk, and pain for borrowers.

Rates near zero after 2008 and COVID-19

Central banks cut rates to very low levels after the 2008 financial crisis and again during the pandemic, and several bought large amounts of government bonds (quantitative easing) to increase the money supply.

Exam use: expansionary monetary policy in action. Evaluate: effects depend on confidence and on whether banks lend, and savers earn less.

Japan and low demand

Japan kept interest rates at or near zero for many years while prices fell or barely rose. Low rates alone did not lift spending as much as hoped when households and firms preferred to save.

Exam use: a limit of monetary policy. Rates cannot fall much below zero, and low confidence can stop borrowing.

The Swiss franc surge, 2015

Switzerland's central bank had held the franc down against the euro. When it stopped doing so, the franc rose sharply. Swiss exporters and tourism firms found their prices suddenly higher abroad.

Exam use: a higher exchange rate making exports less competitive. Link to jobs in export industries.

A weaker currency and imported inflation

When a currency falls sharply, as the Turkish lira did in several years of the late 2010s and early 2020s, imports such as fuel and machinery cost more in the home currency. Firms and households may see their costs rise.

Exam use: the cost of a lower exchange rate. Evaluate any depreciation argument with imported inflation.

Housing markets and rate changes

In countries where most home loans have variable rates, a change in the central bank rate changes household mortgage payments quickly. Where loans are fixed for several years the effect is slower. This was noted in a recent past paper stimulus.

Exam use: why the effect of monetary policy depends on the type of borrowing and on how the housing market is working.

Q1 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a cut in interest rates could create conflicts between macroeconomic aims. (Oct/Nov 2022, P21)
ReminderName two pairs of aims (one pair caps you at 4). For each pair: cut in rates, effect on aim 1, then the problem for aim 2.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

A cut in interest rates could create a conflict between full employment and stable prices. Cheaper borrowing and less incentive to save are likely to increase spending, which raises AD, so firms could take on more workers and unemployment may fall. However, higher demand could outstrip supply, causing demand-pull inflation.

A second conflict could be between economic growth and the balance of payments. Higher incomes from growth may lead people to buy more imports, so the current account deficit could widen (or the surplus could fall).

Q2 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a rise in interest rates could help to reduce inflation.
ReminderDraw AD shifting left. Three parts to the chain: households, firms, then the exchange rate. Say which kind of inflation it addresses (demand-pull).
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

A rise in the central bank's interest rate usually leads commercial banks to raise their own rates. Borrowing becomes more expensive and saving more rewarding, so households are likely to cut spending, and mortgage holders have less income left to spend. Firms may delay investment because fewer projects earn more than the cost of the loan.

Lower consumption and investment shift AD to the left, which reduces demand-pull pressure on prices. Higher rates may also attract foreign savers, raising the exchange rate, which makes imported goods and raw materials cheaper and could lower cost-push inflation too.

Q3 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not an increase in the money supply will benefit an economy. (Feb/Mar 2025, P22)
Reminder"Whether or not": a case for, a case against, then a judgement on what it depends on. Define money supply first. Use "could" and "may".
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

The money supply is the total amount of money in an economy, and a central bank can increase it, for example by buying government bonds.

An increase could benefit an economy because banks have more to lend, which tends to push interest rates down. Cheaper borrowing may raise consumer spending and encourage firms to invest, so AD could shift to the right. Output, employment and economic growth could therefore rise, and firms may find it easier to finance expansion. This is most likely when the economy has spare capacity and unemployment is high.

On the other hand, if the economy is already close to full capacity, extra spending could simply raise the price level, causing demand-pull inflation and reducing the value of money. Higher incomes may also raise imports, which could worsen the current account, and easy credit might leave some households and firms in debt. The effect could also be weak if banks are unwilling to lend, or if firms and households have low confidence.

Overall, a modest increase when there is spare capacity and low inflation is more likely to benefit the economy, whereas a large increase when inflation is already high could do more harm than good. The outcome depends on the size of the increase, the state of the economy and whether banks pass the money on as loans.

Q4 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not a country will benefit from having a fixed foreign exchange rate system. (Oct/Nov 2022, P21)
ReminderDefine fixed rate in a clause. For: certainty, less speculation, discipline on policy. Against: reserves cost, no independent monetary policy, rate may be wrong. Finish with "it depends on…".
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

In a fixed exchange rate system the central bank holds the currency at a set value against another currency, using its reserves or interest rates.

A fixed rate could benefit a country by giving traders certainty about what they will pay or receive, which makes trade and investment less risky. An exporter selling to a country the currency is pegged to knows its future income in advance. It may also limit speculation and force governments to follow sensible policies to keep the rate, which could keep inflation low.

However, defending the rate can use up the foreign currency reserves, and if people expect a devaluation, speculation could make it harder. The central bank may have to set interest rates to protect the exchange rate rather than to suit the domestic economy, so the country loses control of its monetary policy, as when Jordan follows US rates. If the rate is fixed too high, exports may be uncompetitive and unemployment could rise.

Overall, a fixed rate is more likely to benefit a small country that trades mostly with one partner and has limited credibility on its own. A large, diverse economy may prefer a floating rate so that monetary policy can respond to domestic problems.

Top tips

  • Name the aim, then follow the chain. Growth, employment, prices, balance of payments.
  • Always give a cost as well as a gain. Expansionary: inflation, imports. Contractionary: jobs, growth.
  • Conflict questions: name two pairs of aims. For example employment and inflation, growth and the current account.
  • Evaluate with lags, confidence, elasticity and the state of the economy. Spare capacity or near full capacity matters most.
  • Fixed exchange rate: certainty and discipline, but no independent monetary policy.
