Definition of Fiscal Policy

What fiscal policy is, how it works through the components of aggregate demand, and how it differs from monetary and supply-side policy. Syllabus 4.2.4.

The big idea

Fiscal policy is the government's use of spending and taxation to influence aggregate demand and so the economy's performance against its macroeconomic aims. It is decided by the government, usually in a yearly budget, and it works by changing the parts of AD: government spending (G) directly, and consumption (C) and investment (I) through taxes.

What it is made of

AD = C + I + G + (X − M)

Government spending (G)

A direct part of AD. A change in G changes AD at once.

Taxation

Changes how much households and firms have left to spend. Lower income tax raises disposable income and could raise C; lower corporation tax could raise I.

Expansionary, contractionary or neutral

Expansionary

Higher government spending and/or lower taxes, designed to increase AD. Used when there is spare capacity, unemployment or a recession.

Contractionary

Lower government spending and/or higher taxes, designed to reduce AD. Used when the economy is overheating or inflation is too high.

A policy is neutral if the changes in spending and taxes leave AD roughly unchanged.

Fiscal policy compared with other policies

PolicyWho decidesTools
FiscalGovernmentGovernment spending and taxes
MonetaryCentral bank (often independent)Interest rates, money supply, exchange rate
Supply-sideGovernmentEducation, infrastructure, deregulation, labour market reforms: raise AS

Discretionary and automatic

Discretionary fiscal policy is a deliberate decision to change spending or taxes. Automatic stabilisers change the budget without any new decision when the economy moves into a boom or slump.

Stimulus after a crisis

After the 2008 financial crisis and during the COVID-19 pandemic, many governments raised spending (support payments, wage subsidies, infrastructure) and some cut taxes temporarily such as lowering VAT rates. Deficits and national debt rose sharply as a result.

Exam use: expansionary fiscal policy in action. Cover AD, growth and jobs, then the cost: bigger deficit, higher debt and interest payments.

Austerity and the cost of tightening

Some countries with large deficits raised taxes and cut spending to reduce borrowing. Public finances improved, but critics argued the cuts slowed growth and raised unemployment, which cut tax revenue.

Exam use: contractionary fiscal policy, and a standard evaluation point: reducing the deficit may take longer or cost more output than hoped.

Recessions and automatic stabilisers

When the economy slumps, firms lay off workers. Income tax revenue falls and spending on unemployment benefit rises automatically, so the deficit widens without any new policy and support for spending is maintained.

Exam use: a clean example of automatic stabilisers. Explain the mechanism, then evaluate: it limits the damage but does not remove the recession.

Q1 Define 2 marks What is meant by fiscal policy?
ReminderTwo ideas: changes in government spending and taxation, to influence AD (or the economy).
Fiscal policy is the use of changes in government spending and taxation to influence aggregate demand and achieve macroeconomic aims.
Common mistakeMixing it up with monetary policy. Interest rates are not fiscal.
Q2 Identify 2 marks Identify two fiscal policy measures.
ReminderShort phrases. One about tax, one about spending is safest.
A change in taxation, for example raising income tax. A change in government spending, for example on roads.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain the difference between expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy.
ReminderUse 'whereas'. Say what changes, and what happens to AD, in each.
Expansionary fiscal policy is higher government spending and/or lower taxes designed to raise aggregate demand and increase output and employment, whereas contractionary fiscal policy is lower government spending and/or higher taxes designed to reduce aggregate demand, which could reduce inflationary pressure.
Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain one way in which fiscal policy differs from monetary policy.
ReminderWho decides, or what the tools are. Develop it with a second point.
Fiscal policy uses government spending and taxation, whereas monetary policy uses interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate. Fiscal decisions are made by the government, for example in a budget, but interest rate decisions are often made by an independent central bank. This could mean fiscal policy targets particular groups or projects, while a change in interest rates affects the whole economy.

Top tips

  • Fiscal = government spending and taxes. Never interest rates.
  • Expansionary raises AD; contractionary reduces it. Say which and why.
  • Link to AD = C + I + G + (X − M). G changes directly; taxes change C and I.
  • Know who decides. Government for fiscal; central bank for monetary.
