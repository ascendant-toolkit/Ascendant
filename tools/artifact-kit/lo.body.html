Why Less Output Is Not Good for Allocative Efficiency

A data table, unit by unit, showing what society loses when a market produces fewer units than the output where marginal benefit equals marginal cost. Animated diagram, lab, drills, real markets and exam practice.

The whole idea in four sentences

Allocative efficiency is reached at the output where the marginal benefit (MB, the price consumers are willing to pay for the last unit) equals the marginal cost (MC) of producing it. Below that output, every extra unit is worth more to consumers than it costs to make, so society is giving up a net gain on each unit that is not produced. Those forgone gains add up to the welfare loss (deadweight loss). The further output falls below the efficient level, the larger the loss is likely to be.

1 · The data: one unit at a time

Take a market for a good, measured in thousands of units. Consumers' marginal benefit falls as they get more, and the marginal cost of production rises. Net benefit is MB − MC: the gain to society from making that unit.

Total surplus is the running total of net benefit. It stops rising at the 6th unit, where MB = MC = $50, and falls if output goes beyond it.

2 · Reading the table

Units 1 to 5: produce them

MB is above MC, so net benefit is positive. Making each of these units adds to total surplus. A market that stops before them is wasting a gain.

Unit 6: the efficient point

MB = MC = $50, so net benefit is $0. Total surplus is at its maximum of $225. This is the allocatively efficient output.

Units 7 to 10: too much

MC is above MB, so net benefit is negative. Resources could be used more valuably elsewhere. This is overproduction, which is the opposite error.

3 · What happens when output is cut

Suppose output is held at some level below 6 units. The welfare loss is the total of net benefit on the units that are not made.

Welfare loss = total surplus at the efficient output − total surplus at the actual output
Why this mattersA firm with market power can raise profit by restricting output, because a smaller quantity sells at a higher price. That is good for the firm but it is not good for allocative efficiency: the units it leaves out were worth more to buyers than they cost to make. Consumers lose surplus, and the firm's extra profit does not make up for all of it, so total surplus falls.

Each bar is one unit. The green strip is the net benefit of a unit that is produced. The red strip is the net benefit lost on a unit that could have been produced and was not.

Flashcards

Examples are explained in general terms to show how the model is applied. Check current details before quoting a specific figure or date in an exam answer.

OPEC+ output cuts

Oil-producing countries in OPEC+ have agreed cuts to production quotas at several points to support the oil price. A smaller quantity sells at a higher price, which helps producers' revenues. Consumers and oil-using firms pay more, and barrels that were worth more to buyers than they cost to extract are not supplied.

Use in an answer: the cut raises price above marginal cost, so the market is likely to move away from P = MC and allocative efficiency falls. Evaluate: the cut may also help conserve a finite resource.

Diamonds and De Beers

For much of the 20th century De Beers controlled most of the world's rough diamond supply and managed how many stones reached the market. Limiting the flow kept prices high. The units held back could have been valued by buyers at well above their cost of extraction.

Use in an answer: a dominant seller restricts output to raise price, creating a welfare loss. Evaluate: its market share has since fallen, and new producers and lab-grown stones have increased competition.

A patented medicine

In 2015 the price of an old drug, Daraprim, was raised from about $13.50 to $750 a tablet in the US after one company gained control of its supply. The cost of making another tablet stayed tiny. Some patients and hospitals were likely to use less of it than they would at a price near marginal cost.

Use in an answer: price far above MC means output is below the allocatively efficient level for a merit good. Evaluate: patents reward research, so a profit above MC may pay for dynamic efficiency.

Q1 Use data 4 marks Table 1 shows the marginal benefit and marginal cost of each unit of a good. Using the table, explain why a market producing only 3 units is not allocatively efficient.
ReminderQuote figures from the table. Find the output where MB = MC, then compare it with 3. State the welfare loss with its calculation.

Allocative efficiency is reached where marginal benefit equals marginal cost, which in Table 1 is at 6 units, where both are $50. At the 4th unit MB is $70 and MC is $40, and at the 5th unit MB is $60 and MC is $45. Each unit is worth more to consumers than it costs to make, so society gains $30 and $15 from those units, and these gains are lost if output stays at 3. The welfare loss is therefore $45 (30 + 15 + 0), and total surplus is $180 instead of the maximum $225.

Common mistakeSaying the market is inefficient "because output is lower". The reason is that MB is greater than MC on the missing units. Another: forgetting that the 6th unit adds $0.
Q2 Analyse 8 marks A single firm supplies most of the market for a medicine and restricts the number of doses it sells. Analyse why this could reduce allocative efficiency.
ReminderLink profit-maximising behaviour to the position of MB and MC. Use a diagram with the welfare loss triangle, then state who loses.

A firm with market power faces a downward-sloping demand curve, so selling more means lowering the price on all units. It is therefore likely to maximise profit where MR = MC, at an output below the point where MB (demand) equals MC. The doses it does not sell are ones that patients value more than the cost of producing them, so treatment that could be provided at a worthwhile cost is not.

At the restricted output the price is above marginal cost, so the gain from the missing units, the area between MB and MC, is lost as a welfare loss. Part of consumer surplus becomes extra profit for the firm, but the rest disappears, so total surplus falls.

Q3 Evaluate 12 marks Evaluate whether a reduction in output below the market equilibrium is always bad for society.
ReminderStart with the efficiency case, then give two or three reasons it may not be bad, then judge. Hedge: output cuts "could" or "may" be justified.

Where MB exceeds MC, producing less than the equilibrium output means units that add more to benefit than cost are lost, giving a welfare loss. The loss is likely to be larger the further output falls below the efficient level and the bigger the gap between MB and MC.

If there are negative externalities, the market equilibrium is already above the socially efficient output, so a cut towards MSB = MSC could improve allocative efficiency. If the resource is finite, such as oil, lower output now may help future generations, which could outweigh the loss today. A firm that earns monopoly profit from restricting output may reinvest in research, so dynamic efficiency might offset some of the static loss.

Overall, a cut below equilibrium is likely to harm allocative efficiency when it is driven by market power and there are no external costs, but it may be justified where the market output is above the social optimum.

Top tips

  • Name the comparison. Efficiency is judged by MB against MC on the units that are missing, not by the size of output on its own.
  • Use the numbers. In data questions quote MB and MC for the specific units, then subtract. Sum the net benefits for the welfare loss.
  • The unit where MB = MC adds nothing. Do not count it as a gain or a loss.
  • Say "forgone", not "lost profit". The welfare loss is a loss of total surplus to society, not just to the firm.
  • Both directions are inefficient. Output above the efficient level is also a welfare loss. Mention it for balance.
  • Hedge. A cut "could" or "is likely to" reduce efficiency. Add that externalities or finite resources may change the verdict.
  • Link to market structure. Monopoly and cartels restrict output; perfect competition at P = MC does not.

Key words

TermMeaning
Marginal benefit (MB)The extra benefit, measured by willingness to pay, from consuming one more unit
Marginal cost (MC)The extra cost of producing one more unit
Net benefitMB minus MC for a unit
Total surplusConsumer surplus plus producer surplus; the running total of net benefit
Welfare lossThe fall in total surplus when output is not at the efficient level
Allocative efficiencyThe output at which MB = MC (P = MC with no externalities)
