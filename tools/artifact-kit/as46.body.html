Economic Growth: Meaning and Measurement

4.4.1 to 4.4.3: the meaning and measurement of economic growth, and the difference between growth in nominal and real GDP. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Economic growth is an increase in the real value of the goods and services an economy produces over time, usually measured as the percentage change in real GDP from one period to the next (actual growth), while an outward shift of the production possibility curve or LRAS is growth in potential output. Nominal GDP is measured at current prices, so it rises with both output and inflation; real GDP is adjusted for inflation using a price index such as the GDP deflator, so it shows only the change in output. Negative economic growth is a fall in real GDP, and a recession is usually defined as two or more consecutive quarters of falling real GDP. GDP per head, adjusted for prices, is used to compare living standards, though it has limits.

1 · Meaning and measurement (4.4.1 to 4.4.2)

  • Growth rate = (real GDP this year − real GDP last year) ÷ real GDP last year × 100.
  • Actual growth: a rise in real output as spare capacity is used (a movement towards the PPC or AD rising); potential growth: an increase in productive capacity (the PPC or LRAS shifting outwards).
  • Negative growth is a fall in real GDP; a recession is two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP.
  • Per capita: real GDP ÷ population shows the average output per person, a better guide to living standards. GDP can grow while GDP per head falls if the population grows faster.
  • Limits of GDP as a measure: it ignores the informal economy, unpaid work, income distribution, environmental damage and leisure; comparisons across countries depend on exchange rates and different price levels.

2 · Nominal and real GDP (4.4.3)

Nominal (money) GDPReal GDP
Prices usedCurrent pricesConstant (base-year) prices
Changes withOutput and pricesOutput only
CalculationValue of output at today's pricesNominal GDP ÷ price index × 100

  • Example: nominal GDP rises from $500bn to $577.5bn while prices rise 5%. Real GDP = 577.5 ÷ 1.05 = $550bn, so real growth is 10%.
  • Approximately: real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation.
  • If nominal GDP rises by less than the rate of inflation, real GDP falls.

4 · Actual and potential growth

Actual growthPotential growth
MeaningIncrease in real GDP: output actually producedIncrease in the economy's productive capacity
DiagramA move towards (or along) the PPC; AD shifts right along SRASOutward shift of the PPC; LRAS shifts right
CausesHigher AD using spare capacityMore and better factors of production, technology
LimitCannot go beyond potential for long without inflationSlow and costly to achieve
  • The output gap is the difference between actual and potential output. A negative gap means spare capacity; a positive gap means output above sustainable levels.

5 · The business cycle

  • Boom: fast growth, falling unemployment, rising inflation and demand for imports.
  • Slowdown: growth continues but more slowly.
  • Recession: two or more consecutive quarters of falling real GDP; unemployment rises and inflation falls.
  • Recovery: real GDP begins to rise again, initially using spare capacity.
  • Real GDP tends to follow a trend of long-run growth with cyclical fluctuations around it.

6 · Using GDP to compare living standards: limits

  • Population: use per capita figures.
  • Prices: use real figures; compare countries using purchasing power adjustments, since exchange rates do not reflect local prices.
  • Distribution: an average hides inequality.
  • Unrecorded output: informal activity and unpaid work are missed.
  • Quality of life: health, leisure, education and the environment are not captured (indicators such as the Human Development Index try to include some).
  • Composition: output of weapons or the cost of repairing disasters counts as GDP but does not raise welfare.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Prices up, output flat

A country's nominal GDP rises 6% in a year, but prices also rise 6%. Real GDP is unchanged: the economy produced the same amount, and the extra money value only reflects higher prices.

Exam use: nominal versus real GDP.

A recession

Real GDP falls for two successive quarters as spending drops. Firms cut output and jobs, and unemployment rises. Economists call this a recession, a period of negative growth.

Exam use: negative growth and its definition.

Fast GDP growth, fast population growth

An economy grows 3% a year, but its population grows 3% as well. GDP per head does not rise, so average living standards are unchanged even though total output is larger.

Exam use: why per capita figures matter.

The informal economy

In many developing countries much work is done for cash or at home and never recorded. Official GDP therefore understates output, which makes growth hard to measure accurately.

Exam use: limits of GDP as a measure.

Nominal growth that is only inflation

A country reports a 15% rise in nominal GDP, but prices rose 14%. Real GDP grew by about 1%, so output hardly changed.

Exam use: real versus nominal GDP.

A recession

During a recession real GDP falls for two or more quarters, firms cut jobs and investment, and unemployment rises. Governments often respond with expansionary policy.

Exam use: negative growth and its consequences.

Top tips

  • Growth means real. Always use real GDP unless told otherwise.
  • Show the formula. (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
  • Deflate first. Real GDP = nominal ÷ price index × 100.
  • Real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation. Use as a check.
  • Recession = two quarters of falling real GDP. Negative growth.
  • Use per capita for living standards. Divide by population.
  • Know the limits. Informal economy, distribution, environment.
