Income Distribution: Gini Coefficient and Lorenz Curve

11.4.2: drawing and reading a Lorenz curve, calculating the Gini coefficient, poverty, and the policies that change the distribution of income. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

A Lorenz curve plots the cumulative percentage of income against the cumulative percentage of households (ranked from poorest to richest); the further it bows below the 45° line of perfect equality, the more unequal the distribution. The Gini coefficient measures this as the area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve divided by the whole area under the line of equality, giving a value from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality). Developing countries often have high inequality, which affects poverty and growth, and taxes, benefits and public services shift the curve. Absolute poverty is living below a fixed minimum income, whereas relative poverty is having much less than the typical household in the same society.

1 · Drawing the Lorenz curve

  1. Rank households from the poorest to the richest, usually in five groups (quintiles) of 20% each.
  2. Work out the cumulative share of income each group has received (for example the poorest 20% have 5%, the poorest 40% have 15%).
  3. Plot cumulative % of households on the horizontal axis and cumulative % of income on the vertical axis, starting at (0,0) and ending at (100,100).
  4. The line of perfect equality is the 45° diagonal (the poorest 20% have 20% of income). The Lorenz curve lies below it.

2 · The Gini coefficient

  • Gini = A ÷ (A + B), where A is the area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve and B is the area under the Lorenz curve. Since A + B is half the square (0.5), Gini = 2 × A = 1 − 2 × B.
  • Calculation from quintile data: find the area under the Lorenz curve (B) by adding trapezoids. For the table above: 0.2 × (0 + 0.05) ÷ 2 = 0.005; 0.2 × (0.05 + 0.15) ÷ 2 = 0.02; 0.045; 0.085; 0.155. B = 0.31, so Gini = 1 − 2 × 0.31 = 0.38.
  • Interpretation: a lower Gini means a more equal distribution; 0 means everyone has the same income, and 1 means one person has everything. It is sometimes given as a percentage (0 to 100). Values for countries range roughly from the 0.25s (very equal) to above 0.6 (very unequal).
  • Limits: different Lorenz curves can give the same Gini; it measures income before or after tax depending on the data used, and says nothing about wealth unless the wealth is measured; it is affected by the informal economy and by data quality; it does not show how many people are poor in absolute terms.

3 · Policies and the shift of the Lorenz curve

  • Towards equality (curve moves towards the 45° line, Gini falls): progressive income tax, higher benefits and transfers, a minimum wage, free education and health care (supply-side, raises the earnings of the poor), land reform and support for small farmers, stronger trade unions.
  • Away from equality (Gini rises): cuts in top tax rates and benefits, more regressive indirect taxes, skill-biased technology and globalisation that rewards the highly skilled, and inheritance of wealth.
  • Trade-off: high marginal tax rates and benefits may reduce incentives to work and invest (efficiency), so governments balance equity and efficiency.

4 · Poverty

Absolute povertyRelative poverty
MeaningNot having the income to meet basic needs such as food, water and shelter; below a fixed line such as the World Bank's international poverty line of a few dollars a dayIncome well below the typical income in the same society (often below 50% to 60% of the median)
Where it is most commonLow-income countriesAll countries, including rich ones
Falls whenEconomic growth raises the incomes of the poorIncome distribution becomes more equal
  • Economic growth can reduce absolute poverty while relative poverty and inequality rise, if the rich gain faster.
  • Causes of high inequality in low-income countries include unequal land and capital ownership, differences in education and health, a dual economy (a small modern sector and a large low-income informal sector), corruption and weak tax systems.
  • Inequality can affect growth in both directions: it may raise saving by the rich and give incentives, but it can also limit education and health for the poor, create social tension and reduce demand.
Evaluation pointA fall in the Gini coefficient is good if it comes from raising the incomes of the poor, but not if it comes from reducing everyone's income. Always say whether absolute poverty has also fallen, and note that a single number hides the shape of the distribution.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Latin America and inequality

Several Latin American countries have had Gini coefficients above 0.5 for decades. Cash transfer programmes to poor families and higher minimum wages in some countries in the 2000s lowered inequality, showing how policy can move the Lorenz curve.

Exam use: fiscal and supply-side policies for equality, and the Gini coefficient as evidence.

Nordic countries

Countries such as Denmark and Norway have Gini coefficients in the low 0.2s to 0.3s after taxes and transfers. High progressive taxes, generous benefits and strong public education are the main reasons, but tax rates are high and these countries also have strong productivity.

Exam use: a low Gini achieved through taxation and public spending, and the equity versus efficiency debate.

Fast growth with rising inequality

In several rapidly growing Asian economies, absolute poverty fell sharply while inequality rose because workers with skills and owners of capital gained fastest. Poverty fell, yet the Gini coefficient and the share of the richest rose.

Exam use: growth reducing absolute poverty but not relative poverty.

Wealth and income

Wealth (assets such as property and shares) is usually distributed much more unequally than income. A country's income Gini can look moderate while its wealth Gini is far higher, so the distribution of wealth also matters for equity.

Exam use: the distinction between income and wealth distribution.

What the mark scheme wants

  • Higher Gini coefficients are also found where a smaller percentage is below the poverty line, so there is no link to absolute poverty.
  • A cause and effect cannot be determined; the table says nothing about relative poverty.

Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The table does not support the conclusion. The country with the highest Gini coefficient (A, 0.52) has the best literacy ratio (1.00) and the lowest share of people in poverty (5%), whereas the country with the lowest Gini (E, 0.29) has the lowest literacy ratio (0.70) and 31% in poverty. The pattern is the opposite of the claim, since higher inequality goes with better literacy and less absolute poverty.

In any case, five countries are too few to prove a cause, and a link between two variables does not show that one causes the other. The table says nothing about relative poverty, and other factors such as the level of income, education spending and policy could explain the differences.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/41 · Q1(d) 8 marks Use one example of a supply-side policy and one example of a fiscal policy to assess how a government might achieve a more equal distribution of income.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Fiscal policy: a correct example (1), developed (2) and a link to equality (1), for example progressive income tax and higher transfers to the poor.
  • Supply-side policy: a correct example (1), developed (2) and a link to equality (1), for example investment in education and training.

PlanFiscal: progressive tax and transfers, so poorer households gain and richer households lose. Supply-side: education, so low-paid workers gain skills and earn more over time.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A fiscal policy is a more progressive income tax with higher benefits for low-income households. Taking a larger share of the incomes of the rich and transferring it to the poor could reduce the Gini coefficient and move the Lorenz curve towards the line of equality. It works quickly, but high marginal tax rates could reduce incentives to work.

A supply-side policy is greater spending on education and training. This may raise the productivity and wages of low-paid workers, so their incomes rise relative to those of the highly skilled, which could make distribution more equal in the long run. The benefit may take many years and depends on jobs being available.

Top tips

  • Label the Lorenz axes. Cumulative % of households along the bottom, cumulative % of income up the side, and the 45° line of equality.
  • Gini equals A over A plus B. Area between the curves divided by the whole triangle. A bigger curve bow means a higher Gini.
  • Quote the range. Zero is perfect equality, one is perfect inequality.
  • Do the trapezoids. In a calculation find the area under the curve, then Gini = 1 − 2 × that area.
  • Pair absolute with relative poverty. Fixed line against comparison with others.
  • Name policy and mechanism. Progressive tax: takes more from high earners. Education: raises earnings of the poor over time.
  • Mention the trade-off. Equity against efficiency, and the time lags.
