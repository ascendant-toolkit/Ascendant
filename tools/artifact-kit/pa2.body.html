AO2 Analysis: Building Chains of Reasoning in Paper 4

What separates a statement, a one-step comment and a developed chain, how Table A rewards analysis, using diagrams and data, and short Section A parts. Animations, labs and practice.

This part in five sentences

AO2 is analysis: examining economic issues and relationships using relevant concepts, theories and information; selecting, interpreting and organising economic information in written, numerical and diagrammatic form; using information to recognise patterns, relationships, causes and effects; and explaining the impacts and consequences of changes in economic variables. It is 40% of the A Level, 40% of Paper 3 and 37% of Paper 4. In a Paper 4 essay it is marked with AO1 on the 14-mark Table A scale, and Level 3 asks for developed, detailed analysis using accurate concepts and diagrams that are fully explained, in a logical, well-organised answer. In practice analysis is a chain: each step is followed by "because" or "so", and the chain ends at the objective the question names. This page shows what separates a statement from a one-step comment from a developed chain.

2 · The three levels of Table A for analysis

In our words, from the pattern of the Paper 4 mark scheme. Markers award a best-fit level for the whole answer.

Level (of the 14 marks)The analysis looks like
Level 3 (11 to 14)Developed and detailed: each chain runs from the trigger to the objective, uses a correct diagram that is explained, is applied to the context, and the answer is well organised.
Level 2 (6 to 10)Some development, usually one good chain and others thin; a diagram that is drawn but lightly used; limited application. Level 2 tops out at 10.
Level 1 (1 to 5)Statements and lists, with little or no explanation of why, or analysis of the wrong topic.
The step from Level 2 to Level 3Usually it needs a third developed, applied chain, or one that is deepened from one step to the whole route to the objective. Markers do not award a mark per paragraph, but the pattern is steady.

3 · A statement, a step and a chain

Statement: "Higher interest rates reduce investment."
One step: "Higher interest rates make borrowing more expensive, so investment falls."
Chain: "A higher interest rate raises the cost of borrowing, so fewer projects have an expected return above that cost and firms cut investment. Investment is a component of AD, so AD shifts left, and real GDP and employment are likely to fall; in a country with high business borrowing at variable rates the fall could be larger."

4 · How to build a chain

trigger → mechanism → effect on the market or economy → consequence for the objective in the question
  • Trigger: the policy or change named in the question.
  • Mechanism: why it matters, using the right concept (cost, incentive, expected return, relative price).
  • Effect: what moves on the diagram, and in which direction, with figures if you have them.
  • Consequence: the objective the question asks about (growth, efficiency, equity, inflation, the current account). End the paragraph by naming it.

Link words do the work: because, so, which means, leading to, as a result, since. If a paragraph has none of these, it is probably a list.

5 · Use the diagram as part of the analysis

  • Draw the model the question names. A required diagram that is missing caps the 14-mark scale at Level 2.
  • Refer to it in the writing: "the shift from AD1 to AD2 raises the price level from P1 to P2".
  • Explain each shift, not just draw it. A diagram with no explanation, or an explanation with no diagram, loses marks.
  • Stay inside the model: a micro question answered with macro effects, or an indifference curve question answered with marginal utility, loses the marks even when the reasoning is sound.

7 · Analysis in short Section A parts

  • 2 to 4 marks: usually one step per mark, plus a diagram mark if the stem asks for one. Name the step, then its consequence.
  • "Analyse": break the effect into steps, with a diagram where it helps, and link to the objective named.
  • "Explain how": two or three linked steps are enough. Do not write an essay.
  • Last part (7 or 8 marks): develop two points rather than listing four, then use the passage.

8 · Common ways to lose AO2 marks

  • Stopping after one step: "investment falls" and nothing about AD, output or employment.
  • Listing: five brief points instead of three developed chains.
  • Analysing the topic, not the question: discussing GDP when the question said productivity.
  • Wrong model or wrong policy: fiscal policy in a monetary policy question, or a competitive diagram for a monopsony.
  • Ignoring the context: treating cost-push inflation as demand-pull, or leaving out the low-income country element.
  • A diagram drawn and never used.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Answering the topic, not the question

Reports describe efficiency never developed, policies described with no link to allocative efficiency, and GDP discussed when the question said productivity.

Exam use: underlining the key phrases and ending every paragraph by using them to answer the question.

Analysis stops after one step

A common comment is that analysis is a single step, or a list of points, with no development to the objective. This keeps answers at Level 2.

Exam use: using trigger, mechanism, effect and consequence, with because or so between them.

The wrong model

A marginal utility essay when indifference curves were asked was capped at Level 1 for that answer, and micro questions were answered with macro effects.

Exam use: naming the model in the question and staying inside it.

Diagram drawn but not used

Reports note diagrams that are drawn and never referred to, and explanations of shifts that have no diagram where one was required.

Exam use: referring to the diagram by its labels in the text.

Correlation treated as proof

In data response, answers that treat a pattern across a handful of countries as proof of cause lose marks. The best answers say what the pattern suggests and what it cannot show.

Exam use: writing 'this suggests' and giving one limit.

Practice question 4 marks Explain how an increase in the central bank's interest rate may affect aggregate demand.
What the mark scheme wants
  • One mark for the higher cost of borrowing or reward to saving.
  • One mark for the effect on consumption and investment.
  • Up to 2 further marks for development: AD is lowered, and the effect on output, employment or the price level.
PlanFour steps in two or three sentences. Do not evaluate: this part is not asking for it.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A higher interest rate raises the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving. Households may cut spending on durable goods and firms may postpone investment. Consumption and investment are components of AD, so AD may shift left, lowering real GDP and the price level.

Practice question 8 marks Analyse how a tax on a good with a negative consumption externality could move a market closer to allocative efficiency, using a diagram.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Diagram: MPB, MSB below MPB, supply (MPC = MSC), and the tax shifting supply.
  • Over-consumption at the free market output, where MPB meets MPC.
  • The tax raises price and lowers quantity towards the social optimum (MSB = MSC).
  • Link to allocative efficiency and the welfare loss removed.
PlanDraw first, then three linked steps: free market output, the effect of the tax, the efficiency gain.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A negative consumption externality exists when consuming the good harms third parties, so marginal social benefit (MSB) is below marginal private benefit (MPB). Examples are alcohol or cigarettes. In the diagram the MSB curve lies below the demand curve (MPB), and supply is MPC = MSC. In a free market, output is where MPB meets supply, at Q1. At Q1 the social benefit of the last unit is below its cost, because MSB is lower than MPB, so the market over-consumes and there is a welfare loss triangle between Q2 and Q1.

An indirect tax raises the cost of supply, so the supply curve shifts left by the amount of the tax. The price rises from P1 to P2 and the quantity falls towards Q2, where MSB equals marginal social cost. If the tax is set equal to the gap between MPB and MSB at Q2, the market could reach the social optimum, so the welfare loss is removed. This moves the market towards allocative efficiency, because resources are no longer over-allocated to the good. The government also gains tax revenue, which could be spent on treating the harm. The size of the improvement depends on how close the tax is to the external cost and on how responsive demand is.

Practice question 6 marks Table: in five countries, a higher Gini coefficient is associated with a lower ratio of female to male literacy. Consider whether the table shows that inequality causes lower female literacy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Select and quote two countries' figures.
  • Describe the pattern across the five.
  • Suggest a link: unequal access to schooling for girls in poor households.
  • State a limit: five countries cannot prove cause; other factors such as income may be responsible.
PlanQuote, interpret, then say what the table cannot show.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

In the table, the country with the highest Gini coefficient has the lowest ratio of female to male literacy, and the country with the lowest Gini coefficient has the highest ratio. Across all five countries, a higher Gini coefficient goes with a lower ratio, so there is a negative relationship. This pattern suggests that inequality may limit girls' access to schooling. In an unequal country, poorer households may be unable to afford fees, uniforms or transport, and when families must choose, they may send boys to school before girls. If poorer girls leave school early, literacy among women falls relative to men. Lower literacy then limits women's later earnings, which could keep inequality high, so the link could run in both directions.

However, the table does not show that inequality causes lower female literacy. Five countries is a very small sample, and a correlation only shows that two variables move together. Other factors, such as average income, cultural attitudes to girls' education, or government spending on schools, could explain both a high Gini coefficient and low literacy. The table also gives one year, so it cannot show whether changes in inequality led to changes in literacy over time. Therefore the table supports the claim only weakly: it is consistent with inequality reducing girls' schooling, but more countries, several years and controls for income would be needed to show cause.

Practice question 20 marks Assess whether a government should use an increase in public spending to reduce unemployment in a country with a negative output gap.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Chain 1: spending raises AD, the multiplier raises income, output and employment rise with spare capacity.
  • Chain 2: the effect on the budget deficit and borrowing costs, and crowding out.
  • Chain 3: the supply-side effect if the spending is on infrastructure or training.
  • Diagram: AD and AS with the negative output gap, or an injections and withdrawals diagram if the stem asks for it.
  • Evaluation (6 marks): time lags, the size of the multiplier, the state of public finances, alternatives.
PlanThree developed chains, each ending at employment. Name the country context in each paragraph.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

An increase in public spending is a component of AD, so AD shifts right. In an economy with a negative output gap there are unemployed workers and spare capacity, so firms can raise output without much pressure on prices. Real GDP rises, and firms take on more workers, so unemployment falls. The multiplier strengthens the effect: wages paid to newly employed workers are partly spent, which raises demand for other firms' products and creates further jobs. If the marginal propensity to consume is 0.75, the multiplier is 4, so $5bn of spending could raise income by up to $20bn, although leakages such as taxes and imports reduce this.

A second chain concerns the budget. As employment rises, income tax and VAT receipts rise and spending on unemployment benefits falls, so part of the cost is recovered. But if the spending is financed by borrowing, the deficit and the national debt rise. The government may have to pay higher interest, or borrow from the private sector, which could push up interest rates and crowd out private investment. That would lower the net effect on jobs.

A third chain depends on the type of spending. If the money goes into infrastructure, training or technology, it raises the productive capacity of the economy as well as demand, so LRAS shifts right. This means that employment gains could last, and lower costs could help firms compete, so the extra jobs are less likely to disappear when the stimulus ends. Spending on transfer payments or public sector wages, by contrast, may raise demand only temporarily.

However, there are limits. Time lags in planning and building projects could mean that the spending arrives after the recession is over, so it could add to inflation instead. The multiplier may be small in an open economy where much of the extra income is spent on imports, or where firms and households save the money because they are uncertain about the future. If unemployment is structural, because workers lack the skills required, extra demand may not reduce it. On balance, increased public spending is likely to reduce unemployment in this economy, because the negative output gap means extra demand mainly raises output. It is most effective if the spending is on productive projects that can start quickly and if the government's borrowing costs remain low. Where unemployment is structural, supply-side policies could be more effective than extra spending.

B · Multiple choice questions (Paper 1)

Choose an answer, then read the reason. Reworded from the paper and question number shown.

Top tips

  • Name the objective at the end. Growth, efficiency, equity, inflation, the current account.
  • Use link words. Because, so, which means, leading to.
  • Three chains beat five. Develop each, and apply each to the context.
  • Draw and use the diagram. Refer to its labels in your writing.
  • Stay inside the model. Micro with micro, the named policy only.
  • Say what the data suggest. And what it cannot show.
