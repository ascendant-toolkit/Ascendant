Correcting Current Account Imbalances

6.5.1 to 6.5.2: expenditure-switching, expenditure-reducing and supply-side policies, with their limits. Animated chains, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

To correct a current account deficit a government can use expenditure-switching policies, which move spending from imports to domestic goods (a depreciation or devaluation, protectionism such as tariffs and quotas, and export subsidies), and expenditure-reducing policies, which cut total spending and so imports (higher taxes, lower government spending and higher interest rates), as well as supply-side policies that improve long-run competitiveness. Each has costs: switching policies can provoke retaliation and depend on elasticities, reducing policies may cut output and employment and cause deflationary pressure, and supply-side policies are slow and uncertain. For a surplus the policies are broadly the opposite, such as an appreciation, lower tariffs or expansionary policy. The best choice depends on the cause of the imbalance, the size, the time available and the exchange rate system.

2 · Judging the options

  • Floating or fixed: under a floating rate the exchange rate may adjust and offset some policies (higher interest rates may raise the currency and hurt exports); under a fixed rate, devaluation or deflation may be needed.
  • Cause: a deficit from high consumer spending suits reducing policies; one from low competitiveness suits supply-side policy.
  • Time: tariffs and depreciation work faster than supply-side policy.
  • Wider aims: reducing policies may conflict with growth and unemployment targets.
Exam tipFor "discuss" or "assess" questions, give advantages and limitations of each policy, then judge which is most effective given the cause, speed and side effects. Using the term expenditure-switching or expenditure-reducing correctly earns credit.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A tariff on imported goods

A government taxes imported electronics. They become dearer, so people buy fewer imports, but trading partners may retaliate and firms that use imported parts face higher costs.

Exam use: expenditure switching through protection.

Tax rises and spending cuts

A government facing a large deficit raises taxes and cuts spending. Households have less income, buy fewer imports, and the deficit narrows, but growth slows and unemployment may rise.

Exam use: expenditure-reducing policy.

Investing in skills and technology

A government spends on training and research so firms become more productive and export more. The results may take many years.

Exam use: supply-side policy and competitiveness.

A central bank raises rates

Higher interest rates reduce borrowing and spending, cutting imports. They may also attract foreign savings and raise the currency, which makes exports dearer.

Exam use: interest rates and the current account.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/23 · Q5(a) 8 marks Explain how import tariffs might correct an imbalance in the current account of the balance of payments and consider whether these tariffs are the best way of correcting such an imbalance.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): what is meant by an import tariff (1); what is measured by the current account (1); what is meant by an imbalance in terms of a surplus or deficit (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): how import tariffs affect the prices of imports (1), how this may affect domestic consumption of imports (1), and how this may correct the imbalance (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): the final effect is uncertain, for example because of the PED for imports, other policies that could be used, and retaliation by trading competitors; one mark is reserved for a justified conclusion.
PlanDefine tariff, current account, imbalance. Tariff: import prices up, imports down, deficit narrows. Evaluate: PED, retaliation, other policies.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

An import tariff is a tax on imported goods. The current account measures trade in goods and services and primary and secondary income, and an imbalance means a deficit, where outflows exceed inflows, or a surplus.

A tariff raises the price of imports, so households and firms may buy fewer of them and switch to domestic goods. Spending on imports falls, which reduces a current account deficit.

Tariffs are not necessarily the best way. If demand for imports is inelastic, imports fall by little, trading partners may retaliate and cut the country's exports, and other policies such as supply-side measures could tackle the cause of the deficit. A tariff may therefore help in the short run but is unlikely to be the best long-run solution.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/23 · Q4(b) 12 marks Discuss the extent to which expenditure-switching policies and expenditure-reducing policies might be able to reduce a persistent deficit on the current account of the balance of payments.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the advantages and disadvantages of expenditure-switching policies (up to 4; maximum 3 if only advantages or only disadvantages) and of expenditure-reducing policies (up to 4; maximum 3 if one-sided). Expenditure-reducing policies lower domestic AD to cut imports, for example raising taxes or reducing government spending; expenditure-switching policies encourage people to buy domestic goods, for example protectionism, supply-side policies and exchange rate policies.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): compares the advantages and disadvantages of the two approaches to reach a reasoned conclusion on which gives the greater overall benefit in reducing a persistent deficit.
PlanReducing: taxes up, spending down; cuts imports but costs output and jobs. Switching: tariffs, depreciation, supply-side; shifts spending but retaliation, elasticity. Compare and conclude.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Expenditure-reducing policies, such as higher taxes or cuts in government spending, lower AD and so spending on imports, but they may also reduce output and increase unemployment, and the effect may be slow. Expenditure-switching policies, such as a depreciation, tariffs or supply-side measures, move spending from imports to domestic goods without cutting total spending, but a depreciation depends on elasticities, tariffs may provoke retaliation, and supply-side policies are slow.

For a persistent deficit caused by weak competitiveness, switching policies, especially supply-side measures, may have the greater long-run benefit, because reducing policies only cut spending without fixing the cause. In the short run, reducing policies may be more certain. A mix is likely to be most effective.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/21 · Q4(b) 12 marks Discuss whether supply-side policies are an effective way of correcting a deficit on an economy's current account of the balance of payments.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the potential advantages of supply-side policies (up to 4); the potential disadvantages (up to 4). Policies may include lowering income tax, spending on education and training, reducing trade union power, privatisation and deregulation, each of which does not necessarily deliver the intended result.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether supply-side policies are an effective way of correcting a deficit, with a conclusion (1 mark reserved).
PlanSupply-side: productivity, costs, quality, so exports up and imports down. Limits: slow, costly, uncertain. Judge: effective long run, not short run.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Supply-side policies, such as spending on education and training or reducing red tape, may raise productivity and cut costs, making exports cheaper and better and reducing the need for imports. This tackles the cause of a competitiveness-based deficit. But the policies may not increase labour productivity or work incentives as intended, they can be costly, and the effects may take many years to appear.

Supply-side policies may be effective in the long run, as they improve competitiveness, but they are unlikely to correct a deficit quickly. Their success depends on the cause of the deficit and on whether the policies actually raise productivity. They may be best combined with short-run measures.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/21 · Q4(b) 12 marks Discuss whether expenditure-reducing policies are likely to reduce the current account deficit on the balance of payments for an economy with a floating exchange rate.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the potential success of expenditure-reducing policies in reducing the deficit (up to 4) and their limitations (up to 4). Expenditure-reducing policies reduce total spending and aim to cut imports and/or raise exports; examples include raising interest rates, reducing the money supply and increasing taxes.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether the policies are likely to reduce the deficit for an economy with a floating exchange rate, with a conclusion.
PlanReducing policies: less income, fewer imports. Floating rate: higher rates raise the currency, which hurts exports. Costs: output, jobs. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Higher taxes, lower government spending or higher interest rates reduce disposable income and AD, so households buy fewer imports and the deficit may narrow. Lower demand may also reduce inflation, making exports more competitive over time. But with a floating exchange rate, higher interest rates may attract foreign funds and raise the currency, making exports dearer and imports cheaper, which could offset the policy. The policies may also reduce output and increase unemployment.

The policies are likely to reduce the deficit in the short run if the deficit is caused by excess domestic demand, but the effect may be limited by the exchange rate and by lower growth. They are less likely to succeed if the deficit is caused by poor competitiveness.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/21 · Q4(b) 12 marks Discuss whether protectionist policies would be the most effective way to correct a current account deficit on the balance of payments in an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the potential advantages of protectionist policies (up to 4), such as tariffs making imports dearer and discouraging consumption of them, quotas restricting the volume of imports, embargoes banning imports, red tape making imports harder, and export subsidies making domestic products more price competitive; and their limitations (up to 4), such as tariffs being less effective if demand for imports is price inelastic.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether protectionist policies would be the most effective way to correct a current account deficit, with a conclusion (1 mark reserved).
PlanProtection: tariffs, quotas, embargoes, red tape, export subsidies cut imports or raise exports. Limits: inelastic demand, retaliation, higher costs. Compare with other policies and judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Tariffs and quotas raise the price or limit the volume of imports, and export subsidies make exports cheaper, so protectionism could reduce a deficit quickly. But if demand for imports is price inelastic, a tariff reduces imports by little, other countries may retaliate and reduce the country's exports, and firms that rely on imported inputs face higher costs. Protectionism also does not address the underlying lack of competitiveness.

Protectionism may be effective in the short run but is unlikely to be the most effective overall, since supply-side policies could tackle the cause of the deficit, and depreciation or reducing policies may avoid the risk of retaliation. Its effectiveness depends on elasticities and the response of trading partners.

Top tips

  • Know the two types. Switching: imports to domestic. Reducing: cut total spending.
  • Give limits for each. Retaliation, elasticity, lost output, time lags.
  • Match policy to cause. Demand-led deficit or competitiveness-led deficit.
  • Floating or fixed. The exchange rate may offset policy.
  • Short versus long run. Tariffs fast; supply-side slow.
  • Compare. Weigh the policies against each other.
  • Conclude. A mix may work best.
