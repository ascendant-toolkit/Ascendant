National Income and GDP

4.1.1: national income, the output, income and expenditure methods of measuring GDP, value added, real GDP and GDP per head, and the limits of GDP. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and past paper questions.

This part in five sentences

National income is the total value of the goods and services an economy produces in a period, normally a year. Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders, whoever owns the firms. Because every pound or dollar spent is also income for someone, there are three ways to measure it that should give the same total: the output (value added) method, the income method and the expenditure method, where GDP = C + I + G + (X − M). Only final goods are counted, to avoid double counting of intermediate goods. Real GDP adjusts for inflation and GDP per capita divides by population, but GDP has limits as a measure of living standards, since it misses the informal economy, unpaid work, inequality and damage to the environment.

1 · What GDP measures

  • Flow of output: GDP is a flow (value per year), not a stock of wealth.
  • Inside the borders: GDP counts output produced in the country, including that of foreign-owned firms located there, and excludes output of the country's firms abroad. (GNI, on the next page, adjusts for this.)
  • Final goods only: goods bought for consumption, investment, government use or export. Intermediate goods (flour bought by a baker) are used up in making other goods and are not counted separately, because their value is already in the final price.
  • Market value: goods are valued at prices. Output not sold for money (home production, voluntary work) is generally excluded.
  • Circular flow link: the value of output equals the incomes paid to factors of production, which equals the spending on that output.

2 · Three ways to measure GDP

MethodWhat is added upExample
Output (value added)The value added at each stage of production in every industry: value of output minus the cost of intermediate goodsFarm $40 + mill $30 + bakery $50 = $120
IncomeIncomes earned from producing output: wages, rent, interest and profit (before tax)Wages + rent + interest + profit
ExpenditureTotal spending on final goods and services: C + I + G + (X − M)Consumption + investment + government spending + net exports
  • C: household consumption; I: investment by firms (capital goods, stocks); G: government spending on goods and services (not transfer payments such as pensions, which are not payment for output); X − M: exports minus imports, so that spending on imports is removed and foreign spending on exports is added.
  • In practice the three measures differ a little because of data errors; a statistical discrepancy is used to reconcile them.

  • If every sale were added ($40 + $70 + $120 = $230), the output of the farmer and miller would be counted more than once. The correct addition is the final price, $120, which equals the sum of value added.

4 · Real GDP and GDP per capita

  • Nominal (money) GDP is measured at current prices, so it can rise because prices rise. Real GDP is adjusted for inflation using a price index (the GDP deflator): real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ price index × 100. Real GDP shows changes in the actual quantity of output.
  • GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population. It is a better indicator of average living standards than total GDP, since a country can have a large GDP and a low income per person.
  • Example: nominal GDP $550bn with a price index of 110 gives real GDP of $500bn.

5 · Limits of GDP as a measure of living standards

  • Informal and illegal activity is not recorded, so poorer economies may be understated.
  • Unpaid work (housework, voluntary work) is excluded.
  • Distribution: an average hides inequality.
  • Quality and variety of goods, leisure time and working conditions are not captured.
  • Environmental damage and resource depletion are not deducted, and goods that harm welfare still raise GDP.
  • Different price levels across countries make exchange-rate comparisons misleading, which is why purchasing power adjustments are used.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Bread from field to shop

A farmer, a miller and a baker each add value. Only the final price of the loaf enters GDP, which equals the sum of value added, so the wheat and flour are not counted twice.

Exam use: value added and double counting.

A foreign-owned car plant

A car factory owned by an overseas firm produces cars in the country. The output counts in GDP because it is produced inside the borders, even though the profits go abroad.

Exam use: GDP counts output inside the borders, whoever owns the firm.

Unpaid care and the informal economy

Parents caring for children, volunteers and street traders paid in cash may not appear in official figures. GDP may understate output and living standards, especially in lower-income economies.

Exam use: limits of GDP as a measure.

Rebuilding after a flood

Spending on repairs raises recorded GDP, but people are no better off than before the flood. GDP counts the spending, not the loss of wealth.

Exam use: why higher GDP does not always mean higher welfare.

Comparing two countries

One country has a larger GDP but a much bigger population, so GDP per head is lower. Comparing per head figures, and using purchasing power adjustments, gives a better picture of average living standards.

Exam use: GDP per capita and its use.

Top tips

  • Say 'final' and 'inside the borders'. The two key words in the definition.
  • Write the formula. C + I + G + (X − M).
  • Three methods, one total. Output, income, expenditure.
  • Avoid double counting. Count final goods or value added, not every sale.
  • Real not nominal. Adjust for inflation with a price index.
  • Per head. Divide by population to compare living standards.
  • Give limits. Informal economy, inequality, environment, unpaid work.
