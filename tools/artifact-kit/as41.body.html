National Income Statistics: GDP, GNI and NNI

4.1.1 to 4.1.4: national income, GDP, GNI and NNI, adjusting from market prices to basic prices and from gross to net values. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

National income is the total value of the goods and services an economy produces in a year, which equals the total income earned and the total expenditure on that output. Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the value of final output produced within a country's borders, whoever owns the firms; Gross National Income (GNI) is GDP plus net primary income from abroad (income earned by residents overseas minus income sent home by foreign owners); and Net National Income (NNI) is GNI minus capital consumption (depreciation). Figures can be quoted at market prices (including taxes on products, excluding subsidies) or at basic prices (what producers actually receive: market prices minus taxes on products plus subsidies), and as gross values (before depreciation) or net values (after it).

1 · The three measures (4.1.1 to 4.1.2)

MeasureWhat it isCalculation
GDPValue of all final goods and services produced inside the country in a year (C + I + G + X − M)Output, income or expenditure
GNIIncome of a country's residents, wherever it is earnedGDP + net primary income from abroad
NNINational income after allowing for the wear and tear of capitalGNI − capital consumption (depreciation)
  • Primary income from abroad = profits, interest, dividends and wages earned by residents overseas. Net primary income = income received from abroad − income paid abroad. A country with many foreign-owned firms may have GNI below GDP; one with many citizens working or investing overseas may have GNI above GDP.
  • Capital consumption is the value of capital goods worn out or made obsolete in the year; subtracting it gives a better picture of the income that can be spent without running down capital.
  • Per capita figures divide by population, and real figures adjust for inflation. They are used to compare living standards but have limits (they ignore the informal economy, unpaid work, inequality and the environment).

2 · Market prices, basic prices, gross and net (4.1.3 to 4.1.4)

  • Market prices are what consumers pay: they include taxes on products (such as VAT) and exclude subsidies.
  • Basic prices are what producers receive: market prices − taxes on products + subsidies on products.
  • Gross → net: subtract capital consumption (depreciation). A net value is lower than the corresponding gross value.
  • Example: GDP at market prices $120bn; taxes on products $15bn; subsidies $5bn. GDP at basic prices = 120 − 15 + 5 = $110bn.
Exam tipFor calculations, write the steps: GDP + net primary income = GNI; GNI − depreciation = NNI. Be careful with signs: net income from abroad can be negative.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A country with many foreign factories

Overseas firms own large factories and send their profits home. GDP counts all the output made inside the country, but some income leaves, so net primary income from abroad is negative and GNI is smaller than GDP.

Exam use: GDP versus GNI and net primary income.

A country with many overseas workers

Millions of citizens work abroad and send wages home. Those earnings count as primary income from abroad, so GNI exceeds GDP.

Exam use: net primary income from abroad being positive.

Replacing worn-out machines

A firm spends $10m a year replacing machinery that wears out. National income after allowing for this wear and tear is lower than the gross figure, which is why NNI is lower than GNI.

Exam use: capital consumption.

Sales tax and basic prices

A $100 product includes $15 of sales tax, so the producer receives $85 (before any subsidy). GDP at market prices counts $100, while GDP at basic prices counts the amount producers actually receive.

Exam use: market prices versus basic prices.

Top tips

  • GDP is inside the borders. Whoever owns the firms.
  • GNI adds net primary income. Income from abroad less income paid abroad. It may be negative.
  • NNI subtracts depreciation. Capital consumption: the wear and tear of capital.
  • Write the steps. GDP, then GNI, then NNI. Mark the signs.
  • Market to basic prices. Subtract taxes on products, add subsidies.
  • Gross is bigger than net. Net means after depreciation.
  • Know the limits. Informal output, inequality and the environment are missed.
