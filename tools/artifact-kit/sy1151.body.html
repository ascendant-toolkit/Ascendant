International Aid: Forms, Reasons and Effects

11.5.1: the forms of aid, why countries give it, how it can raise growth through the savings and foreign exchange gaps, and why it sometimes fails. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

International aid is the transfer of resources (money, goods, expertise) from governments, international organisations and charities to developing countries, on concessional (easier than market) terms. It comes in many forms (grants, soft loans, technical assistance, food and emergency aid; bilateral or multilateral; tied or untied), is given for humanitarian, political and economic reasons, and can raise growth by filling the savings and foreign exchange gaps and funding infrastructure, health and education. Its effects depend on how it is used, and critics argue that it can create dependency, corruption, distortion and debt.

2 · Why countries give aid

  • Humanitarian: to relieve hunger, disease and disaster, and to reduce absolute poverty.
  • Economic self-interest: to create future markets and trading partners, secure supplies of raw materials, and reduce migration pressures. Tied aid directly supports the donor's exports.
  • Political and strategic: to gain allies, influence or access, and reflect historical ties.
  • Global concerns: to deal with shared problems such as pandemics, climate change and instability.
  • The UN target is for high-income countries to give 0.7% of their gross national income as aid. Few meet it, though several do.

3 · How aid can promote growth: the two gaps

  • The savings gap: a poor country has low saving, so it cannot fund the investment needed for growth. In the Harrod-Domar idea, growth = investment rate ÷ capital-output ratio. Aid can add to the investment funds.
  • The foreign exchange gap: a developing country needs to import capital goods and technology but earns little foreign currency. Aid provides foreign exchange to pay for them.
  • Human capital and infrastructure: aid spent on schools, health, roads, water and power raises productivity and potential output (an outward shift of the PPC and long-run aggregate supply).
  • Emergency and food aid saves lives in the short run and prevents the loss of human capital.

4 · Problems with aid

  • Dependency: long-term reliance can weaken incentives for reform and local initiative, and over-reliance on technical assistance may crowd out local skills.
  • Corruption and waste: money may be diverted or spent on prestige projects, and weak governance reduces the benefit.
  • Tied aid raises the cost of projects and favours donor firms; conditions (privatisation, trade opening) may harm some groups.
  • Distortion: free food aid can lower local food prices and hurt local farmers; large inflows can raise the exchange rate (an aid version of Dutch disease).
  • Debt: loan aid must be repaid, so if it is poorly invested it adds to the debt burden.
  • Unpredictability: aid can be cut when donors' politics change, making planning hard.

The importance of aid

Aid matters most for the poorest, least developed countries, where it may be a large share of government spending and of foreign exchange. For emerging economies it is small relative to trade, investment and remittances. Evidence on its impact on growth is mixed: it works best in countries with sound institutions and when it funds health, education and infrastructure.

Evaluation pointWhether aid works depends on the type (grants for health versus tied loans), the recipient's governance, how it is spent and whether it is predictable. Alternatives such as trade access, debt relief, remittances and FDI may matter as much as aid.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

The 0.7% target

In the 1970s the United Nations suggested that rich countries give 0.7% of their gross national income as aid. A handful of countries, mostly in northern Europe, meet or exceed it, while many of the largest economies give a much smaller share of income, though not necessarily a small amount in absolute dollars.

Exam use: the importance and scale of aid, and the difference between the amount and the share of income.

Tied aid and health supplies

When aid for health care must be spent on the donor's own medicines, recipients may pay more than they would for cheaper generic drugs from other countries. Tying can therefore reduce the number of people treated per dollar of aid.

Exam use: tied aid as a cost to the recipient and a benefit to donor exporters.

Emergency aid after disasters

After an earthquake or flood, food, shelter and medical teams from governments, UN agencies and charities arrive quickly. This saves lives, but long-term rebuilding depends on grants and loans for infrastructure and on local capacity.

Exam use: emergency aid versus long-term development aid.

Aid and local farmers

When large amounts of free food are shipped in, local grain prices can fall and farmers lose income, which may reduce local production. Aid agencies now often buy food locally or give cash to avoid this distortion.

Exam use: unintended effects of aid on local markets.

Aid takes several forms. Grants are gifts, soft loans have low interest and long repayment, technical assistance provides experts and training, and food and emergency aid meet urgent needs. Aid can be bilateral from one government or multilateral through bodies such as the World Bank, and it can be tied to purchases from the donor. Aid could promote development by filling the savings gap, since investment in schools, health, roads and power raises productivity and potential output. It also supplies foreign exchange for imports of capital goods, which may raise growth by increasing investment.

However, aid may create dependency and weaken local initiative. Tied aid may raise costs, and corruption may divert funds. Free food could lower prices and harm local farmers, and loans may add to the debt burden. Overall, aid is likely to promote development when it is given as grants, spent on infrastructure, health and education, and where governance is sound. If it is tied or poorly used, the benefit may be small.

Practice question · modelled on the 8 mark style (no past paper) 8 marks Explain two reasons why high-income countries give aid to low-income countries.
What the mark scheme wants

  • Economic or political self-interest with development: future markets and trade partners, access to raw materials, reducing migration pressure, strategic alliances.
  • Each reason explained with a short chain of analysis.

One reason is humanitarian. Governments and their voters may want to reduce absolute poverty and respond to hunger, disease and disasters. Helping poor countries grow could also reduce conflict and the spread of disease, which benefits donors in the longer term.

A second reason is economic self-interest. Aid, especially tied aid, can create markets for the donor's exports and help recipient countries become richer trading partners. A richer neighbour may also reduce pressures to migrate, which may benefit the donor.

Top tips

  • Split the forms. Grants or loans, tied or untied, bilateral or multilateral, emergency or long-term.
  • Give two types of reason. Humanitarian and self-interest (economic or political).
  • Use the two gaps. Savings gap and foreign exchange gap link aid to investment and growth.
  • Say what the aid is spent on. Capital goods, health and education may raise long-run growth. Consumption may not.
  • Always include costs. Dependency, corruption, tied aid, distortion of local markets and debt.
  • Judge with conditions. Aid works better with good governance, grants and untied funding.
  • Mention alternatives. Trade access, FDI, debt relief and remittances can matter as much.
