AS Level Economics · 9708 · Syllabus 3.1.1

Reasons for Government Intervention in Markets

3.1.1: market failure, public goods and the free rider problem, merit and demerit goods, information failure and why governments control prices, with animated diagrams, labs and past paper questions.

This part in five sentences

The price mechanism allocates resources well when buyers and sellers are well informed and every good can be sold to those who pay for it, but in some markets it produces an inefficient allocation of resources, which is called market failure, and that is the main reason governments intervene. Public goods are non-excludable and non-rival, so the free rider problem means private firms cannot earn revenue and the free market does not supply them. Merit goods are better for people than they realise and so are under-consumed, and demerit goods are worse for people than they realise and so are over-consumed, both mainly because of imperfect information (and also income, price and addiction). Governments also fix maximum prices to make essentials affordable and minimum prices to protect producers' incomes, secure food supplies or discourage demerit goods. Intervention has an opportunity cost and can fail, so a good answer explains the reason for intervening and then considers whether the intervention is likely to work.

1 · The starting point: when the market fails

  • The price mechanism rations scarce resources and signals to producers what consumers want. Resources go where they are most valued, and an efficient allocation of resources results if consumers know what they are buying and producers can charge for what they supply.
  • Market failure is an inefficient allocation of resources by the free market. The market produces too much of some goods, too little of others, or none at all.
  • That gives a government a reason to intervene. This page is about the reasons; the methods (taxes, subsidies, direct provision, regulation) are in Syllabus 3.2.

2 · Public goods and the free rider problem

  • Non-excludable: once the good is provided, nobody can be stopped from using it, even if they have not paid.
  • Non-rival: one person's use does not reduce the amount available to others.
  • Free rider problem: because people can use the good without paying, many will not pay, so a private firm cannot earn enough revenue to cover its costs, will make a loss, and will not supply it, even if the benefit to society is greater than the cost.
  • Government provision: the state provides public goods and pays for them from tax revenue, so that everyone contributes. The good is free at the point of use, but it is not free to society: it has an opportunity cost.
  • Quantity is hard to judge: because people do not reveal how much they value the good, the government may not know how much to provide, which can lead to over- or under-provision.

  • Public good or free good? Both are non-excludable and non-rival, but a public good costs resources to produce, while a free good has zero opportunity cost.
  • Can the market ever supply public goods? Sometimes. Technology or fees can make a good excludable (a toll road, a ticketed beach, encrypted TV), which turns it into a private good, and charities may fund some. Pure public goods such as national defence are rarely supplied this way.
  • Merit goods are not public goods. Education and healthcare can be charged for and used up by one person, so a market can supply them, but it supplies too little.

3 · Merit goods: under-consumption

  • Definition: a merit good is a good that is thought to be more beneficial to consumers than they realise, so it is under-consumed and under-provided by the free market.
  • Why consumers under-consume them: imperfect information (they do not appreciate the full value, for example of education or vaccinations), low income (they cannot afford them) and a high price.
  • Why firms under-provide them: firms supply only if they can earn a profit, which is less likely where many people cannot pay.
  • Why the state provides them: they are essential, many people cannot pay, and the whole society gains from a healthier, more educated population, which supports growth and a fairer distribution of income.
  • Provided by both sectors: because the cost to the state of providing all of it may be too high, many countries rely on private providers as well, especially for healthcare in low and middle-income countries.
  • Extension (not required at AS): economists also explain merit goods through positive externalities, where others gain from your consumption.

4 · Demerit goods: over-consumption

  • Definition: a demerit good is a good that is thought to be worse for consumers than they realise, so it is over-consumed and over-provided by the free market.
  • Why consumers over-consume them: imperfect information about the health risks, addiction or habit, low prices, low income and poor education, and peer influence.
  • Why firms over-provide them: the products are profitable, and sellers have little incentive to warn consumers about the harm.
  • Why the state intervenes: to protect health, reduce healthcare costs and raise the productivity of the workforce. These benefits can outweigh the loss of tax revenue from lower sales.
  • Some goods are both: a plastic bottle is a private good (excludable, rival) and a demerit good, as its use can harm the environment.
  • Extension (not required at AS): negative externalities give another explanation.

  • Governments respond with information campaigns, labelling, regulation or direct provision (covered in 3.2).

7 · Other reasons you may meet

  • Equity: governments may intervene so that everyone has access to essentials whatever their income.
  • Employment and growth: a healthier, better-educated workforce is more productive.
  • Protecting vulnerable groups and consumers from harm, exploitation and unsafe products.
  • Externalities, monopoly power and macroeconomic stability are also reasons in A Level work, but the AS mark schemes focus on the reasons above.

8 · Does intervention always work?

  • Opportunity cost: money spent on provision cannot be spent elsewhere, especially where the tax base is small.
  • Information: the government may not know which goods to provide or how much.
  • Cost and efficiency: state provision may be wasteful without the profit motive, and free provision can cause excess demand and waiting.
  • Unintended consequences: price controls create shortages or surpluses and may help the wrong people.
  • Behaviour: if people still do not see the benefits of a merit good, making it free may not raise consumption; time, travel or waiting costs can also deter use.
  • Political aims and time lags can lead to poorly chosen or late action.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Education in Sub-Saharan Africa

Education is a merit good and would be under-provided if left to the private sector. In many low and lower middle-income countries governments provide education directly or subsidise it, and may make it compulsory. Sub-Saharan Africa has some of the highest rates of children not in education, mainly because of poverty. Kenya has made government education free from primary to high school, with subsidies for boarding fees for low-income families.

Exam use: an example of a merit good, information failure and the limits of funding. Evaluate with opportunity cost.

Healthcare reform in Pakistan

Pakistan's government has promised to reform healthcare so that quality care is available to all, not only those who can pay private providers. The main issues are funding with a limited tax base, building hospitals in rural areas, preventative care such as immunisation, and regulating private and state providers.

Exam use: healthcare as a merit good, provided by both sectors, with real funding constraints.

Vaccination programmes

Governments often offer vaccinations free because individuals may not appreciate the benefit, cost may deter some, and wide coverage protects the whole population. A vaccine is a private good, but protection against the spread of disease works much like a public good.

Exam use: merit goods, information failure and why intervention can raise overall welfare.

Smoking bans and graphic warnings

Many countries ban smoking in public places and require health warnings and graphic photographs on packaging. Governments justify this to protect health, make the workforce more productive and cut healthcare spending. These arguments have usually outweighed the loss of tax revenue from falling tobacco sales.

Exam use: cigarettes as the standard demerit good, with information failure as the reason.

Lighthouses, street lighting and flood defences

These are classic public goods. Everyone nearby benefits and nobody can be stopped from benefiting, so private firms cannot charge and the government pays from taxation.

Exam use: a lighthouse or flood barrier as a clear example of both characteristics of a public good.

A beach or park

A beach that is open to everyone looks like a public good. But if an entry fee is charged, or visitor numbers can be limited, it becomes excludable, and if it becomes overcrowded it is rival. It may then be better described as a private good.

Exam use: the difference between a public good and a good that merely looks free.

Bread price ceilings

In some former socialist economies, basic foods such as bread were given a maximum price to protect low-income households. The price was held below the market level, and shortages and queues were common.

Exam use: a maximum price example. State the aim (affordability) and explain the consequences.

Rent controls for key workers

Rent controls aim to provide affordable housing for low-income families and to attract key workers such as nurses and teachers to areas with high housing costs. Where rent is held below the equilibrium, the supply of rented housing may fall and demand exceed supply.

Exam use: an aim for a maximum price other than low income, linked to shortages in Syllabus 3.2.

Wheat and rice price floors

Many governments support farmers' incomes with a minimum price for key crops such as wheat and rice. Farm incomes are unpredictable because supply is uncertain, and governments also want to keep food supplies secure.

Exam use: a minimum price example. Evaluate: farmers gain, consumers pay more and it can be an inefficient use of resources.

Minimum unit pricing for alcohol

Scotland introduced a minimum price per unit of alcohol in 2018 to reduce harmful drinking. It targets cheap, strong drinks, but its effect depends on how many drinkers respond to the higher price and on whether they switch to other products.

Exam use: a minimum price used for a demerit good, and the question of whether it is the best policy.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/23 · Q2(a) 8 marks A government has decided to spend money building a new barrier to prevent flooding across a city. Explain why this flood defence barrier is likely to meet the characteristics of a public good.
What the mark scheme wants

  • Application (up to 6): that a flood defence system is a public good (1), why it is non-excludable (1) and why it is non-rival (1); that it cannot be a private good (1), with accurate reference to both excludability and rivalry (1) and the free rider problem (1).
PlanPrivate good: excludable and rival. Public good: neither. Flood barrier: nobody can be excluded, one person's protection does not reduce another's. Free riders, so no private provision.

A private good is excludable, so non-payers can be stopped from using it, and rival, so one person's use reduces what is left for others. A public good is non-excludable and non-rival.

A flood barrier protects everyone in the city. It is non-excludable because it is impossible to protect only those who paid, and non-rival because one household's protection does not reduce the protection of others. Because people can benefit without paying, there is a free rider problem, so a private firm could not raise enough revenue, and the government is likely to fund it from taxation.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/22 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain, using examples, the difference between private goods and public goods and why public goods will not be supplied by private enterprise.
What the mark scheme wants

  • Application (up to 6): why public goods will not be supplied in a free market because of non-excludability and the lack of rivalry in consumption (4 marks), with due reference to the free rider issue (2 marks).

Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A private good, such as a loaf of bread, is excludable, because only those who pay get it, and rival, because once eaten it is not available to others. A public good, such as street lighting, is non-excludable and non-rival.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/21 · Q2(b) 12 marks Assess whether education should be classified as a public good rather than classified as a merit good.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): education is a merit good because it is a private good with the characteristics of being rival and excludable, so it can be provided through a market because it is possible to charge a price, although it can also be provided by the state; a public good is non-rival and non-excludable, so it is difficult or impossible to charge a price because of the free rider problem, and it therefore has to be provided by the state.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): public goods can only be provided by the state; merit goods are under-provided by the free market and governments should intervene; if demand for education is extremely low, a government could provide it entirely through the state; a conclusion that education should be classified as a merit good rather than a public good.
PlanMerit good: rival and excludable, can be charged for, under-consumed. Public good: neither, free riders. Education is rival (class size) and excludable (fees), so merit.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Education is a private good because it is rival, as a place in a class is used by one student and limited by teachers and space, and excludable, as schools can charge fees and exclude those who do not pay. Because a price can be charged, a market can provide it, although many people undervalue it, so it is a merit good and tends to be under-consumed. A public good is both non-excludable and non-rival, so nobody could be charged because of free riders, and only the government could provide it. Education does not meet these characteristics.

Education should therefore be classified as a merit good, not a public good. The government may still provide it mainly or entirely itself, particularly if demand is very low in a country, but that is because of under-consumption and ability to pay, not because it is a public good.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Consider the problem of the provision of merit goods in a market economy and assess whether direct provision of such goods is the most effective way to deal with this problem.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the under-production and under-consumption of merit goods because of information failure in a market economy (up to 4); a comparison of direct provision with at least one alternative, such as subsidising these goods or public education campaigns (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether direct provision is the most effective way to deal with the problem, with a conclusion.
PlanProblem: information failure, under-consumption. Direct provision: free access, but cost, waste, excess demand. Alternatives: subsidy, information campaigns. Judge by cost and effect.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

In a market economy merit goods such as healthcare are under-produced and under-consumed because consumers lack information about their full benefits and many cannot afford them. Direct provision, financed by taxes, can ensure everyone has access, but it has a high opportunity cost, may be wasteful and may lead to excess demand and waiting. A subsidy lowers the price and encourages private provision, but it may not reach those who most need it and may still leave consumers unaware of the benefits. Information campaigns tackle the cause, imperfect information, and are cheap, but they may take time and may not change behaviour much.

Direct provision may be the most effective for essential goods where many cannot pay, but it is costly, so a combination of provision, subsidies and information may be more effective and affordable. The best approach is likely to depend on the good and the country's resources.

Top tips

  • Define market failure properly. An inefficient allocation because the price mechanism fails.
  • Use both characteristics of a public good. Non-excludable and non-rival, then the free rider problem.
  • Information failure is the key AS reason. Say who lacks what: consumers lack the full benefit or harm.
  • Merit under, demerit over. Do not swap them.
  • A price control only bites on the right side of equilibrium. Maximum below, minimum above.
  • Reasons before methods. This topic is why; 3.2 is how.
  • Evaluate with opportunity cost and information. Funding is limited and governments can fail.

Define / StateGive the exact meaning, briefly. No explanation needed.
ExplainGive reasons, with a clear chain of cause and effect.
AnalyseBreak the issue into steps of cause and effect, using a diagram where it helps.
DiscussArguments on both sides, then a judgement.
AssessJudge how important or effective something is, with reasons.
