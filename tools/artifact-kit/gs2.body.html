Economies of Scale, LRAC and the Limits to Growth

The long-run average cost curve, economies and diseconomies of scale, minimum efficient scale and how many firms a market can hold. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

The long-run average cost (LRAC) curve is usually U-shaped. While it falls, the firm enjoys economies of scale; at the lowest point it reaches minimum efficient scale (MES); beyond that it may face diseconomies of scale from problems of management and communication. The size of MES compared with the market decides how many firms can survive at the lowest cost: a small MES means room for many firms, a large MES leaves room for only a few. Growth is therefore not always good: costs can rise, and several things limit how far a firm can grow.

1 · What limits growth?

Diseconomies of scale

Past a certain size average cost may rise: poorer communication and coordination, lower worker motivation, slow decisions, and layers of management. These are internal diseconomies. External ones come from the industry growing too big, for example congestion or higher wages in the area.

Other limits

  • Finance: banks and shareholders may not lend
  • The size of the market: not enough customers
  • Competition law and regulators
  • Owner's wishes: some owners want to stay small and keep control

2 · The picture: long-run average cost and minimum efficient scale

The LRAC curve is U-shaped. Falling LRAC means economies of scale. The lowest point is the minimum efficient scale (MES), the smallest output at which average cost is as low as it can get. Rising LRAC means diseconomies of scale.

3 · Types of economy of scale

EconomyMeaningExample
TechnicalLarger machines and more efficient processes at high outputA bigger oven that bakes more loaves for the same fuel
PurchasingDiscounts on bulk buyingA supermarket chain negotiating prices with suppliers
FinancialCheaper borrowing for a large firmLower interest rates for a well-known company
ManagerialSpecialist managers become affordableA separate finance director
MarketingAdvertising costs spread over more outputA national campaign costs little per unit

Diseconomies of scale come from slower decisions, poorer communication, weaker motivation and difficulty coordinating, so LRAC rises beyond MES. Economies of scale are internal when they come from the firm's own size; external economies come from the growth of the industry (for example a skilled labour force nearby).

4 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Supermarket buying power

A large supermarket chain buys in huge quantities and can negotiate discounts from suppliers, which is a purchasing economy of scale. A small corner shop cannot match that unit cost on the same goods.

Exam use: a purchasing economy of scale. Evaluate: savings may not be passed on, and suppliers may be squeezed.

A very large organisation

A company with many divisions in many countries may find decisions slow, communication poor and staff less motivated. Some large firms have been broken up or have sold divisions to cut costs.

Exam use: diseconomies of scale, and a reason why growth may not lower unit costs.

Car plants and minimum efficient scale

Making cars needs very large plants to reach the lowest cost, so MES is a big share of the market. Only a few firms can reach it, which is a barrier to entry.

Exam use: a large MES relative to the market means few firms; a small MES means many.

B · Practice question with a model answer

Practice (not a past paper question) 8 marks Explain what is meant by economies of scale and why long-run average cost may rise beyond minimum efficient scale.
PlanDefine economies of scale and MES. Give two types with examples. Explain diseconomies and say what happens to LRAC.
KANE

Economies of scale are falls in long-run average cost as a firm increases its output. The minimum efficient scale is the lowest output at which long-run average cost is at its minimum.

A supermarket chain may buy in bulk and negotiate lower prices from suppliers (a purchasing economy), and a large manufacturer may use specialised machinery (a technical economy), so each loaf or product costs less to produce. Beyond minimum efficient scale, long-run average cost may rise because of diseconomies of scale. As the firm becomes very large, communication may become slower and decisions more complicated, and workers may feel less motivated, so costs per unit could increase. Whether a firm reaches diseconomies may depend on how well it is managed, because firms with good systems might delay them.

Top tips

  • Do not assume costs fall. Economies of scale are possible, but diseconomies are too. Say "may" and "could".
  • Label the LRAC curve: economies of scale on the falling part, MES at the minimum, diseconomies on the rising part.
  • Say which type of economy it is, technical, purchasing, financial, managerial or marketing, and give an example.
