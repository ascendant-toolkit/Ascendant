Government Intervention to Address Market Failure

Maximum and minimum prices, indirect taxes, subsidies, regulation, privatisation, nationalisation, direct provision and quotas. Syllabus 2.10.3.

The big idea

Governments intervene to correct market failure. They can control prices (maximum and minimum prices), change prices and output with indirect taxes and subsidies, or use regulation, privatisation, nationalisation, direct provision and quotas. Each has advantages and disadvantages; the best choice depends on the failure being tackled.

Price controls and taxes (with diagrams)

PolicyDefinitionEffectAdvantagesDisadvantages
Maximum price (price ceiling)A legal limit on how high a price may be, set below equilibriumQuantity demanded > quantity supplied: shortageKeeps essential goods affordable (rent, food)Shortages, queues, black markets, less supply
Minimum price (price floor)A legal limit on how low a price may be, set above equilibriumQuantity supplied > quantity demanded: surplusProtects producers' incomes (farm prices) or discourages demerit goods (minimum price for alcohol)Surpluses, cost to store or buy them, higher prices for consumers
Indirect taxA tax on goods and services, paid by consumers or producers to the governmentSupply shifts left: price up, quantity downReduces demerit goods; raises revenue; internalises external costsRegressive; may be passed to consumers; smuggling; hurts jobs
SubsidyA payment to producers (or consumers) to lower prices and raise outputSupply shifts right: price down, quantity upEncourages merit goods and external benefits; helps new industriesCosts taxpayers; opportunity cost; may be inefficient; hard to remove

Other forms of intervention

PolicyDefinitionAdvantagesDisadvantages
RegulationLaws and rules that firms and consumers must followControls pollution, protects consumers and workers, limits monopolyCosts of compliance and enforcement; red tape; may be ignored
PrivatisationSelling state-owned firms to the private sectorCompetition and profit may raise efficiency; raises revenueLoss of public control; job losses; private monopolies; neglect of unprofitable services
NationalisationThe government takes over private firmsProtects essential services; avoids private monopoly power; considers social benefitPossible inefficiency; losses funded by taxpayers; compensation costs
Direct provisionThe government supplies goods and services itself (public goods, merit goods)Ensures provision of public and merit goods for allHigh tax cost; may be inefficient; no choice
Quotas (e.g. natural resources)A legal limit on the quantity that may be produced or extractedProtects scarce resources (fish, timber) and the environmentHigher prices; illegal extraction; enforcement cost; job losses

Use an example and a judgement: intervention works if the benefits exceed the costs and the policy targets the failure.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Rent controls

Cities such as New York and Berlin have limited rents; the policy helps tenants but can reduce the supply of rental homes.

Exam use: A maximum price.

Minimum alcohol prices

Scotland introduced a minimum price per unit of alcohol to cut harmful drinking.

Exam use: A minimum price.

Tobacco and sugar taxes

Taxes on cigarettes and sugary drinks raise prices and funds.

Exam use: Indirect taxes.

Renewable energy subsidies

Payments for solar and wind helped cut prices and expand use.

Exam use: Subsidies.

Fishing quotas

Limits on catches aim to protect fish stocks.

Exam use: Quotas.

Exam-style questions

Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Q1 Define 2 marks Define a maximum price.
ReminderA legal limit on how high a price may be.
A maximum price is a legal limit on how high a price can be set below the equilibrium price.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain how a maximum price on rent could affect the housing market.
ReminderShortage; black market; less supply; affordable for some.
A maximum price set below equilibrium means quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. There is a shortage of rented housing, so landlords may supply less and a black market may develop. Some tenants benefit from lower rents but others cannot find homes.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse, using a demand and supply diagram, how an indirect tax on cigarettes could affect the market. (Oct/Nov 2023, P22 style)
ReminderS left; price up; quantity down; revenue; inelastic demand.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
An indirect tax raises producers' costs, so supply shifts left from S to S₁. The equilibrium price rises to P₁ and the quantity falls to Q₁. Consumption of cigarettes falls and the government raises tax revenue. Because demand for cigarettes is inelastic, quantity falls only a little.
Q4 Analyse 6 marks Analyse, using a demand and supply diagram, how a subsidy given to producers could affect the market for solar panels. (Feb/Mar 2022, P22 style)
ReminderS right; price down; quantity up; cost to government.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A subsidy lowers producers' costs, so supply shifts right from S to S₁. The price falls to P₁ and the quantity rises to Q₁. Consumers buy more solar panels at a lower price. The cost to taxpayers is an opportunity cost.
Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether a government should nationalise essential industries.
ReminderFor: affordable, social benefit, no monopoly profit; against: inefficiency, cost, compensation. Judgement.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Nationalisation is when the government takes over private firms. It can protect essential services such as water and railways, and stop a private monopoly raising prices. It allows the government to consider social benefits. However, state-run firms may be inefficient because there is no competition or profit motive. Losses are paid by taxpayers and compensation to owners is costly. Overall, nationalisation may suit natural monopolies with big social importance, but regulation or competition may achieve the same aims more cheaply.

Top tips

  • Max price below equilibrium; min price above. Learn which is which.
  • Tax shifts S left; subsidy shifts S right. Draw the shift and label S₁.
  • Always give an advantage and a disadvantage. Then judge.
  • Link each policy to a market failure. Demerit goods, merit goods, public goods.
  • Use examples. Rent control, tobacco tax, solar subsidy.
