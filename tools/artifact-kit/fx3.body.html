Determination of the Foreign Exchange Rate

Floating and fixed exchange rates, demand and supply of a currency, equilibrium, appreciation and depreciation, and the causes of fluctuations. Syllabus 6.3.3.

The big idea

In a floating exchange rate system the rate is set by the demand for and supply of the currency in the foreign exchange market. A rise in the value of a floating currency is an appreciation; a fall is a depreciation. The equilibrium rate is where demand equals supply. Changes in demand or supply, from exports and imports, interest rates and speculation, move the rate. In a fixed system the government or central bank sets the rate.

The foreign exchange market for a currency

On the diagram the vertical axis is the price of the currency (for example dollars per pound) and the horizontal axis is the quantity of pounds traded. Demand slopes down (a cheaper pound makes UK goods cheaper for foreigners, so they buy more pounds). Supply slopes up (a dearer pound makes imports cheaper for UK residents, so they sell more pounds).

Causes of fluctuations

Changes in demand for exports and imports

If foreigners buy more of a country's exports (higher incomes abroad, better quality, lower prices, lower inflation), demand for the currency rises: appreciation. If residents buy more imports (higher incomes at home), supply of the currency rises: depreciation.

Changes in the interest rate

A higher interest rate attracts foreign savers and 'hot money': demand for the currency rises and supply falls, so it appreciates. A lower rate has the opposite effect.

Speculation

If speculators expect a currency to rise they buy it, pushing it up; if they expect it to fall they sell it, pushing it down. Expectations can be self-fulfilling.

Other causes

Lower inflation than other countries (exports more competitive), more foreign investment, remittances, and central bank intervention.

In a fixed system, if demand for the currency falls the central bank must buy its own currency using reserves or raise interest rates, otherwise the rate would fall.

The pound after the Brexit vote

After the June 2016 referendum the pound fell about 10% against the dollar within days, as speculators sold pounds and investors worried about trade and investment.

Exam use: Speculation and expectations.

Hong Kong's peg

Hong Kong has kept its dollar fixed to the US dollar for decades, so its interest rates follow US rates closely.

Exam use: A fixed exchange rate and loss of independent interest rates.

Thailand 1997

Thailand fixed the baht to the dollar but ran short of reserves while speculators sold baht. It was forced to float the baht, which fell sharply, triggering the Asian financial crisis.

Exam use: A failed fixed rate.

Jordan's peg

A past paper notes that Jordan fixes its currency to the US dollar and follows US interest rates, because it wants certainty for trade.

Exam use: Advantages and costs of a fixed rate.

Q1 Define 2 marks What is meant by a floating exchange rate, an appreciation and a depreciation?
ReminderRate set by market forces; rise in value; fall in value.
A floating exchange rate is one determined by the demand for and supply of the currency in the foreign exchange market. An appreciation is a rise in its value; a depreciation is a fall.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain two reasons why a country's foreign exchange rate may depreciate. (May/June 2022, P22)
ReminderIncrease in imports; lower interest rates; speculation; fall in exports; investment abroad.
A rise in imports is one reason. Residents sell more of their currency to buy foreign currency to pay for imports, so supply rises and the value falls. A fall in interest rates is another. Foreign savers and hot money move to countries with higher returns, reducing demand for the currency and increasing supply.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain two causes of an increase in the value of a country's floating foreign exchange rate. (May/June 2023, P22)
ReminderHigher exports, higher interest rates, speculation, foreign investment, lower inflation.
An increase in exports is one cause. Foreign buyers need more of the currency to pay for them, so demand for it rises and its value increases. A higher interest rate is another. It attracts foreign savers and hot money, raising demand for the currency and so its value.
Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain two differences between a floating foreign exchange rate and a fixed foreign exchange rate. (Oct/Nov 2021, P23)
ReminderWho sets it; how often it changes; terms (appreciation/depreciation vs revaluation/devaluation).
A floating exchange rate is determined by market forces, whereas a fixed exchange rate is set by the government or central bank. A floating rate can change every day, whereas a fixed rate does not change often and a rise in value is a revaluation and a fall a devaluation.
Q5 Analyse 6 marks Analyse the possible causes of a fall in a country's foreign exchange rate. (Oct/Nov 2024, P23)
ReminderFixed: government devalues. Floating: lower interest rate, more imports or investment abroad (supply up), fewer exports or remittances (demand down), speculation.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
In a fixed system the central bank may devalue the currency, for example to correct a balance of payments deficit. In a floating system a lower interest rate reduces hot money inflows, lowering demand and raising supply, so the currency depreciates. More imports, or investment abroad by residents, increase the supply of the currency. Fewer exports or lower remittances from workers abroad reduce demand for it. Speculators who expect it to fall sell it, which could make it fall.
Q6 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not a country will benefit from having a fixed foreign exchange rate system. (Oct/Nov 2022, P21)
ReminderFor: certainty, less speculation, discipline. Against: reserves, no control of interest rates, rate may be wrong, speculation.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A fixed exchange rate is set by the government or central bank. It could benefit a country by giving traders and investors certainty about prices, making trade and investment less risky. There may be less speculation, and the government may be forced to follow sensible policies, which could keep inflation low. This is more useful for a small country that trades mostly with one partner. However, defending the rate can use up reserves and if people expect a devaluation, speculation could force one. The country may also lose control of its interest rates, and the fixed rate may be set at the wrong level, hurting exports. Overall, a fixed rate is more likely to benefit a small open economy with large reserves, but a large diverse economy may prefer a floating rate.
Q7 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not a country should switch from a fixed foreign exchange rate system to a floating system. (Oct/Nov 2020, P22)
ReminderFor floating: automatic adjustment, interest rate freedom, no reserves. Against: uncertainty, volatility, imported inflation.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Under a floating system the exchange rate is set by demand and supply. Switching could allow the rate to adjust automatically to trade imbalances, remove the need to hold large reserves, and let the central bank use interest rates to manage the domestic economy. It would also avoid forced devaluations and speculative attacks on the fixed rate. These benefits are larger if the fixed rate has been overvalued. However, a floating rate can be volatile and uncertain for traders and investors, and a sharp fall could raise import prices and inflation. Overall, a switch is likely to benefit a country that cannot defend its fixed rate, but a small country may prefer certainty. It depends on reserves, trade pattern and inflation.

Top tips

  • Floating: market sets it. Fixed: government sets it. Know the vocabulary: appreciation, depreciation, revaluation, devaluation.
  • Draw the currency market. Label the axes: exchange rate and quantity of the currency; shift D or S.
  • Three causes: exports and imports, interest rates, speculation.
  • Higher interest rate = appreciation. Hot money flows in.
  • For fixed rates, mention reserves. The central bank must buy or sell currency.
