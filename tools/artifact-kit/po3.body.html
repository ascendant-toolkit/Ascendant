Policies to Alleviate Poverty and Redistribute Income

Growth, education, healthcare, state benefits, progressive taxation and a national minimum wage: how each reduces poverty and the catches. Syllabus 5.2.3.

The big idea

Governments can use many policies to alleviate poverty and redistribute income: promoting economic growth, improved education, improved healthcare, more generous state benefits, progressive taxation and a national minimum wage (NMW). Redistribution of income is the transfer of income from some people to others, usually by taxing the rich and spending on the poor. Each policy has advantages and costs: say how it raises the incomes or opportunities of the poor, then evaluate.

The six policies

PolicyHow it reduces povertyPossible problems
Economic growthMore jobs and higher incomes; more tax revenue to spend on the poorGains may go to the rich; takes time; may harm the environment
Improved educationHigher skills, productivity and wages; more job opportunities; better health knowledgeCostly; takes years; skills must match jobs; poor families may still not send children to school
Improved healthcareHealthier workers can earn; lower medical bills; less lost incomeCostly; slow results
More generous state benefits (unemployment, pensions, child benefit)Raise incomes of those who cannot work or earn little; reduce absolute poverty at onceCost to taxpayers; may reduce incentive to work; benefits may be too small or poorly targeted
Progressive taxationTakes a larger share from the rich; the revenue pays for benefits and services for the poorMay reduce incentives to work, save and invest; high earners may avoid tax
National minimum wageRaises the pay of the lowest paid; narrows the gapIf above the equilibrium wage, firms may hire fewer workers; does not help those unable to work

Fiscal policy and poverty

Many of these are fiscal policy: government spending on education, healthcare, benefits, subsidised food and housing, financed by taxes. Cutting government spending or relying on regressive taxes (such as VAT) could increase poverty.

Other measures

  • Subsidies or free provision of food, housing, school meals and water.
  • Reducing regressive taxes such as VAT on necessities.
  • Lower interest rates and expansionary policy can create jobs and reduce unemployment.
  • Regional policy and help for the unemployed to find work.

Evaluating

  • Cost and funding: who pays, opportunity cost, deficits.
  • Incentives: high benefits or taxes may discourage work and enterprise; high minimum wages may cost jobs.
  • Time lags: education and growth take years; benefits work at once.
  • Targeting: does the help reach the poor?
  • Absolute versus relative poverty: growth and education help absolute poverty most; progressive taxes, benefits and the NMW help relative poverty.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

The UK's national living wage

The UK introduced a national minimum wage in 1999 and raised it many times. Studies found raising pay for the low-paid with only small effects on employment, though results depend on the level.

Exam use: A minimum wage with a modest effect on jobs.

Cash transfers in Brazil

Brazil's Bolsa Familia paid poor families cash if their children attended school and had health check-ups. Extreme poverty fell and school attendance rose.

Exam use: Benefits linked to education and health, with targeting.

Scandinavian welfare states

Countries such as Denmark and Sweden use progressive taxes and generous benefits and have lower relative poverty than most others, but also high tax rates.

Exam use: Redistribution through tax and benefits and the cost.

Austerity and poverty

After 2010 some European governments cut benefits and public services to reduce deficits. Reports linked this to a rise in food bank use and child poverty.

Exam use: A cut in government spending increasing poverty.

Q1 Define 2 marks Define redistribution of income. (Oct/Nov 2021, P21)
ReminderTransfer of income from some to others; tax the rich and spend on the poor.
Redistribution of income is the transfer of income from some people to others, for example taxing the rich and spending the revenue on the poor.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain two reasons why governments redistribute income. (Oct/Nov 2021, P21)
ReminderReduce poverty; equity; social problems; productivity.
One reason is to reduce poverty. Transferring income to poor households raises their purchasing power so they can afford basic necessities such as food, healthcare and housing. Another reason is to promote equity. Redistribution narrows the gap between rich and poor, which is seen as fairer and may reduce social problems.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain two ways a government could redistribute income. (Oct/Nov 2023, P22)
ReminderBenefits; progressive taxes; free services.
A government could provide state benefits such as unemployment benefit. These raise the income of people with low or no income and help them afford basic necessities. It could use a progressive income tax system. This takes a higher proportion of the income of the rich, and the revenue can be spent to help those on low incomes.
Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain how having a more educated labour force can reduce poverty. (Oct/Nov 2023, P21)
ReminderSkills, productivity, wages; job opportunities; health knowledge.
A more educated labour force is more skilled and productive. Firms are likely to pay higher wages, so workers can afford basic necessities. Better educated workers have more job opportunities and are less likely to be unemployed, so they may earn wages that are higher than unemployment benefits and move above the poverty line.
Q5 Analyse 6 marks Analyse why a cut in government spending might increase poverty. (Oct/Nov 2022, P22)
ReminderCuts to benefits, health, education; job losses in the public sector; lower AD.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A cut in government spending might reduce spending on benefits, healthcare and education. Poor households would receive less income and fewer services, so they could find it harder to afford basic necessities. Cuts to public sector jobs and contracts could raise unemployment, and lower spending reduces aggregate demand, so private firms may also cut jobs. Less education and healthcare reduce skills and health, so people find it harder to escape poverty.
Q6 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not a national minimum wage will reduce poverty. (May/June 2020, P21; Feb/Mar 2022, P22)
ReminderFor: raises pay of low-paid; reduces absolute and relative poverty. Against: set below market wage no effect; unemployment; does not help those unable to work; some receiving it are not poor.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A national minimum wage is a legal lowest rate of pay. If it is set above the equilibrium wage it will raise the pay of the low paid, which could reduce absolute poverty by letting them afford basic necessities and reduce relative poverty by narrowing the gap with higher earners. It may also raise employment if it motivates workers and raises demand. This is most likely if the wage is not too far above the market level. However, if it is set too high, firms' costs rise and they could hire fewer workers, so unemployment may rise and some of the poor lose their income. It does not help those who cannot work, and some people receiving it may not be in poor households. If it is set below the equilibrium wage it has no effect. Overall, a minimum wage is likely to reduce poverty among those in work if set at a sensible level, but it must be combined with other policies for the unemployed and those unable to work.
Q7 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not an increase in unemployment benefit payments would reduce poverty. (May/June 2023, P21)
ReminderFor: raises income at once; helps the unemployed afford necessities. Against: cost, may reduce incentive to look for work, may be small, does not help the low-paid.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Unemployment benefit is a payment by the government to people without work. An increase would raise the incomes of unemployed households straight away, so they could afford more food, housing and healthcare, and the extra spending could support demand. It could reduce absolute poverty among those who have lost their jobs, especially in a recession. The effect is largest where benefits are very low. However, higher benefits cost taxpayers and may require higher taxes or borrowing. If benefits are close to wages, some people may have less incentive to take a job, which could raise unemployment, and benefits do not help the working poor. Overall, higher unemployment benefit is likely to reduce poverty for the unemployed in the short run, but long-term solutions such as education and job creation may be needed.
Q8 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not fiscal policy is effective in reducing poverty. (Oct/Nov 2024, P21)
ReminderFor: spending on benefits, health, education; progressive taxes; subsidies. Against: costs and deficits; incentives; time lags; targeting; other policies (growth, supply-side).
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Fiscal policy is the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy. It can reduce poverty through spending on benefits, education and healthcare, which raise the incomes and skills of the poor, and through progressive taxes that take more from the rich. Subsidies or free provision of food and housing directly help poor households. These measures can act quickly, particularly benefits. However, spending needs funding, which could mean higher taxes or a bigger deficit, and high taxes and benefits could reduce incentives to work and invest. Education and healthcare take years to have an effect, and help may not reach those who most need it. Overall, fiscal policy is likely to be effective, particularly when well targeted, but it works best alongside policies that raise growth and employment, and its effect depends on the size of the measures and how they are funded.

Top tips

  • Six policies: growth, education, healthcare, state benefits, progressive taxation, national minimum wage.
  • Explain the mechanism. 'Education raises skills so wages rise' beats 'education helps'.
  • Absolute or relative? Growth and benefits help absolute poverty; progressive taxes and the NMW help relative poverty.
  • Always evaluate with a cost. Funding, incentives, unemployment, time lags, targeting.
  • Cutting spending can raise poverty. Fiscal policy works both ways.
