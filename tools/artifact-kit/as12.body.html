Economic Methodology: Positive and Normative, Ceteris Paribus and Time

1.2.1 to 1.2.4: economics as a social science, positive and normative statements, ceteris paribus and the short, long and very long run. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Economics is a social science: it studies human behaviour using models and evidence, but it cannot run controlled laboratory experiments, so results are tendencies rather than laws. A positive statement is about facts and can be tested with evidence (it may be true or false), whereas a normative statement is a value judgement or opinion about what ought to be and cannot be settled by facts alone. Ceteris paribus means "all other things being equal": economists change one variable at a time to isolate its effect. The time period matters: in the short run at least one factor is fixed, in the long run all factors can be varied, and in the very long run technology and other conditions can change.

1 · Economics as a social science (1.2.1)

  • Science means building theories, testing them against evidence and revising them. Economics does this with data and models.
  • Social means the subject is human behaviour, which is varied and changes over time, so economists cannot control the conditions as in a laboratory.
  • Economists therefore use models: simplified descriptions of reality that rely on assumptions (such as ceteris paribus and rational behaviour). Models are useful if they predict well, but the assumptions limit them.
  • Results are probabilities and tendencies, not laws. For example, "a rise in price will reduce demand" is usually true but not in every case.

  • A positive statement can be wrong: "A rise in the minimum wage will have no effect on employment" is positive even if the evidence shows it is false. What makes it positive is that it can be tested.
  • A statement that mixes both is normative if it contains a value judgement. To turn a normative statement into a positive one, remove the value word: "the government should cut tax" becomes "cutting tax will raise disposable income".
  • Economists use positive economics to explain and predict, and normative economics to recommend policy. Policy choices combine facts with values, which is why economists disagree.

3 · Ceteris paribus (1.2.3)

  • Ceteris paribus is Latin for "other things being equal". An economist changes one variable and holds all the others constant, so the effect of that one variable can be seen.
  • For example, the demand curve shows how the quantity demanded changes as the price changes, ceteris paribus. Income, tastes, the prices of related goods and expectations are held constant. A change in price causes a movement along the curve; a change in one of the others shifts the whole curve.
  • Without the assumption you cannot tell which variable caused a change. If the price rises 5% and the quantity demanded rises 3% while consumers' incomes rise 4%, the law of demand has not been broken: other things did not remain equal, because the income rise shifted the demand curve.
  • Limitations: in real life other things rarely stay equal, so predictions based on ceteris paribus are approximate.

4 · The importance of the time period (1.2.4)

Time periodMeaningExample
Short runAt least one factor of production is fixed; firms can change only variable factors such as labour and materialsA bakery can hire extra staff but cannot build a new oven
Long runAll factors of production can be varied, but the state of technology is fixedThe bakery can build a bigger factory and buy more machines
Very long runAll factors can be varied and technology and other conditions can changeNew production methods and automation are developed
  • Time matters because elasticities and responses differ: the price elasticity of supply is higher in the long run, so a price rise brings a bigger increase in the quantity supplied. Demand also tends to be more elastic in the long run as consumers find substitutes.
  • Policies and shocks have different effects in the short and long run: a tax rise may cut demand a little at first and more later.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Minimum wage debates

Economists can test whether a minimum wage reduces employment by studying data from different regions. That is positive economics. Whether the minimum wage should be raised depends on how much weight people give to the incomes of low-paid workers against possible job losses, which is normative.

Exam use: the difference between testing a claim and making a policy judgement.

Why fuel prices respond slowly

When the price of oil rises sharply, producers cannot increase output much in the short run because wells and refineries take years to build. In the long run, new fields and methods increase supply a lot, so the price effect fades.

Exam use: the importance of the time period and how elasticities differ.

Ice cream and the weather

An economist who studies the effect of a price change on ice cream sales has to hold the weather, incomes and the price of other desserts constant. If a hot summer lifts demand while the price rises, the observed sales do not show the effect of the price alone.

Exam use: ceteris paribus and why real-world data can be misleading.

Forecasts that miss

Central banks and governments publish economic forecasts based on models with many assumptions. When an unexpected event such as a pandemic occurs, the forecasts can be far off. This is a reminder that economics is a social science with probabilities, not exact laws.

Exam use: economics as a social science and the limits of models.

Practice question · modelled on the 8 mark style (no past paper) 8 marks Explain what economists mean by ceteris paribus and consider whether the assumption makes economic models useful.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (3): ceteris paribus means all other things being equal; one variable is changed while the others are held constant; an example such as the demand curve.
  • Analysis (3): it isolates the effect of one variable; a movement along a curve is caused by a price change and a shift by a change in a variable that was held constant; without the assumption cause and effect cannot be separated.
  • Evaluation (2): in reality other things rarely stay equal, so predictions are approximate; models are still useful as a starting point; reach a conclusion.
PlanDefine with an example. Why economists use it. The limit: real life changes many things at once. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Ceteris paribus means all other things being equal. For example, when drawing a demand curve an economist changes the price and holds income, tastes and the prices of other goods constant, so that the effect of price on quantity demanded is isolated. This makes models useful because it lets economists see cause and effect. A change in the price causes a movement along the demand curve, whereas a change in income, which was held constant, causes the whole curve to shift. Without the assumption it would be impossible to say which variable caused a change in demand.

However, in the real world other things rarely stay equal. If the price of a good rises while incomes also rise, the quantity demanded may rise, so a model based on ceteris paribus could mislead. Models are therefore useful as a starting point, but predictions are approximate and the assumption needs to be checked against the data.

Top tips

  • Ask 'can it be tested?' If data could show it is true or false it is positive. If it depends on opinion it is normative.
  • Spot the value words. Should, ought, fair, better, too high: normative.
  • Positive can be wrong. A false prediction is still a positive statement.
  • Define ceteris paribus with an example. All other things equal, with demand as the example.
  • Say what is held constant. Income, tastes, prices of related goods and expectations.
  • Give the three time periods. Short run: one fixed factor. Long run: all factors variable. Very long run: technology can change.
  • Link time to elasticity. Supply and demand are usually more elastic in the long run.
