Consumer and Producer Surplus

2.5.1 to 2.5.2: the meaning and significance of consumer surplus and producer surplus, shown on supply and demand diagrams. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Consumer surplus is the difference between the price consumers are willing to pay and the price they actually pay, shown on a diagram as the triangle below the demand curve and above the price. Producer surplus is the difference between the price producers receive and the minimum price they would be willing to accept, shown as the triangle above the supply curve and below the price. At the equilibrium price both are at work and the total surplus (economic welfare) is greatest, which is why economists see a competitive market as allocatively efficient. A change in price, or a policy that moves the market away from equilibrium, shifts surplus between consumers and producers and may reduce the total.

1 · Consumer surplus (2.5.1)

  • The demand curve shows the maximum price each consumer is willing to pay for each unit. If the market price is lower, every buyer except the last pays less than they were willing to.
  • Consumer surplus (CS) = willingness to pay − price paid. On a straight-line demand curve: CS = ½ × quantity × (highest price on the demand curve − market price).
  • Significance: it measures the benefit (welfare) consumers gain from a market beyond what they pay. A fall in price raises CS; a rise lowers it.

2 · Producer surplus (2.5.2)

  • The supply curve shows the minimum price producers will accept for each unit (roughly the marginal cost).
  • Producer surplus (PS) = price received − minimum acceptable price. On a straight-line supply curve: PS = ½ × quantity × (market price − price where supply begins).
  • Significance: it measures the benefit to producers, a bit like economic profit. A rise in price raises PS; a fall lowers it.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A bargain at the shops

A shopper would pay up to $80 for a coat and finds it on sale for $50. The $30 difference is that shopper's consumer surplus. Summed across every buyer, it measures the benefit consumers get from the market.

Exam use: willingness to pay minus price paid.

A farmer at harvest

A farmer would accept as little as $3 per kilo to grow tomatoes but can sell them for $5. The $2 gap is producer surplus on that unit. Over the whole crop it shows how much better off the farmer is for being able to sell at the market price.

Exam use: price received minus willingness to accept.

Concert ticket prices

If a promoter sets the ticket price too low, many fans get surplus, but not everyone can buy and the promoter earns less. If it is set too high, fewer people go and total surplus falls. The best level for total surplus is where demand equals supply.

Exam use: total surplus is highest at equilibrium.

Cheaper flights

As airfares have fallen over time, passengers who would have paid more now pay less, so consumer surplus has risen for millions of travellers, even before counting new people who can now afford to fly.

Exam use: a fall in price raising consumer surplus.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/22 · Q2(a) 8 marks With the help of a diagram, explain what is meant by consumer surplus and producer surplus and consider whether a rise in the price of a product because of higher costs of production is likely to always reduce the consumer surplus.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): consumer surplus is the difference between the price a consumer is willing to pay and the market price (1); producer surplus is the difference between the price a producer is willing to accept and what is actually paid (1); both shown on an accurately labelled diagram (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): a diagram showing the supply curve shifting left (1), explaining and showing the change in consumer surplus for a product with elastic demand (1) compared with one with inelastic demand (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): consumer surplus will always fall (1), but the extent of the fall depends on the value of the price elasticity of demand (1).

Consumer surplus is the gap between the price consumers are willing to pay and the price they actually pay, the area under the demand curve above the price. Producer surplus is the gap between the price producers receive and the lowest price they would accept, the area above the supply curve below the price.

A rise in the costs of production shifts the supply curve to the left. The equilibrium price rises and quantity falls, so the consumer surplus triangle becomes smaller. If demand is price elastic, quantity falls a lot and consumer surplus falls by more. If demand is inelastic, quantity falls less but the price rise is larger, so the loss is still substantial.

Consumer surplus is likely to fall in either case, since the price rises and quantity falls. But the extent of the fall depends on the price elasticity of demand, so it is a matter of degree and not whether it falls.

On a demand and supply diagram, the initial consumer surplus is the triangle between the demand curve and the equilibrium price. A rise in production costs shifts the supply curve to the left, raising the price and reducing quantity. The new consumer surplus triangle is smaller, because the gap between what consumers are willing to pay and the market price has fallen, so consumer surplus will fall. The size of the fall depends on how price elastic demand for olive oil is: if buyers have good substitutes, quantity falls more and the price rises less.

Top tips

  • Know both definitions exactly. CS: willing to pay minus price. PS: price minus willing to accept.
  • Name the areas. CS above the price, below demand. PS below the price, above supply.
  • Draw and label. Axes, D, S, equilibrium, price line, shaded areas.
  • Price rise lowers CS. And raises PS (for the same quantity).
  • Say total surplus is largest at equilibrium. Away from it, some surplus is lost.
  • Don't confuse with revenue or profit. Surplus is a gain beyond what is paid or the minimum accepted.
  • Mention elasticity. It decides how big the change is.
