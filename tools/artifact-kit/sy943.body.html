The Quantity Theory of Money (MV = PT)

9.4.3: the equation MV = PT, the monetarist assumptions about velocity and transactions, and an evaluation of the theory. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

The quantity theory of money is built on the identity MV = PT: the money supply (M) times the velocity of circulation (V) equals the price level (P) times the volume of transactions (T). If V is stable and T is fixed at full employment, a rise in M leads to a proportional rise in P, so inflation is caused by growth of the money supply. This is the monetarist view and it supports controlling the money supply to control inflation. Critics say V is unstable, T can change when there is spare capacity, causation may run from prices to money, and the money supply is hard to define and control.

1 · The equation

MV = PT
SymbolMeaning
MThe money supply: the stock of money in the economy
VThe velocity of circulation: the average number of times a unit of money is used in transactions in a period
PThe average price level
TThe number (volume) of transactions of goods and services in the period, often taken as real output (the Fisher form)

MV = PT is an identity: both sides measure total spending, so it is true by definition. It becomes a theory when assumptions are added about V and T.

2 · The monetarist argument

  • V is stable or changes predictably in the short run, because it depends on institutions and habits (how often people are paid, the use of credit cards and cash machines).
  • T is fixed in the long run at the level determined by real factors: the economy tends to full employment and output is determined by supply.
  • Therefore a change in M changes only P. To raise prices 5% a year more than output grows, the money supply must grow more than output.
  • Policy conclusion: to control inflation, control the growth of the money supply (set a rule or target).
% change in P ≈ % change in M + % change in V − % change in T

3 · What affects velocity?

  • Frequency of income payments: weekly pay means people hold smaller balances, so V is higher than with monthly pay.
  • Interest rates: higher rates give a reward for holding less money, so V rises.
  • Payment technology: credit and debit cards, online banking and cash machines let people hold smaller balances, raising V.
  • Expectations: if people expect prices to rise, they spend money quickly, raising V.
  • Confidence and uncertainty: in a crisis people hold on to money and V falls.

4 · Evaluation

CriticismExplanation
V is not stableFinancial innovation, changes in interest rates and in confidence move V. After 2008 V fell sharply, so large increases in M (quantitative easing) did not raise prices much.
T is not fixedWith spare capacity (a negative output gap) extra money can raise output and employment rather than prices (the Keynesian view).
CausationThe money supply may be endogenous: it responds to demand for loans and rising prices, rather than causing them.
Defining and controlling MWhich measure of the money supply? Banks create deposits by lending, and the central bank cannot control them precisely.
Time lagsThe effect of money on prices is long and variable.
Other causes of inflationCost-push inflation (oil prices, wages) is not due to the money supply, though it may need monetary accommodation to continue.
Strong conclusionThe theory may hold in the long run, in high-inflation episodes (hyperinflation is almost always accompanied by very rapid money growth), but it is a poor guide in the short run, when V and T are changing, especially in a recession.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Weimar Germany, 1923

The government financed its spending by printing money. The money supply rose by many times a month and prices rose even faster, as V also rose because people spent money as soon as they got it.

Exam use: the strongest evidence for the quantity theory: very fast money growth and hyperinflation. Add that V rose too.

Quantitative easing after 2008

Central banks created trillions of dollars of reserves, but inflation stayed low for a decade. Banks held reserves instead of lending them, V fell, and the economy had spare capacity.

Exam use: a counter-example for the theory: V is not stable and T can rise when there is spare capacity.

Monetary targeting in the 1980s

The US and UK tried to control inflation by targeting the growth of the money supply. They found it hard to control and unstable relationships between money and prices, and moved to targeting interest rates and then inflation directly.

Exam use: the practical difficulty of defining and controlling M and unstable velocity.

Digital payments and velocity

As contactless cards and mobile payments spread, people hold less cash and can move money faster. This tends to raise V and means that a given money supply supports more spending.

Exam use: a factor that changes velocity and a reason V is not constant.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/41 · Q5(a) 12 marks Explain the quantity theory of money and discuss why the theory might not work in practice.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explain MV = PT and the assumptions about V and T and the conclusion that inflation is a monetary phenomenon.
  • Why it might not work: velocity changes with payment technology, interest rates and expectations; with spare capacity extra money raises output not prices; the money supply is endogenous and hard to control; cost-push inflation.
  • Credit evidence: QE after 2008 raised M without much inflation; hyperinflation episodes support the link.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/43 · Q4 20 marks 'Central banks can control the money supply. An increase in the money supply will cause inflation. Therefore central banks can control inflation.' Evaluate this statement.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (14): how a central bank can influence the money supply (open market operations, interest rates, reserve requirements, QE); the quantity theory MV = PT and the link from M to P; link to inflation targets; other causes of inflation.
  • Evaluation (6): commercial banks create most money, so control of M is incomplete; velocity is unstable; with spare capacity more money raises output; cost-push and imported inflation are not controlled by M; time lags and expectations; central banks usually target interest rates and use M as an indicator.
PlanTwo links in the argument: can the bank control M, and does M cause P? Test each. Then other causes. Conclude: partly.
Based on Feb/March 2020 · 9708/42 · Q6 25 marks 'Monetarists insist that control of the money supply is the key to solving inflation, Keynesians argue that inflation can only be controlled by controlling expenditure.' To what extent do you agree that both approaches are only partially correct?
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explain the monetarist argument (quantity theory, V and T stable, rules for money) and the Keynesian argument (inflation from excess aggregate demand, controlled by fiscal and monetary policy aimed at expenditure; cost-push causes).
  • Show that the approaches overlap: controlling expenditure and controlling the money supply are linked through MV, and both only address demand-side inflation.
  • Both are partial: cost-push inflation and expectations need other policies (supply-side, exchange rate, incomes policy); the best answer depends on the cause and the state of the economy.
PlanTwo views. Overlap. What each misses (cost-push, expectations). Conclude: partial.

B · Multiple choice questions (Paper 3)

Choose an answer, then read the reason. Reworded from the paper and question number shown.

Top tips

  • Write the equation and define every letter. MV = PT with M, V, P and T.
  • State the assumptions. V stable and T fixed at full employment. Without them it is only an identity.
  • Give the conclusion. Inflation is caused by excess growth of the money supply, so control M.
  • Criticise with three points. Unstable velocity, T changes with spare capacity, causation or control of M.
  • Use evidence. Hyperinflation supports it, QE after 2008 weakens it.
  • Distinguish short run and long run. The theory is more convincing in the long run.
  • Use 'could' and 'may'. These are theories about tendencies, not certainties.
