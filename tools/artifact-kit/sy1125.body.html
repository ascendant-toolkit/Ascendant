Marshall-Lerner and the J Curve

11.2.5: the effect of a change in the exchange rate on the current account, the Marshall-Lerner condition and the J curve. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

A fall in the exchange rate makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home, but whether the current account improves depends on how much the quantities change. The Marshall-Lerner condition says a depreciation or devaluation will improve the current account only if the sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports is greater than 1. Because buyers and sellers are slow to react (contracts, habits, lack of substitutes), elasticities are low in the short run, so the current account often gets worse before it gets better: the J curve. A rise in the exchange rate has the opposite effects.

1 · What a depreciation does

  • Price effect: each export is cheaper in foreign currency, so foreigners buy more. Each import costs more in home currency, so residents buy fewer.
  • Volume effect: the extra export sales and the cut in imports add to the demand for domestic goods, raising aggregate demand and employment. A net exports (X − M) increase raises AD.
  • The trade balance rises only if the gain in volume outweighs the higher price paid for imports. This is what the elasticities decide.
  • Other effects: higher imported inflation (dearer raw materials and goods), a higher burden on debts in foreign currency, and possible retaliation (competitive devaluation).

2 · The Marshall-Lerner condition

  • PEDx + PEDm > 1 (taking the elasticities as positive numbers). Then a depreciation or devaluation improves the current account.
  • If PEDx + PEDm = 1 the balance is unchanged. If it is less than 1 the current account worsens: the extra amount paid for imports is greater than the gain in export sales.
  • Worked example (trade initially balanced at $100m each way, depreciation of 10%): if PEDx = 0.8 and PEDm = 0.6 (sum 1.4), exports rise by 8% to $108m and import spending is 100 × 1.10 × 0.94 = $103.4m, so the balance improves by about $4.6m. If PEDx = 0.3 and PEDm = 0.2 (sum 0.5), exports rise to $103m and imports cost $107.8m, so the balance worsens by about $4.8m.
  • Elasticity is high when there are good substitutes, when the product is a luxury, when spare capacity lets exporters respond, and when more time has passed. It is low for essential imports such as oil or food and for primary commodity exports with inelastic supply.

3 · The J curve

  • Short run: contracts are already agreed, buyers are slow to notice price changes, and firms need time to find new suppliers or expand capacity. Short-run PEDs are low (sum below 1), so the dearer import bill outweighs any gain: the current account worsens.
  • Medium to long run: contracts end, consumers switch and exporters expand output. PEDs rise (sum above 1), so the current account improves and eventually moves above its starting point.
  • The graph of the current account balance against time then looks like the letter J. A revaluation may produce an inverse J (the account first improves, then worsens).

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Sterling after 2016

After the pound fell in 2016, the UK trade deficit did not narrow quickly. Many importers and exporters were tied to contracts and supply chains, and imported components became dearer. This is often used as an example of a slow, drawn-out J curve.

Exam use: short-run elasticities being low, and why the current account may take time to improve.

Japan's weak yen after 2013

Japan's yen fell sharply in 2013. Exporters' profits rose when converted into yen, but the volume of exports rose less than hoped because many firms already produced abroad. Import costs for energy and food rose, and the trade balance first worsened.

Exam use: an example of the volume effect being limited, and imported inflation as a cost of depreciation.

Commodity exporters

Many low-income countries export primary products such as coffee, copper or cocoa. Supply in the short run is inelastic because it takes time to plant or open mines, so a depreciation may not raise export volumes much, even if the price in foreign currency falls.

Exam use: the supply side limit on a depreciation in a low-income economy, which is the link in the Paper 4 question on growth.

Oil importers

Countries that rely on imported oil have a very inelastic demand for imports, at least in the short run. When their currency falls, the oil bill rises sharply in local currency, which can worsen the trade balance and add to inflation.

Exam use: very low PEDm, so the Marshall-Lerner condition may fail.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/42 · Q4 20 marks Consider the extent to which the depreciation of its foreign exchange rate contributes to the economic growth of a low-income country.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge and analysis of the effect of a depreciation on export and import prices, and so on the demand for and supply of exports and imports.
  • Link to AD through net exports (X − M), shown with an AD and AS diagram or the multiplier; state whether the growth is actual or potential.
  • Evaluation: the ability of supply capacity in the low-income country to respond; primary product exports often have inelastic supply compared with, say, oil; short-run employment gains but also inflation from dearer imports; the effect on debt, which rises in cost if borrowing is in foreign currency but falls in cost if in domestic currency.
PlanPrice effects. Net exports raise AD (diagram). Actual growth. Supply constraints. Inflation. Debt in foreign currency.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A depreciation lowers the price of a low-income country's exports in foreign currency and raises the price of its imports. If the Marshall-Lerner condition holds, export revenue could rise by more than the import bill, so net exports increase. Because X − M is part of aggregate demand, AD may shift to the right, raising output through the multiplier. This is actual growth, as it uses spare capacity.

However, many low-income countries export primary products with inelastic supply in the short run, so export volumes may rise little. Dearer imported inputs and capital goods could also raise costs and inflation, and reduce potential growth. If much of the country's debt is in dollars, the cost of repayments in local currency rises. So the contribution to growth may be modest and temporary.

Practice question · modelled on the 12 mark style (no past paper) 12 marks Explain, with the help of a diagram, the J curve effect and why a depreciation may make the current account worse in the short run.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Diagram: current account balance against time, falling after the depreciation and then rising above its starting point.
  • Short run: price elasticities of demand for exports and imports are low (sum below 1) because of existing contracts, slow reactions and lack of substitutes, so the dearer import bill outweighs the extra export sales.
  • Long run: elasticities rise (sum above 1), consumers switch and exporters expand, so the account improves (Marshall-Lerner).
  • Mention the condition and that the speed depends on the economy.
PlanDiagram. Short-run elasticity and why. Long-run improvement. Marshall-Lerner condition.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The J curve shows the current account getting worse for a time after a depreciation before it improves, so the graph of the balance over time looks like the letter J. In the short run, contracts have already been agreed, consumers are slow to change their habits and there may be no easy substitutes, so demand for imports and exports is price inelastic. The dearer import bill is then greater than the extra revenue from exports, so the balance worsens.

Over time, contracts end, consumers switch to cheaper domestic goods and exporters expand output, so elasticities rise. When the sum of the elasticities is above 1, the Marshall-Lerner condition is met and the current account may improve, eventually above its starting level. How long this takes may depend on the economy's spare capacity and import dependence.

Practice question · modelled on the 8 mark style (no past paper) 8 marks Explain the Marshall-Lerner condition and why it might not hold in a low-income country.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Condition: a depreciation improves the current account if PEDx + PEDm > 1.
  • Reason it may fail: inelastic demand for essential imports (oil, food, capital goods); inelastic supply of commodity exports; limited spare capacity; low-quality exports; short time period.
  • A developed chain of analysis.
PlanCondition. Reasons. A conclusion about the likelihood.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The Marshall-Lerner condition states that a depreciation or devaluation will improve the current account only if the sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports is greater than 1.

In a low-income country the condition may not hold. Imports such as oil, food and machinery are essential, so demand could be inelastic, and the import bill will rise sharply when the currency falls. Exports are often primary products with inelastic supply in the short run, so export volumes may rise little. The condition is more likely to be met in the long run, when more substitutes and capacity are available.

Top tips

  • State the condition fully. PEDx + PEDm greater than 1 for a depreciation to improve the current account.
  • Say which direction. Depreciation: exports cheaper, imports dearer. Appreciation is the opposite.
  • Label the J. Current account balance on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal axis, a dip then a rise above the start.
  • Short run versus long run. Elasticities are low in the short run and higher in the long run. That is the whole reason for the J.
  • Link net exports to AD. X minus M is part of aggregate demand, so a rise shifts AD right.
  • Mention costs. Imported inflation, dearer foreign debt and possible retaliation.
  • Evaluate for the economy. Low-income countries often have inelastic commodity exports and import needs.
