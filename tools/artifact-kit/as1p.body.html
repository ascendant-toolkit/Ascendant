The Planned Economy

1.4.3: how a planned economy allocates resources, its strengths and weaknesses, and the effects of moving to a market economy. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and past paper questions.

This part in five sentences

In a planned (command) economy the government, through a central planning authority, decides what to produce, how to produce it and for whom, and the factors of production and most firms are owned by the state. Planners set output targets, allocate resources between industries and often set prices and wages, usually aiming to provide essentials for everyone and a more equal distribution of output. The system can provide public and merit goods, avoid duplication and direct resources to long-term goals. But without price signals planners lack the information to match output to preferences, so there are shortages and surpluses, and weak incentives for workers and managers lead to low efficiency, little innovation and limited consumer choice. Few economies are fully planned; some have moved towards markets, and the effects of such a transition include both gains in efficiency and costs such as unemployment and inequality.

1 · Features of a planned economy

  • State ownership of land, capital and most firms; there is little private enterprise.
  • Central planning: a government authority sets output targets for industries and firms, often in multi-year plans.
  • Allocation by instruction, not price: inputs are directed to firms, and prices and wages may be set by the state.
  • Aims: equity (a fairer distribution), full employment, provision of essentials, and rapid development of chosen industries.

3 · Strengths of a planned economy

  • Public and merit goods such as health and education are provided to everyone, regardless of income.
  • Greater equality in the distribution of income and goods.
  • Long-term planning: resources can be directed to large, long-term projects that markets might neglect.
  • Reduced unemployment in principle, since the state can direct people to jobs.
  • No wasteful competition or duplication in some industries; no monopoly profits for private owners.

4 · Weaknesses of a planned economy

  • Information problem: without prices to signal scarcity and preferences, planners cannot know how much of each good to produce, so there are shortages (queues) of some goods and surpluses of others.
  • Weak incentives: no profit motive and little reward for effort, so managers and workers have little reason to be efficient or to innovate.
  • Limited consumer choice and often poor quality, since consumers cannot signal what they want.
  • Bureaucracy and slow decisions: a large administration is costly and slow to respond.
  • Possible lack of economic freedom and black markets where goods are scarce.

5 · Changing the system

  • Transition from planned to market (as in the former Soviet bloc and in China's reforms): prices are freed, firms are privatised, competition and private enterprise grow.
  • Possible benefits: more efficiency, innovation and choice; higher growth in the long run.
  • Possible costs: rising prices, structural unemployment as state firms close, widening inequality, and under-provision of public and merit goods.
  • Whether the change is beneficial depends on how quickly it is done, how well institutions work and who gains and loses. Many economies move towards a mixed system.
Exam tipDo not write that a planned economy has "no" markets. Say who makes the main decisions and what the consequences are. The key weakness is the lack of price signals, which leads to shortages and surpluses, and the lack of an incentive.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Central planning and queues

In the former Soviet Union, state planners set output targets and prices. Goods such as bread were cheap, but some goods were always in short supply and others were unwanted. Queues and black markets were common.

Exam use: shortages and surpluses without price signals.

Output targets and quality

When a factory is judged on the quantity it produces, managers may care less about quality or about what consumers want. Goods may be made but not used.

Exam use: weak incentives and targets.

Moving towards a market

China and several former Soviet bloc countries freed prices, allowed private firms and opened to trade. Output and incomes grew, but state firms closed, causing unemployment, and inequality widened.

Exam use: the benefits and costs of transition.

Free provision of essentials

A planned system may offer housing, health and education to everyone at low or zero prices. This reduces inequality, but supply can be limited and quality may suffer without funding or incentives.

Exam use: the strength of equity, and its limits.

North Korea and Cuba

These are often cited as examples of economies where the state plays an extremely large role in allocating resources. Even they are not purely planned, because informal markets exist.

Exam use: no real economy is purely planned.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/23 · Q2(a) 8 marks Explain how the fundamental economic problem is addressed in a planned economy and in a market economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): the fundamental economic problem is scarce resources (1) but unlimited wants (1).
  • Application to a planned economy (up to 3): central planning and government allocation of resources, perhaps with an emphasis on welfare.
  • Application to a market economy (up to 3): the price mechanism, with rationing, signalling and the interaction of demand and supply.
PlanScarcity. Planned: government decides what, how and for whom. Market: price mechanism and profit.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The fundamental economic problem is that resources are scarce but wants are unlimited, so choices must be made about what, how and for whom to produce.

In a planned economy the government decides. A central authority sets output targets, directs resources to chosen industries and decides how output is shared, often aiming for a fairer distribution.

In a market economy the price mechanism decides. Prices ration scarce goods to those willing and able to pay, signal consumer preferences to firms and give firms an incentive to supply what is profitable, so demand and supply allocate resources.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/21 · Q2(b) 12 marks Assess whether a planned economic system should always switch to a mixed economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): advantages and disadvantages of a planned system, including its emphasis on welfare and the provision of public and merit goods, but the lack of competition may mean inefficient allocation.
  • Advantages and disadvantages of a mixed economy: it may contain the best of both systems, but this depends on how mixed it is; the ease of the switch can also be assessed.
  • Balanced analysis reaches the top level; one-sided answers are limited. Evaluation (up to 4): a valid conclusion.
PlanPlanned: pros and cons. Mixed: pros and cons. Judge: depends on the starting point and the speed of the switch.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A planned economy has strengths: the state can provide public and merit goods for everyone, aim for equity and direct resources to long-term goals. Its weaknesses are the lack of price signals and competition, which can cause shortages, inefficiency and limited choice. A mixed economy keeps the efficiency and choice of markets, while the government provides public goods and corrects market failure. Its disadvantages are possible government failure, and that the outcome depends on how the balance is set.

A switch is probably beneficial if the planned economy suffers from inefficiency and shortages, but it may bring unemployment and inequality in the short run, and the benefit depends on how quickly it is done. So a planned economy should not always switch, but a move towards mixed is likely to help where the planning system is failing.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/21 · Q2(b) 12 marks Discuss whether the advantages of a transition from a planned economy to a market economy always outweigh the disadvantages.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): advantages of a market economy: the price mechanism, prices as signals of consumer preferences, private ownership and decision-making with little government intervention, competition leading to efficiency.
  • Disadvantages: some products under-provided and under-consumed (merit goods), some over-provided (demerit goods), some not provided at all (public goods), and further consequences of the transition.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): judgement on whether the advantages always outweigh the disadvantages; reserve 1 mark for a conclusion.
PlanAdvantages (prices, competition). Disadvantages (market failure, unemployment, inequality). Judge: depends on speed and institutions.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A transition to a market economy introduces a price mechanism that signals what consumers want. Private ownership and competition give firms an incentive to be efficient and to innovate, and resources can move quickly to where they are most valued.

But a market economy may under-provide merit goods, over-provide demerit goods and fail to provide public goods. In the transition, state firms may close and cause structural unemployment, prices may rise and inequality may widen.

The advantages do not always outweigh the disadvantages. Gains in efficiency may take years, while the costs fall quickly on workers and the poor. A gradual change with strong institutions and a government that still provides public and merit goods is likely to give the best result.

Top tips

  • Say who decides. The government or a central planning authority.
  • Name the key weakness. No price signals: shortages and surpluses.
  • Mention incentives. No profit motive, so little innovation.
  • Give the strengths. Equity, public and merit goods, long-term planning.
  • Avoid 'no markets'. Informal markets exist.
  • Cover transition. Efficiency gains versus unemployment and inequality.
  • Judge. Depends on the speed and the institutions.
