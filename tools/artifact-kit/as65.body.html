Causes and Consequences of Current Account Imbalances

6.3.3 to 6.3.4: why deficits and surpluses arise and what they may mean for the domestic and external economy. Animated chains, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

A current account deficit means payments to the rest of the world for goods, services, income and transfers exceed receipts; a surplus means the opposite. Deficits may be caused by strong domestic demand and rising incomes (which pull in imports), poor price or non-price competitiveness (high costs, inflation, low productivity, low quality), a high exchange rate, a deterioration in the terms of trade, weak world demand for a country's exports, and net outflows of income such as profits sent abroad; surpluses are the opposite, often with low domestic demand or high saving. A persistent deficit could lead to downward pressure on the exchange rate, falling reserves or rising borrowing, lower AD, output and jobs, and pressure for contractionary policy; a surplus could raise AD and jobs but may also reflect weak spending and harm trading partners. How serious either is depends on size, cause, how long it lasts and how it is financed.

2 · Consequences

  • Deficit, domestic: net exports lower AD, which could reduce output and employment; imports may raise living standards in the short run; if the deficit is persistent, contractionary policy may be needed to reduce it.
  • Deficit, external: a floating currency tends to depreciate; under a fixed rate the reserves fall; the country may need to borrow or sell assets, so more future income goes abroad as interest and profits.
  • Surplus, domestic: exports and AD may rise, with more jobs and growth; but a surplus can reflect weak domestic demand, and resources are used to produce for others rather than for domestic living standards.
  • Surplus, external: upward pressure on the exchange rate, which may erode competitiveness; partners' deficits may rise and lead to trade tension.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A consumer boom

Rising incomes and cheap credit lead households to buy more imported cars and electronics. Imports grow faster than exports, and the current account moves into deficit.

Exam use: strong demand pulling in imports.

A commodity exporter

A country selling mainly oil sees export income fall when oil prices drop. Its terms of trade worsen and the current account could move into deficit.

Exam use: a fall in commodity prices.

A manufacturing exporter

A country with high saving, strong exports and subdued consumer spending runs a surplus year after year. Its partners may complain, and its currency may be under upward pressure.

Exam use: a persistent surplus and its external effects.

Profits sent abroad

A country can have a surplus in goods but a current account deficit if foreign firms send large profits and interest abroad. This is a deficit on primary income.

Exam use: primary income outflows.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/23 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain why some countries consistently have a deficit on the current account of the balance of payments.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): the current account is trade in goods, services plus other income (1); a deficit is where payments made by a country exceed payments received, or where income from exports is exceeded by expenditure on imports (1).
  • Application (up to 6): explanation of causes of a current account deficit (up to 3), such as an appreciation of the exchange rate, a reduced supply of primary goods for export, a deterioration in the terms of trade, reduced aid, reduced transfers from migrant workers and increased protection from trading partners; and why some countries may experience a persistent deficit (up to 3).
PlanDefine the current account and a deficit. Causes: high exchange rate, falling terms of trade, weak exports. Persistence: structural weaknesses such as low productivity.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The current account measures trade in goods and services together with primary and secondary income. A deficit means that payments made by the country exceed payments received, so spending on imports and other outflows is greater than income from exports.

A country may have a persistent deficit if its exchange rate is too high, making exports dear and imports cheap, or if its terms of trade have deteriorated, so that export earnings buy fewer imports. A reliance on a few primary exports that earn little, or low productivity and poor quality that reduce competitiveness, could make the problem structural. Trade barriers imposed by trading partners, and profits sent abroad by foreign-owned firms, could add to it.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/23 · Q5(b) 12 marks A country has a persistent deficit on the current account of its balance of payments. Assess the likely consequences for the domestic and external economy of this country.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): what a persistent deficit means; the domestic consequences, such as employment, growth, inflation, living standards, the government budget, business confidence and a need to restructure the economy; and the external consequences, such as the exchange rate, ability to trade, need for imports and debt interest. Maximum Level 2 if only the domestic or only the external economy is considered.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): clearly considers the likely consequences for both parts of the economy and reaches a conclusion on the most likely consequences. A one-sided response cannot gain evaluation marks.
PlanDefine persistent deficit. Domestic: lower AD, output and jobs; contractionary policy. External: depreciation, debt, reserves. Judge by size and financing.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A persistent deficit means imports and outflows of income exceed exports and inflows year after year. Domestically, net exports are negative, so AD and output could be lower, with weaker employment and growth, and the government may feel pressure to adopt contractionary policy. On the other hand, imports may raise living standards in the short run. Externally, a floating currency could depreciate, raising import prices and inflation, while under a fixed rate the reserves may fall. The country may need to borrow or sell assets, so more income could flow abroad as interest and profits.

The consequences are likely to be more serious the larger and longer the deficit, and the weaker the way it is financed. A deficit financed by long-term investment into productive capacity may be less damaging than one financed by short-term borrowing. Overall the likely consequences are slower growth and external pressure, but they could be limited if the deficit is small.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/21 · Q5(b) 12 marks Assess whether an economy will always benefit from having a surplus on the current account of its balance of payments.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the potential benefits, such as surplus foreign exchange to invest abroad, more jobs in the export sector, more domestic purchasing and employment, and possible growth (up to 4); the potential disadvantages, such as weak domestic demand and lower employment, lower growth, a surplus caused by recession, and lower output and employment in deficit countries (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): considers both the benefits and disadvantages, the size of the surplus and its possible causes, and concludes whether an economy will always benefit. A one-sided response cannot gain evaluation marks.
PlanBenefits: exchange earnings, export jobs, growth. Disadvantages: may reflect weak demand or recession; harm to partners. Judge by size and cause.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A surplus could give a country foreign exchange to invest abroad, and high exports may create jobs in the export sector and support growth. If imports are low because people buy domestic goods, employment at home could rise too. But a surplus could also be a sign of weak domestic demand, with low consumer spending reducing employment and growth, or even of a recession that cuts imports. It could also lower output and jobs in countries with deficits.

An economy will not always benefit. A surplus caused by strong competitiveness is likely to be good, while one caused by weak demand may not be. The size of the surplus and the reason for it are likely to matter most.

Top tips

  • Say which part. Goods, services, primary or secondary income.
  • Cause then effect. State the cause, then what it does to X and M.
  • Explain persistence. Structural: competitiveness, productivity, terms of trade.
  • Split the consequences. Domestic and external.
  • Both sides for a surplus. Not always good: weak demand, partners hurt.
  • Use size and cause. A small deficit funded by investment is less serious.
  • Hedge. Use may, could, likely.
