The Multiplier

How an initial change in injections becomes a larger change in national income, the formulae with and without taxes and imports, what changes its size and its limits. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

The multiplier is the number of times an initial change in injections is multiplied into a larger change in national income: ΔY = k × ΔJ. Each round of spending becomes someone else's income, but part of it leaks out as saving, taxes and imports, so the rounds get smaller. The size of the multiplier is k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) in a simple closed economy, and more generally k = 1 ÷ MPW, where the marginal propensity to withdraw is MPS + MRT + MPM. The smaller the leakages, the larger the multiplier, but it only gives real output gains when there is spare capacity.

1 · The multiplier process

Suppose investment rises by $100m and households spend 60% of any extra income (MPC = 0.6). Firms and workers who earn the $100m spend $60m. That $60m becomes income for others, who spend $36m, and so on.

3 · What makes the multiplier bigger or smaller

Larger multiplier

  • High marginal propensity to consume
  • Low marginal propensity to save
  • Low marginal rate of taxation
  • Low marginal propensity to import

Smaller multiplier

  • High marginal propensity to save
  • Higher tax rates (and benefits that are withdrawn)
  • High marginal propensity to import: spending leaks abroad
  • Households and firms that save windfalls

4 · Evaluating the multiplier

  • Spare capacity. The multiplier assumes unemployed resources and fixed prices. Near full employment extra spending mostly raises prices.
  • Time lags. Each round takes time, so the full effect may take a year or more.
  • Crowding out. Extra government spending financed by borrowing may raise interest rates and reduce private investment.
  • Changes in the leakages. The MPC, MRT and MPM may not be constant as income changes.
  • Direction. The multiplier works in reverse: a fall in injections reduces income by a multiple.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A new factory in a small town

Building and running a factory pays wages and buys local supplies. Workers spend on food, rent and services, and those businesses pay their own staff. A town where most spending stays local has a larger multiplier than one where people buy mainly from outside.

Exam use: the multiplier effect of extra employment, as in the Ghana oil question.

Stimulus payments

When a government sends cash to households in a recession, the effect depends on how much is spent. If most is saved, MPS is high, the multiplier is low and the rise in income is small.

Exam use: why the size of the MPC matters for fiscal policy.

A small, open economy

In an economy that imports much of what it consumes, a large share of extra income leaks abroad. The marginal propensity to import is high and the multiplier is small.

Exam use: how openness reduces the multiplier.

What the mark scheme wants
  • Two marks for understanding: the multiplier is the total rise in national income or output that follows an initial rise in injections into the circular flow.
  • Two marks for application: the article identifies new jobs and incomes in the extraction industry, and these create a further round of spending, jobs and income in other sectors.
  • To stay in the same band, use the case: workers in the oil sector spend on local goods and services, so other firms produce more and hire more.

B · Practice question with a model answer

Practice (not a past paper question) 8 marks Explain why a rise in government spending of $100m might raise national income by more than $100m.
PlanDefine the multiplier. Walk through two rounds. Give the formula. Then one evaluative point on leakages or spare capacity.
KANE

The multiplier is the ratio of the change in national income to the initial change in injections. The multiplier is given by 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) in a closed economy with no government, or more generally 1 ÷ MPW, where MPW is the marginal propensity to withdraw (MPS + MRT + MPM).

Government spending of $100m becomes income for the firms and workers who provide the goods. If they spend 60% of any extra income (MPC = 0.6), $60m is spent on other firms' output, which becomes their income, and they spend $36m, and so on. The rounds get smaller because some income leaks out as saving, taxes and imports, but the total is $100m × 1 ÷ 0.4 = $250m, which is more than the original $100m.

The multiplier could be smaller in practice if households save much of the extra income, if the economy imports a lot, or if higher taxes follow. It also assumes spare capacity: near full employment the extra spending may raise prices more than output.

Top tips

  • Write the formula and show working: k = 1 ÷ MPS (closed, no government) or 1 ÷ (MPS + MRT + MPM). Then ΔY = k × ΔJ.
  • Check which propensities the question gives. MPC alone is only enough in the closed, no-government model. Add MRT and MPM for the full leakage.
  • Say why the rounds shrink: part of each round leaks out as saving, taxes and imports.
  • Remember it works in reverse. A fall in injections reduces income by a multiple.
  • Evaluate with spare capacity, time lags, crowding out and changing propensities.
  • Link the multiplier to policy: a larger multiplier means a smaller injection is needed to close a gap in output.
