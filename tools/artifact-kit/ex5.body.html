AS Level Economics · 9708 · Exam technique

Paper 2 Data Response and Essays: Technique and Practice

How Paper 2 is built, a 120 minute plan, what each part is marked for, the structure of an 8 mark and a 12 mark answer, command and scope words, and one real paper worked part by part.

This part in five sentences

Paper 2 lasts 2 hours, carries 60 marks and has three sections, each worth 20 marks: Section A is one compulsory data response question (Question 1, parts (a) to (e), on an unseen extract), Section B asks you to answer one microeconomics essay question from two, and Section C asks you to answer one macroeconomics and international question from two; each essay question has a part (a) worth 8 marks and a part (b) worth 12 marks. That is 2 minutes per mark, and the marks are split between knowledge (AO1), analysis (AO2) and evaluation (AO3) in different proportions in each part, so the skill is to give each part exactly what it is marked for and no more. The short data parts reward precise, data-based answers with no padding; the 8 mark part rewards a definition or diagram, a developed chain and a short judgement; and the 12 mark part rewards balanced, developed analysis and a justified evaluation. Most marks lost are for answering a different question from the one asked (the command word or scope word), vague answers that could be about any economy, and poor use of time.

4 · Section A: the data response

  • Skim the extract first: what is the story (inflation falling, a recession fear)? Underline the figures. Note the units (%, $bn, index).
  • Use the data: mark schemes for (d) and (e) say the explanation should be "correctly applied to the data". Quote the figures: "from 0.25% in January 2022 to 4.5% in December 2022".
  • (a) is not a description: a comparison needs both series and a link: "the inflation rate rose and then fell while the interest rate rose continuously".
  • Do not over-write (a) and (b): two marks do not need a paragraph. Spend the time on (d) and (e).
  • For 4 and 6 mark parts, keep the evaluation short but specific: one reason the effect might be small or large, and a conclusion on the extent.

5 · Essay part (a): 8 marks in about 13 minutes

The question has two halves: explain something, and consider a limit, extent or alternative. The marks follow the same split: AO1 3, AO2 3, AO3 2.

ParagraphWhat it containsMarksSize
1 · KnowledgeThe definition or formula the question needs, and the diagram if it says "with the help of a diagram" (labelled, with the shift)AO1: up to 32 to 4 lines
2 · AnalysisA developed chain from cause to effect, referring to the diagram, applied to the caseAO2: up to 35 to 7 lines
3 · ConsiderA condition that affects the result (elasticity, spare capacity, time), and a short conclusion in the question's wordsAO3: up to 23 to 4 lines
  • If the question says two causes, two methods, one demand-side and one supply-side, give exactly that, labelled.
  • If it says "with the help of a formula" or "a diagram", that is an AO1 mark: do not omit it.
  • Do not write a 12 mark answer for an 8 mark part: the extra length earns nothing.

6 · Essay part (b): 12 marks in about 22 minutes

ParagraphWhat it containsSize
Opener (optional)One definition only if the question turns on a term, or one sentence naming the scope. Do not restate the question.1 to 2 lines
1 · Analysis: the case forThe strongest argument as a developed chain with a diagram where possible, applied to the good or economy6 to 8 lines
2 · Analysis: the case against or an alternativeA second developed chain: a limit, a cost or an alternative policy6 to 8 lines
3 · EvaluationTwo conditions (elasticity, time, spare capacity, context, trade-off), each as Condition, If, Then, then weigh one against the other8 to 10 lines
4 · ConclusionA direct answer in the question's words, the main reason, and the condition that would change it. No new content.2 to 3 lines

If you are short of time: drop the opener, keep paragraphs 1 and 3 in full, shorten paragraph 2, but never drop the conclusion: it is where much of the evaluation credit sits.

8 · Diagrams, calculations and application

  • Diagrams: draw one clear, large diagram, label axes and curves, show the shift and the new equilibrium, then refer to it in the text. Place it next to the paragraph it supports.
  • Calculations: write the formula, show working and give the unit; use the extract's figures.
  • Application: use the named good, the country in the extract, or an economy you know. A sentence that could be about any market is generic.
  • Time between parts: if you run out of time, finish with a brief conclusion rather than leaving (b) unfinished.

9 · Common ways to lose marks

  • Not answering the question (NAQ): writing about the topic, ignoring the command word or the scope word.
  • Vague answers: "the economy suffers", "this could affect things". Name the mechanism.
  • Writing too much for (a) and (b) in Section A, and too little for (d) and (e).
  • Writing a long (a) and a short (b): (b) is worth more.
  • One-sided essays: capped at Level 2, with no evaluation marks.
  • No conclusion, or a conclusion that does not use the question's words.
  • Unlabelled diagrams or diagrams not referred to.
  • Ignoring the data in the extract.
Exam tipBefore writing each part, write in the margin: marks, command word, scope word. Then plan the number of paragraphs the marks justify (2 marks: 2 sentences; 6 marks: two chains and a short evaluation; 12 marks: four paragraphs).

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Data part: an explicit comparison

In a question asking for a comparison of the inflation rate and the interest rate from a table, the mark scheme said marks go to an explicit comparison, not to a description of the changes in each month: one series rose then fell, the other rose continuously, and the interest rate rose proportionately faster.

Exam use: linking both series in the answer.

Data part: use the figures

A 6 mark question on interest rate rises said the explanation should be applied to the data: rates rose from 0.25% to 4.5% over the year. Quoting those figures is how the answer is 'applied'.

Exam use: using the extract.

An 8 mark part with two halves

'Explain how the circular flow changes from a closed to an open economy and consider what determines the extent of the change.' The explanation earns knowledge and analysis marks; the 'consider' half earns the evaluation marks.

Exam use: writing both halves.

Part (b) and the word 'always'

'Assess whether expansionary fiscal policy always benefits an economy.' The word always asks for a case where it does not: when there is no spare capacity, or when debt is already high.

Exam use: the scope word.

Choosing the essay

Part (b) is worth 12 of the 20 marks in an essay question, so choosing a question because part (a) looks easy is a risk. Read both parts of both questions before choosing, and check the scope words in each part (b).

Exam use: choosing by the whole question.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q1(a) 2 marks Data response: using a table of the inflation rate and the interest rate in the US in three months, compare the changes in the inflation rate and the changes in the interest rate.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Marks are for an explicit comparison of the changes in the two series, not for a description of each in turn: the inflation rate rose and then fell while the interest rate rose continuously (1); the proportionate changes in the interest rate were always greater than those in the inflation rate (1).
PlanOne sentence comparing the pattern, one using figures and proportions.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The inflation rate rose and then fell, while the interest rate rose continuously. The proportionate rises in the interest rate were always greater than the changes in the inflation rate.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q1(b) 2 marks Explain one reason why using the Consumer Price Index (CPI) to measure the inflation rate in an economy may not produce an accurate result.
What the mark scheme wants
  • The CPI is based on the average basket of products bought by all households (1), but as buying patterns change the basket used may no longer be typical (1).
  • OR the weights and products are usually updated every two or three years to reflect changes in buying patterns (1), but this may not be frequent enough if there is rapid change in the products bought or the proportion of income spent on them (1).
PlanBasket or weights out of date as buying patterns change.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The CPI uses a fixed basket of goods meant to represent what a typical household buys. As buying patterns change, for example with new products, the basket becomes out of date and may no longer be typical of all households, so the measured inflation rate may be inaccurate.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q1(c) 4 marks In an attempt to bring about disinflation, a central bank used a contractionary monetary policy. Consider whether disinflation is more harmful than deflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 3): disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation (1), so although the problems of inflation are less they remain, such as the effect on the distribution of income, purchasing power, the competitiveness of exports, menu costs, shoe leather costs and investment planning (1); deflation is a fall in the general price level (1), which can bring problems such as a fall in AD leading to a recession, lower output and higher unemployment, and negative effects on investment (1). A maximum of 2 if only one of the two is considered.
  • Evaluation (up to 1 or 2): for example, the extent of the disinflation, that is, by how much the rate is falling.
PlanDisinflation: prices still rise, smaller problems. Deflation: delayed spending, recession, debt. Judge: deflation is usually more harmful, unless disinflation is deep.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation, so prices still rise and the problems of inflation, such as menu costs and redistribution, remain though they are smaller. Deflation is a fall in the general price level, which can make consumers delay spending, causing a fall in AD, lower output and higher unemployment, and raising the real burden of debt. Deflation is generally more harmful because it can cause a spiral of falling output, whereas disinflation lowers the cost of inflation, unless the fall in inflation is so large that it risks pushing the economy into deflation.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q1(d) 6 marks Assess whether increases in the interest rate make a recession in an economy inevitable. (The extract showed rates rising from 0.25% in January to 4.5% in December.)
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 4): inevitable: higher interest rates make borrowing more expensive for consumers and firms, so there is a fall in both AD and AS, leading to higher unemployment and a recession (up to 3); not inevitable: demand and supply may not be interest elastic and there are other factors that can influence AD and AS (up to 3).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): relevant evaluation which considers both the likelihood of recession and the possibility of there not being one, and weighs up the factors (1); reserve 1 mark for a conclusion on whether the increases make a recession inevitable (1).
PlanInevitable: dearer borrowing, C and I fall, AD left (and costs rise, AS). Not inevitable: interest-inelastic demand, other factors. Weigh; conclude.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

With the interest rate raised from 0.25% to 4.5%, borrowing is dearer for households and firms, so consumption and investment fall, AD shifts left, and rising costs may also reduce AS. Output falls and unemployment rises, so a recession is likely. But a recession is not inevitable: if households have fixed-rate loans or high savings, demand may not respond much to the interest rate, and other factors such as government spending or exports may offset the fall. A recession is therefore likely but not inevitable. The outcome depends on how sensitive demand is to interest rates and what else is happening to AD, so increases in the rate make a recession more likely rather than certain.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q1(e) 6 marks Assess whether a central bank setting an inflation target as part of its monetary policy is likely to be helpful for its economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 4): possibly helpful, because it helps to create and maintain stability and confidence at a time of significant price increases, informing consumers and firms that action will be taken to stop inflation becoming too high, and it can influence inflation expectations; if these stay stable, it may help to prevent a wage-price spiral (up to 3); possibly unhelpful, because the bank may not be willing to take the measures needed to keep inflation at the target, knowing there is a delicate balance between holding inflation close to a target and the risk of an economic downturn (up to 3).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): relevant evaluation, for example in relation to the particular target adopted, and a reserved mark for a conclusion on whether it is helpful.
PlanHelpful: confidence, expectations, no wage-price spiral. Unhelpful: bank may not act; target may risk a downturn. Judge: credibility decides.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

An inflation target could help by giving consumers and firms confidence that the central bank will act to stop prices rising too quickly, which keeps inflation expectations stable and may prevent a wage-price spiral. But the target may not help if the bank is reluctant to raise interest rates enough, because that could cause a slowdown and higher unemployment.

The target is likely to be helpful if the central bank is credible and independent, and if the target level is realistic. Its benefit depends on whether the bank is willing to meet it at the cost of lower growth.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q3(a) 8 marks With the help of a diagram, explain how changes in a subsidy can influence the price and quantity sold of a product in a market and consider how expenditure on a subsidy is affected by the price elasticity of demand for the product.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): a correct diagram with labelled axes, demand and supply (1); an increase in a subsidy shifting supply to the right, lowering price and raising quantity, with new equilibrium positions shown (1); a decrease in a subsidy shifting supply to the left, raising price and lowering quantity (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): a subsidy is a sum of money paid by a government to a producer (1); an increase lowers the price charged to consumers and raises quantity (1) and the explanation of how that affects the cost to the government.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): the cost of a subsidy depends on price elasticity of demand: the more elastic, the more quantity rises and the greater the expenditure, with a justified conclusion.
PlanDiagram: subsidy shifts supply right, price down, quantity up. Cost = subsidy × quantity. Elastic demand: quantity rises more, so cost is higher.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A subsidy is a payment by the government to producers per unit of output. On a diagram it shifts the supply curve to the right, by the amount of the subsidy, lowering the equilibrium price and increasing the quantity sold. A bigger subsidy shifts supply further, and removing it shifts supply back to the left, raising price and cutting quantity.

Because the subsidy lowers firms' costs, they supply more at each price, and the price consumers pay falls. The government pays the subsidy on every unit sold, so the cost is the subsidy per unit multiplied by the quantity sold after the subsidy.

The cost depends on the price elasticity of demand. If demand is elastic, the fall in price brings a large rise in quantity, so the total spent on the subsidy is larger. If demand is inelastic, quantity rises less, so the subsidy costs less but also does less to raise consumption.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain how the circular flow of income in an economy changes when that economy moves from a closed to an open economy and consider what determines the extent of this change.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): the way income flows around an economy from firms to households and back to firms (1); payments to factors of production in the form of income, profit and rent (1); payments from all these sources are spent on goods and services (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): closed economy injections of I and G and withdrawals of S and T; open economy adds spending on exports as an injection and spending on imports as a withdrawal. Maximum 2 if only a closed or only an open economy is considered, with no evaluation marks.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): consideration of what determines the extent of the change, such as the size of trade relative to GDP, with a conclusion.
PlanFlow of income and spending. Closed: I, G, S, T. Open: add X and M. Extent depends on the size of the trade sector.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The circular flow shows income paid by firms to households for factors of production, spent again on goods and services, in a continuous process.

In a closed economy, injections are investment and government spending, and withdrawals are saving and taxes. In an open economy, exports are added to injections and imports to withdrawals, so international trade now affects national income.

The size of the change depends on how open the economy is, measured by trade as a share of GDP, and on the balance between exports and imports. Economies with large trade sectors change more than economies that mainly trade internally.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q5(a) 8 marks Explain the difference between a budget surplus and a budget deficit and consider the extent to which a budget surplus is better than a budget deficit.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): understanding of a government budget (1), a budget surplus (1) and a budget deficit (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): the benefits and limitations of a budget surplus (up to 2) and of a budget deficit (up to 2). If only a surplus or only a deficit is considered the maximum is 2, with no evaluation marks. The question is not about the current account of the balance of payments.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a valid judgement on the extent to which a surplus is better than a deficit (1) and a conclusion (1).
PlanBudget: revenue and spending. Surplus: revenue greater. Deficit: spending greater. Weigh benefits and limits of each. Judge: depends on the economy.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The government budget shows planned revenue and spending. A budget surplus is when revenue exceeds spending, and a budget deficit is when spending exceeds revenue, which has to be financed by borrowing.

A surplus allows the government to repay debt and reduces interest payments, but it takes demand out of the economy and may mean cuts to services or higher taxes. A deficit can boost AD and support output and jobs, especially in a recession, and can finance investment, but it adds to the national debt and interest costs, and may crowd out private investment.

A surplus is not always better. In a boom a surplus can help control inflation and reduce debt, but in a recession a deficit is probably more appropriate to support demand. The right position depends on the stage of the economic cycle.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess whether the potential benefits of free trade always outweigh the arguments for protectionism.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the potential benefits of free trade, such as an increase in world output, more efficient allocation through specialisation, lower costs, wider choice, economic growth and improved living standards (up to 4); the arguments for protectionism, such as the infant industry argument, declining or sunset industries, strategic industries, a current account deficit and anti-dumping (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): balanced consideration of alternatives, explaining which is stronger in particular circumstances, with a conclusion.
PlanFree trade: output, efficiency, cost, choice, growth. Protection: infant, sunset, strategic, deficit, dumping. Judge: benefits may usually outweigh, but not always.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Free trade could raise world output because countries specialise where they have a comparative advantage. Resources are allocated more efficiently, costs may fall and consumers may enjoy a wider choice and higher living standards. But infant industries may need temporary protection to grow, sunset industries may need time to adjust, strategic industries may matter for security and a deficit could be reduced by cutting imports. Anti-dumping measures may stop unfair competition.

The benefits of free trade may often outweigh the case for protection, especially over a long time, but not always. Protection can be justified when it is temporary, targeted and unlikely to provoke retaliation, such as support for a genuinely strategic industry.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q5(b) 12 marks Assess whether expansionary fiscal policy always benefits an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the benefits, such as a rise in AD, real output and employment, and a fall in cyclical unemployment, with a diagram (up to 4); the costs and limits, such as a higher budget deficit and national debt, inflation near full capacity, crowding out, imports and time lags (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether expansionary policy always benefits the economy, depending on spare capacity, the size and type of spending and the level of debt, with a conclusion.
PlanBenefits: AD, output, jobs. Costs: deficit, debt, inflation, crowding out, time lags. Judge: depends on spare capacity and debt.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Expansionary fiscal policy, through higher spending or lower taxes, raises AD, which increases real output and reduces cyclical unemployment, especially in a recession with spare capacity. But it increases the budget deficit and the national debt, and if the economy is near full capacity the rise in AD mainly raises prices. Higher borrowing may crowd out private investment by raising interest rates, some of the extra demand leaks abroad as imports, and time lags may mean the policy takes effect too late.

The policy does not always benefit an economy. It is most beneficial in a deep recession with spare capacity and a manageable debt, and least beneficial when the economy is close to full employment or debt is already high.

B · Multiple choice questions (Paper 1)

Choose an answer, then read the reason. Reworded from the paper and question number shown.

Top tips

  • Write marks, command word and scope word first. In the margin before each part.
  • 2 minutes a mark. But spend less on 1(a) and 1(b), more on (d) and (e).
  • Use the data. Quote figures in Section A.
  • (a): knowledge, chain, judgement. Three paragraphs for 8 marks.
  • (b): analysis, analysis, evaluation, conclusion. Four paragraphs for 12 marks.
  • Always conclude in the question's words. Even if short of time.
  • Choose the essay by both parts. Read (a) and (b) of both questions.
