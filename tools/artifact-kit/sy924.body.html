Policies to Promote Economic Growth and Their Effectiveness

9.2.4: demand-side and supply-side policies for growth, how each works on AD and AS, and how to judge their effectiveness. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Policies to promote growth fall into two groups. Demand-side policies (fiscal, monetary, exchange rate) raise aggregate demand and aim at actual growth by using spare capacity. Supply-side policies raise the quantity, quality or productivity of factors of production and aim at potential growth, shifting the LRAS to the right. Their effectiveness depends on the size of the output gap, time lags, cost and borrowing, crowding out, inflation, the balance of payments and how people and firms respond.

1 · Demand-side policies: actual growth

PolicyHow it raises growthLimits
Expansionary fiscal policy (more G, lower T)AD shifts right; the multiplier magnifies the rise in income, output and jobsBudget deficit and debt, crowding out, time lags, may be inflationary near full capacity, leakages to imports
Expansionary monetary policy (lower interest rates, QE)Cheaper borrowing raises consumption and investment, and a weaker currency raises exportsWeak effect if confidence is low or in a liquidity trap, long lags, inflation risk, asset bubbles
Exchange rate depreciationExports cheaper, imports dearer, so net exports (X − M) riseImported inflation, retaliation, depends on elasticities (Marshall-Lerner)
The key conditionDemand-side policy works well when there is a negative output gap: spare capacity lets output rise without much inflation. Near full capacity (on the vertical LRAS) the same policy mainly raises prices.

2 · Supply-side policies: potential growth

Market-based

  • Privatisation and deregulation: profit motive and competition may raise efficiency.
  • Lower income tax and benefits: more incentive to work and invest.
  • Labour market reform: weaker union power, more flexible hiring.
  • Competition policy and free trade: pressure to cut costs and innovate.

Interventionist

  • Education, training and health: better human capital.
  • Infrastructure: transport, energy and broadband cut firms' costs.
  • R&D and investment subsidies, tax breaks.
  • Attracting FDI and managed migration: more capital and labour, technology transfer.
What supply-side policy does on the diagramIt shifts LRAS right (and SRAS), giving higher potential output and a lower price level. It is slow, but it can raise growth without adding inflation. Evaluate on lags, cost to the government, equity (tax cuts and weaker unions may widen inequality) and environment.

3 · How to judge effectiveness

  • Spare capacity: is there a negative gap, or is the economy near full capacity?
  • Time lags: fiscal and monetary effects take months; education and infrastructure take years.
  • Size of effect: multiplier, interest rate sensitivity, the productivity gain from an investment (its marginal efficiency).
  • Costs and side effects: deficits, debt, crowding out, inflation, current account deficit, inequality, environmental damage.
  • Constraints: political resistance (for example from unions), global conditions, shortages of skilled labour or finance.

Different schools disagree. Keynesians emphasise demand and active fiscal policy. Monetarists and new classical economists argue that markets reach full employment in the long run, so growth comes mainly from the supply side and stable prices.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Government infrastructure programmes

Spending on roads, railways, ports and power networks is an injection that raises demand straight away and, once built, lowers firms' costs and widens markets. Large projects often run over budget and take years before the benefits appear.

Exam use: a policy with both actual and potential effects. Say the demand effect is quick and the supply effect slow, and that the benefit depends on how well the money is spent.

Education and training

Better schools and apprenticeship systems raise skills, and skilled workers can earn more and produce more. Countries that invested heavily in education, such as South Korea after the 1960s, grew quickly over decades.

Exam use: a classic supply-side example. Evaluate with time lags (10 to 20 years), cost and whether training matches the skills firms need.

Tax cuts and deregulation in the 1980s

Several governments cut top tax rates and privatised state industries, aiming to raise incentives and efficiency. Productivity rose in some sectors, but inequality also widened and some privatised firms became private monopolies.

Exam use: market-based supply-side policy. Give both sides: efficiency gains and equity or monopoly concerns.

Attracting multinational investment

Countries such as Vietnam and Ireland have used tax incentives and stable policy to attract foreign firms. This can bring capital, technology and jobs, but profits flow abroad and the country can become dependent on a few firms.

Exam use: FDI as a supply-side policy. Link to potential growth and add the outflow of profits and dependence as evaluation.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/44 · Q5 20 marks Evaluate how a country could raise its potential economic growth.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge and analysis (14): define potential growth and show it on LRAS or a PPC. Identify and analyse at least two supply-side policies (privatisation, education and training, labour market reform, infrastructure, R&D, migration, FDI, competition policy) and explain how each raises long-run output.
  • Evaluation (6): cost and borrowing limits, political constraints, shortages of labour or resources, time lags, environmental damage, monopoly or exploitation risks after privatisation.
PlanDefine and draw LRAS shift. Two policies with chain of reasoning. Then evaluate with cost, lags and side effects. Conclude on which matters most.
Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/41 and 43 · Q4 20 marks Many governments see higher public spending as a cause of growth, and argue that it is therefore sensible to spend more to raise growth. To what extent do you agree?
What the mark scheme wants
  • Distinguish actual from potential growth. Higher spending raises AD, helped by the multiplier, so output and investment may rise.
  • It works best when there is spare capacity. Near full capacity extra spending raises prices or imports and harms the balance of payments.
  • Long-run growth also depends on capital, skills, workforce, technology and access to finance, which spending alone does not supply.
  • Evaluation: quality of spending (investment versus consumption), borrowing and debt, inequality, environmental costs, a judgement on which side is stronger.
PlanSplit short run (actual) from long run (potential). Spare capacity condition. Quality of spending. Conclude with 'it depends'.
Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/41 · Q7(a) 12 marks Explain what is meant by infrastructure and consider the view that more investment in infrastructure will raise both actual and potential economic growth.
What the mark scheme wants
  • A clear explanation of infrastructure with examples: roads, rail, ports, power, broadband, schools and hospitals.
  • Distinguish actual from potential growth, ideally with a PPC or AD and AS diagram.
  • Actual growth: spending on construction raises AD, output and jobs in the short run. Potential growth: better transport and energy lower costs and raise productivity, shifting LRAS right.
  • A conclusion that both are affected but the supply-side effect is slower and depends on project quality.
PlanDefine, then short-run demand effect, long-run supply effect, then a conclusion about timing.
Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/42 · Q7(b) 13 marks 'Policies to achieve economic growth will inevitably cause a government budget deficit.' How far do you agree?
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explain a budget deficit and the policies: fiscal (G up or T down raises the deficit), monetary (lower interest rates need not), supply-side (education spending adds to expenditure but tax cuts may raise revenue later).
  • Link policy to the type of growth: demand policies give actual growth in the short run, supply-side policies give potential growth in the long run.
  • Evaluation: growth itself raises tax receipts and can shrink the deficit; some policies (deregulation, trade liberalisation, monetary easing) need no extra spending.
PlanTwo policies, each linked to a deficit or not. Growth feeding tax revenue. Judge: 'not inevitably'.
Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/42 · Q5 20 marks In the decade to 2020 very low interest rates encouraged low-income countries to borrow from foreign lenders to fund long-term growth. Evaluate this approach to promoting long-term growth.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Long-term growth needs more or better inputs (capital, labour, technology). Low-income countries have low savings, so funds may come from foreign governments, agencies (IMF, World Bank) or multinationals.
  • Borrowed funds for infrastructure and capital raise AS and AD. The investment must earn a return above the interest rate (marginal efficiency of capital).
  • Evaluation: when interest rates rise, debt service costs rise, the currency may fall and the balance of payments worsens; projects may fail or be badly chosen; borrowing in foreign currency; alternative sources of funds (FDI, aid, domestic saving).
PlanShow why funds are needed. How borrowing becomes growth. Then rising rates, exchange rate and debt risk. Judge on the quality of the projects.
Based on Feb/March 2021 · 9708/42 · Q7(a) 12 marks 'Economic growth should be a government's most important macroeconomic aim.' Discuss.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Define growth and state the other macro aims: price stability, low unemployment, a sustainable balance of payments (and often equity and the environment).
  • Benefits of growth: higher incomes, jobs, tax revenue, funds for public services. Costs: inflation, current account deficit, inequality, pollution, resource depletion.
  • The aims are linked, so pursuing one affects the others. Level 4 needs a conclusion on how far the statement holds.
PlanDefine growth. Case for. Case against, with trade-offs. Linked aims. Judgement.

B · Multiple choice questions (Paper 3)

Choose an answer, then read the reason. Reworded from the paper and question number shown.

Top tips

  • Say which type of growth each policy affects. Demand-side for actual growth, supply-side for potential growth. Examiners reward the distinction.
  • Draw AD and AS. AD shifts for demand-side policy. LRAS and SRAS shift for supply-side policy. Label the equilibrium before and after.
  • Always check spare capacity. A demand boost helps most with a negative output gap. Near full capacity it mainly raises prices.
  • Give the chain, not a list. For each policy: what changes, how it affects factors of production or AD, what happens to output.
  • Use time lags in evaluation. Education and infrastructure take years. Interest rate changes take months.
  • Name the costs. Deficits and debt, crowding out, inflation, current account deficit, inequality, environmental damage.
  • Finish with a conclusion. Say which policy is most effective for this economy and why: it depends on the size of the gap and on the time horizon.
