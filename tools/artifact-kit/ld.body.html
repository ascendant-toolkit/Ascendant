The Demand for Labour

Why a firm hires the workers it does. Marginal physical product, marginal revenue product (MRP), the hiring rule, what shifts the demand for labour, and how responsive that demand is to the wage. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions, with mark scheme points.

This part in four sentences

Firms want workers for the output they help to sell, so the demand for labour is a derived demand. The extra revenue from one more worker is the marginal revenue product (MRP = MPP × MR), and it falls as more workers are hired because of diminishing marginal returns. A profit-maximising firm hires up to the point where MRP = the marginal cost of labour (the wage, in a competitive labour market), so the MRP curve is the firm's demand curve for labour. Anything that changes MPP or MR shifts the demand; a change in the wage is a movement along it.

This is part 1 of syllabus 8.3. Part 2 covers the supply of labour and wage differentials, part 3 economic rent, part 4 monopsony and trade unions, and part 5 the minimum wage.

1 · From output to revenue: MPP and MRP

Marginal physical product (MPP)

The extra output from employing one more worker. It falls as more workers are added to fixed capital: diminishing marginal returns.

Marginal revenue product (MRP)

The extra revenue from employing one more worker.

MRP = MPP × MR

If the firm is a price taker in the product market, MR = price, so MRP = MPP × P.

Example: product price $2. Each extra worker adds less output than the one before, so the MRP falls.

2 · The hiring rule

Each worker adds MRP to revenue and costs the wage. The firm keeps hiring while MRP is above the wage, and stops where they are equal. With a wage of $20 a day the firm above hires 4 workers (the 4th adds $24, the 5th only $18).

Hire up to the point where MRP = marginal cost of labour (the wage in a competitive labour market)
Why this is the demand curveThe downward sloping MRP curve tells you how many workers the firm wants at each wage, so MRP is the firm's demand for labour. Adding up all firms gives the market demand.

3 · Derived demand

Nobody wants a worker for their own sake. The firm wants the output they help make. If the demand for the product rises, MR and MRP rise and the demand for labour shifts right. If product demand falls, so does the demand for labour. This is why demand for labour is a derived demand.

5 · The wage elasticity of demand for labour

Wage elasticity of demand = % change in quantity of labour demanded ÷ % change in the wage

Demand is likely to be more elastic when:

  • labour is a large share of total costs, so a wage rise changes costs a lot;
  • there are good substitutes for labour (such as machines) that can be introduced easily;
  • the demand for the product is elastic, so higher costs passed on in prices cut sales and jobs;
  • the time period is long, so firms can change their methods of production.

The elasticity matters later: the less elastic the demand for labour, the smaller the job losses from a higher wage, whether it comes from a union or a minimum wage (parts 4 and 5).

6 · Perfect and imperfect competition

  • Perfectly competitive labour market: the firm is a wage taker, the supply of labour to the firm is horizontal at the market wage, and it hires where MRP = wage. A firm that raised its wage above the market rate would no longer maximise profit.
  • Imperfectly competitive labour market (monopsony): the firm faces an upward sloping supply and hires where MRP = MCL. See part 4.
  • Assumptions of MRP theory: firms maximise profit, workers are identical, firms know the MRP of workers, and diminishing returns. In practice MRP is hard to measure (for example for teachers or nurses), and managers may not maximise profit.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting figures.

A café in the school holidays

When customers increase, each extra waiter or barista helps sell more drinks, so MRP rises and the café may take on more staff. When trade drops in the quiet season, MRP falls and fewer staff are needed. Demand for the workers is derived from demand for coffee.

Exam use: a clear example of derived demand and of the MRP curve shifting with product demand.

Training and new equipment

A firm that trains its warehouse staff to use scanners handles more orders per worker, so MPP and MRP rise. The demand for those workers shifts right, which could support a higher wage and more jobs.

Exam use: a productivity rise as a shift in demand. The May/June 2025 20-mark essay asked about a rise in labour productivity.

Robots in car factories

When machines become cheaper, firms may use more of them and fewer workers. The demand for labour may become more elastic over time as automation is introduced.

Exam use: substitutes for labour and the time period in the elasticity of demand for labour.

The switch to electric vehicles

As car makers move from petrol to electric vehicles, demand for workers who make combustion engines falls. Some may retrain, but until they do there may be structural unemployment in the areas that built the old engines.

Exam use: the May/June 2024 data response question: a fall in demand for labour shown on a diagram, with falling wages and a need for retraining.

Low-paid care work

It is hard to measure the extra revenue from one more care worker, because the output is a service whose value is not always sold in a market. This makes MRP theory harder to apply, which is a standard evaluation point.

Exam use: a limitation of MRP theory: it is difficult to measure productivity in services such as health and education.

May/June 2025 · 9708/42 · Q3 20 marks With the help of a diagram, assess whether the impact of an increase in labour productivity on the wages and employment of a firm is likely to be greater in a perfectly competitive labour market than in an imperfectly competitive labour market.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (14): a diagram; the impact of higher productivity (MPP, so MRP) on wages and employment in both types of market; and a judgement of which is greater, with reference to both wages and employment.
  • Evaluation (6): perfect competition rests on unrealistic assumptions (perfect mobility, identical labour, perfect knowledge); productivity is hard to measure, especially in services; training costs and skill differences challenge the model; productivity could come from replacing labour with capital, causing unemployment; the effect on jobs depends on how easily capital can substitute for labour; in an imperfect market some gains may pass to wages but the costs of raising productivity may cancel them.
Diagram to drawTwo diagrams side by side. Left: a perfectly competitive firm with a horizontal wage line and the MRP curve shifting right. Right: a monopsony with ACL, MCL and the MRP shift. Mark the old and new employment and wage in each.
KANE

Marginal revenue product (MRP) is the extra revenue from employing one more worker, equal to marginal physical product multiplied by marginal revenue. A profit-maximising firm in a perfectly competitive labour market is a wage taker and hires where MRP equals the wage, while a firm in an imperfectly competitive labour market, such as a monopsony, faces an upward sloping supply curve and hires where MRP equals the marginal cost of labour. An increase in labour productivity raises MPP and so shifts the MRP curve to the right in both cases.

In a perfectly competitive labour market the firm can hire as many workers as it wants at the market wage, so the supply of labour to the firm is horizontal. The rightward shift of MRP raises employment, with the wage unchanged for the individual firm, because the firm cannot influence the market wage. If the productivity rise happens across the whole industry, demand for labour in the market rises and the market wage could also rise, so wages in a perfectly competitive industry might increase in the long run.

In a monopsony the supply curve slopes upwards, so more workers can only be hired by paying a higher wage to all of them. The shift in MRP raises employment where the new MRP meets MCL, and the wage, read from the supply curve at the higher employment, also rises. However, the wage is still below the worker's MRP, and the rise in employment is smaller than in the competitive case because the firm must pay more to attract each extra worker.

The assumptions behind the competitive model, such as perfect mobility and identical workers, are unlikely to hold, and productivity is hard to measure in services such as health and education. Training to raise productivity costs money and could increase skill differences between workers. If productivity rises because firms use more machines in place of labour, employment could fall rather than rise. Overall, the effect on employment is likely to be greater in a perfectly competitive market, where the supply of labour to the firm is horizontal, while the effect on the firm's wage is likely to be greater in an imperfect market, where some of the gain may pass to workers. The extent depends on how easily capital can substitute for labour and on how the costs of raising productivity are shared.

Feb/March 2022 · 9708/42 · Q4(a) 12 marks A profit maximising firm operating in a perfectly competitive labour market might decide to increase the number of workers it employs but it will not choose to increase its workers' wage rates. Discuss the extent to which you agree with this statement.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Assumptions of a perfectly competitive labour market: homogeneous labour, perfect mobility, no barriers to entry, firms are wage takers, perfect knowledge.
  • A profit-maximising firm employs labour up to where the marginal product (in revenue terms, MRP) equals the wage; explain the link between MRP and the demand for labour. The supply of labour to each firm is perfectly elastic at the market wage.
  • If productivity or the product price rises, demand for labour rises and more workers are employed but the wage does not change. Raising the wage would stop it maximising profit. A conclusion should recognise both sides.
KANE

In a perfectly competitive labour market there are many employers and many identical workers, workers are mobile and everyone has perfect knowledge, so the firm is a wage taker. The supply of labour to the firm is perfectly elastic at the market wage, and a profit-maximising firm employs workers up to the point where the marginal revenue product of labour equals that wage.

If productivity rises or the price of the firm's product rises, the MRP curve shifts to the right. At the unchanged market wage the firm now finds that more workers add more to revenue than to cost, so it hires more workers until MRP again equals the wage. This supports the statement that the firm might increase employment but not wages.

The firm is also unlikely to raise wages by itself. If it paid more than the market wage, its marginal cost of labour would exceed MRP at the old level of employment, so it would cut employment to restore profit maximisation, and workers would be available elsewhere at the market wage. However, wages in the whole market may rise if demand for labour rises across the industry, and a firm may pay above the market rate if the higher wage raises productivity through better motivation or lower turnover, which would break the assumptions of the model.

Overall, the statement is largely correct for an individual firm in a perfectly competitive labour market, but it depends on the model's strict assumptions, which many real labour markets do not meet.

Top tips

  • Define MRP in full. The extra revenue from one more worker, equal to MPP × MR. Do not confuse it with MPP (extra output).
  • Say "derived demand". Demand for labour comes from demand for the product the workers make.
  • Hire where MRP = marginal cost of labour. In a competitive labour market that is the wage; for a monopsony it is MCL.
  • Explain why MRP slopes down: diminishing marginal returns, so MPP falls.
  • Separate shifts from movements. A wage change moves along the curve; product demand, productivity, and substitutes shift it.
  • Link productivity to MPP, then to MRP, then to the diagram. Three steps, in that order.
  • For elasticity, give the four determinants (share of costs, substitutes, product elasticity, time) and say what the elasticity means for job losses.
  • Evaluate MRP theory: productivity is hard to measure, workers are not identical, firms may not maximise profit, and real labour markets are not perfectly competitive.
