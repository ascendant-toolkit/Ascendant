Government Spending and Fiscal Policy

5.2.5 to 5.2.7: current and capital government spending, expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy, and AD/AS analysis of their effects. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Fiscal policy is the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy. Government spending is either current (day-to-day: wages of public workers, benefits, running costs) or capital (investment in roads, hospitals, schools), and governments spend to provide public and merit goods, redistribute income, support the economy and raise productive capacity. Expansionary fiscal policy (higher spending or lower taxes, often a bigger deficit) shifts AD right to raise output and employment; contractionary fiscal policy (lower spending or higher taxes, a smaller deficit or a surplus) shifts AD left to reduce inflation. The results depend on spare capacity and on limits such as time lags, the cost in higher debt, crowding out of private spending and imports leaking abroad.

  • Transfer payments are not part of G in AD; they change AD indirectly through household incomes.

2 · Expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy (5.2.6 to 5.2.7)

ExpansionaryContractionary
ToolsRaise spending, cut taxesCut spending, raise taxes

  • Strengths: can be targeted at particular sectors or groups; can act directly on AD; spending on infrastructure and education can also raise AS.
  • Limits: time lags (recognition, decision, implementation); higher debt and interest costs; crowding out if borrowing raises interest rates; imports leak some of the rise in demand abroad; political pressures to avoid unpopular tax rises; uncertain effect on confidence.
  • Evaluation: an expansionary policy increases AD only if consumers and firms spend the extra income, and is most effective with spare capacity.

3 · Worked examples

An expansionary fiscal policy. Suppose the government increases spending by $10bn and the economy has spare capacity. If households spend 70% of any extra income, the first round of spending raises incomes by $10bn, of which $7bn is spent again, creating a further $7bn of income, and so on. The total rise in income is larger than the original $10bn. (The multiplier is not required at AS, but you can use the idea that an injection raises incomes by more than the initial amount.)

  • A rise in government spending has a bigger direct effect on AD than an equivalent tax cut, because some of the tax cut is saved or spent on imports.

5 · Discretionary policy and automatic stabilisers

  • Discretionary fiscal policy: a deliberate change in taxes or spending decided by the government.
  • Automatic stabilisers: changes that happen without a decision: in a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises, supporting demand; in a boom, the reverse. They reduce the size of the swings in the economy.
  • Time lags: there are delays in recognising a problem, deciding what to do, passing laws and for the money to be spent. A policy aimed at a recession may take effect after the economy has recovered.

6 · Limits in more detail

  • Crowding out: borrowing to fund a deficit may push up interest rates and reduce private investment, partly offsetting the rise in AD.
  • Imports: in an open economy part of the extra spending leaks abroad, so the rise in domestic output is smaller.
  • Saving: if households are worried about debt or taxes, they may save a tax cut rather than spend it (consumers may anticipate that taxes will rise later).
  • Debt and interest costs: persistent deficits add to the debt, raising the cost of servicing it. Large debts may make it hard to borrow more in the next recession.
  • Spare capacity: if the economy is near full capacity, extra AD mainly raises prices instead of output.
  • Political constraints: tax rises and spending cuts are unpopular; governments may time policies for elections rather than the economic cycle.
  • Quality of spending: a good return from spending on infrastructure or education is not guaranteed; poor projects may waste money.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A stimulus package

In a recession a government announces extra spending on infrastructure and cuts income tax. AD shifts right, output and jobs recover, but the budget deficit and the national debt rise.

Exam use: expansionary fiscal policy and its cost.

Austerity

To reduce a large deficit, a government cuts spending and raises taxes. AD falls, which reduces borrowing but can slow growth and raise unemployment in the short run.

Exam use: contractionary fiscal policy and its short-run cost.

Building a rail network

Capital spending on railways gives jobs in the short run (AD) and improves transport for firms in the long run (AS). Investment spending can therefore serve both demand and supply.

Exam use: capital expenditure raising capacity.

A tax cut that is saved

If households save most of a tax cut, or spend it on imports, consumption of domestic goods rises by less, so the policy does not raise AD as much as expected.

Exam use: why fiscal policy may not always raise AD.

Stimulus spending in a recession

A government pays for new school buildings and road repairs to support demand and jobs during a downturn. AD shifts right, but the deficit and debt rise and projects may take a long time to start.

Exam use: expansionary fiscal policy, the budget and time lags.

Austerity

A government with a large deficit cuts spending and raises taxes. The deficit falls, but AD shifts left, which may reduce growth and raise unemployment in the short run.

Exam use: contractionary fiscal policy and its costs.

Based on Feb/March 2025 · 9708/22 · Q5(a) 8 marks With the help of an AD/AS diagram, explain what is meant by an expansionary fiscal policy and consider the extent to which an expansionary fiscal policy will always increase the level of aggregate demand.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): expansionary fiscal policy is an increase in government spending and/or a reduction in tax rates (1); aggregate demand is the total demand for an economy's goods and services at a given price level, or C + I + G + (X − M) (1); an accurate diagram showing AD and AS curves (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): an explanation of how a decrease in income tax, for example, increases disposable income (1), which increases consumption and so aggregate demand (1); a diagram showing a rightward shift of the AD curve (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): an explanation of whether expansionary fiscal policy will increase AD (1) and a justified conclusion (1).
PlanDefine expansionary policy and AD. Tax cut: more disposable income, more consumption, AD right, with diagram. Evaluate: savings, imports, confidence.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Expansionary fiscal policy is a rise in government spending or a cut in taxes. Aggregate demand is total spending on an economy's output, C + I + G + (X − M). The diagram shows AD and SRAS meeting at the initial price level and output.

A cut in income tax raises households' disposable income, so consumption rises, and as consumption is a component of AD the AD curve shifts to the right, raising real output and the price level.

AD will not always rise by as much as expected. Households may save the tax cut or spend it on imports, and higher government borrowing could raise interest rates and crowd out private spending. The effect is largest when there is spare capacity and consumers are confident.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q5(b) 12 marks Assess whether expansionary fiscal policy always benefits an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the benefits, such as a rise in AD, real output and employment, and a fall in cyclical unemployment, with a diagram (up to 4); the costs and limits, such as a higher budget deficit and national debt, inflation near full capacity, crowding out, imports and time lags (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether expansionary policy always benefits the economy, depending on spare capacity, the size and type of spending and the level of debt, with a conclusion.
PlanBenefits: AD, output, jobs. Costs: deficit, debt, inflation, crowding out, time lags. Judge: depends on spare capacity and debt.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Expansionary fiscal policy, through higher spending or lower taxes, raises AD, which increases real output and reduces cyclical unemployment, especially in a recession with spare capacity. But it increases the budget deficit and the national debt, and if the economy is near full capacity the rise in AD mainly raises prices. Higher borrowing may crowd out private investment by raising interest rates, some of the extra demand leaks abroad as imports, and time lags may mean the policy takes effect too late.

The policy does not always benefit an economy. It is most beneficial in a deep recession with spare capacity and a manageable debt, and least beneficial when the economy is close to full employment or debt is already high.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/21 · Q5(b) 12 marks Assess whether a government should always aim for an expansionary fiscal policy rather than a contractionary fiscal policy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the case for expansionary policy: raises AD, output and employment, useful in a recession (up to 4); the case for contractionary policy: reduces inflation and the budget deficit and debt (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement, depending on the economic situation (recession or boom), inflation, unemployment and debt, with a conclusion that neither should be used always.
PlanExpansionary: recession, jobs. Contractionary: inflation, debt. Judge: depends on the stage of the cycle and the objectives.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Expansionary fiscal policy is suitable when output is below potential and unemployment is high, since extra spending or tax cuts raise AD and create jobs. But it adds to the deficit and may cause inflation if used when the economy is at capacity. Contractionary policy reduces inflationary pressure and the deficit, but it reduces AD, so growth slows and unemployment may rise.

A government should not always choose expansion. The right policy depends on where the economy is in the cycle, on the level of debt and on its objectives, so expansionary policy is best in recession and contractionary policy in a boom.

Based on Feb/March 2022 · 9708/22 · Q3(b) 12 marks Discuss the ways in which fiscal policy can be used to increase the aggregate supply of products in an economy and consider whether this approach is likely to be effective.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): ways fiscal policy can raise AS, such as capital spending on infrastructure, spending on education, training and health, tax incentives for investment and research, lower income tax to encourage work, and subsidies for technology (up to 4 for explaining ways, up to 4 for how each raises AS).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): whether the approach is likely to be effective: time lags, cost and opportunity cost, uncertainty about returns, the possibility of crowding out, the need for matching AD, with a conclusion.
PlanWays: infrastructure, education, tax incentives. Effect: shifts LRAS right. Limits: time lags, cost, uncertain return.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Fiscal policy can raise aggregate supply through capital spending on roads and railways, which lowers firms' costs, and through spending on education and training, which raises the productivity of labour. Tax incentives for investment and research encourage firms to expand capacity, and lower income taxes may encourage more people to work. Each shifts LRAS to the right, raising potential output.

These policies are likely to be effective in the long run, but they take years to work, they cost money and raise the deficit, and the return is uncertain if the money is spent badly. In the short run the spending also raises AD, so inflation could rise before supply benefits arrive.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess the extent to which using fiscal policy would be the best way to reduce a high rate of inflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): fiscal policy involves raising taxation and reducing government spending; the advantages include being relatively quick and effective, particularly against demand-pull inflation; the disadvantages include reducing economic growth, possibly causing unemployment, and being less effective if the main cause is cost-push inflation; these must be analysed against an alternative policy, likely to be supply-side or monetary, with a focus on its likely success in reducing a high rather than a low or sustainable rate of inflation.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a clear comparison of fiscal policy with at least one other policy and a justified conclusion as to which is best.
PlanFiscal: tax up, spending down, AD left; effective for demand-pull, costs growth and jobs, weak for cost-push. Alternative: monetary or supply-side. Judge by cause.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Raising taxes and cutting government spending reduces disposable income and government demand, so AD shifts left and demand-pull inflation falls. It can be targeted and acts directly, but it also reduces output and employment, takes time to legislate and does little against cost-push inflation, where costs rather than demand are the problem. Contractionary monetary policy, through higher interest rates, is quicker to decide but also works with a lag, while supply-side policy could address cost-push inflation but is slow.

Fiscal policy is likely to be effective against high demand-pull inflation, but it is not the best on its own: its cost in jobs and growth means that a combination with monetary policy, and supply-side measures for cost-push inflation, is likely to be better. The best choice depends on the cause of the inflation.

Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Discuss whether a government should use monetary policy or fiscal policy to solve the problem of deflation in an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): how monetary policy can stimulate aggregate demand, with its strengths and weaknesses (up to 4; a maximum of 3 if only strengths or only weaknesses); how fiscal policy can stimulate aggregate demand, with its strengths and weaknesses (up to 4; a maximum of 3 if one-sided).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a clear comparison of the use of each policy to solve deflation (up to 3) leading to a reasoned conclusion as to which may be the most appropriate (1). No knowledge marks for merely defining terms in the question.
PlanMonetary: lower rates, more credit; quick to decide, limited if rates near zero or confidence low. Fiscal: higher spending, tax cuts; direct, but debt and lags. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Monetary policy can cut interest rates and increase the money supply, which makes borrowing cheaper and raises consumption and investment. It can be changed quickly, but in deflation consumers expect prices to fall and may not borrow, banks may not lend and rates may already be near zero. Fiscal policy can raise government spending or cut taxes, which injects demand directly and can target projects, but it increases the deficit and debt, takes time to implement and may crowd out private spending.

In a deep deflation fiscal policy is likely to be more reliable because it adds directly to demand, while monetary policy may be ineffective at near-zero interest rates. But monetary policy is quicker and cheaper for mild deflation. The best approach is probably a combination.

Top tips

  • Define expansionary and contractionary. Spending up or taxes down; spending down or taxes up.
  • Show the chain. Tax cut, income, consumption, AD, output.
  • Use an AD/AS diagram. Right shift for expansionary, left for contractionary.
  • Say when each is used. Recession versus inflation.
  • Evaluate with spare capacity. Output rises most if there is spare capacity.
  • Mention limits. Time lags, debt, crowding out, imports.
  • Capital spending can raise AS. Infrastructure, education.
