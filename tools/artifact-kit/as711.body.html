Ethics, Copyright and Software Licences

Coursebook: Chapter 7 · Ethics and ownership · section 7.1 Ethics and ownership

Acting ethically toward colleagues, clients and the public, professional ethical bodies, copyright, and the commercial, shareware, freeware, open source and free software licences, with animations, a licence chooser and the real Paper 1 questions from 2021 to 2026.

This page in five sentences

Ethics is about acting responsibly and fairly towards colleagues, clients and the public. A programmer who joins a professional ethical body (such as the BCS, ACM or IEEE) follows ethical guidelines, gains recognition, and gets help, support and training. Copyright gives the author the legal right to control copying, changing and selling a program, so it protects their intellectual property and lets them take legal action against theft. A software licence states what users may do with the software: commercial (pay, no source code, no copying), shareware (free trial, then pay), freeware (free to use, source not available) and open source / free software (source code available, users may edit and redistribute, as defined by the Open Source Initiative and the Free Software Foundation). Exam answers name the licence and give reasons that fit the scenario.

1 · Acting ethically

TowardA programmer shouldWhy
ColleaguesTreat colleagues fairly and avoid discrimination; accept and offer critique; credit their contributions; help and train themSo technical work improves and colleagues feel valued and respected; a team works best when its members feel valued
The publicMaintain their health, safety and welfare; be honest and realistic in claims about the software; keep the public's data secure; not spread malwareSo that nobody is endangered and public trust is maintained
Clients and the companyAct in the best interest of the product, the client and the profession; report errors foundAn unreported error may make the product fail, let the client down and harm the profession

Students on a school network should also respect privacy, not copy work that is not their own, not use social networking inappropriately or cyberbully, follow the school rules for the network, use it only for legitimate purposes and not hack other computers.

2 · Professional ethical bodies

Benefits of joining: there are ethical guidelines to follow (so clients and staff know the standards, and the programmer does not have to decide what is ethical because it is written down); clients know the programmer is reputable (there may be a test for entry), which gives recognition of skills; the body gives help and support, such as legal advice; and it runs training courses to keep skills up to date. Not joining may mean no legal advice if action is taken, fewer networking opportunities (and so contacts and jobs), less training and no clear ethical guidelines, possibly leading to unethical actions.

3 · Copyright

Copyright gives the owner legal recognition as the author and the right to stop others copying, changing or selling the program without permission. A programmer should copyright their program to identify themselves as the owner (formal recognition of ownership), to allow legal consequences if anyone steals or copies it, and to restrict competition.

4 · Software licences

LicenceSource codeCost and useBenefits
CommercialNot available; users cannot legally change or share itA fee is charged before use; no free trial; limits on use (number of installs)The developer earns a fee; support and maintenance (updates, bug fixes) usually provided; usually well tested; the program is copyrighted
SharewareNot availableFree trial (maybe with limited features or a time limit), then payUsers can test before buying; no initial cost; the word spreads; the developer gets feedback and bug reports; income from users who buy
FreewareNot availableFree to use; usually cannot be changed or soldSpreads widely at no cost to the user
Open Source Initiative (OSI)Available; users can edit and redistributeOften free of charge; developer can still sell support and updatesUsers can customise it, report and fix errors, add features and collaborate
Free Software Foundation (FSF)Available; users can run, study, change and redistributeFree to use as freedom (not necessarily no cost)As open source; the code stays free for others

Choosing a licence: sell to the public and keep the code private: commercial (or shareware to let people try first). A business that needs to modify the code and pays for maintenance: open source (OSI) or FSF. A program whose wrong output could cause harm (such as one that reads allergen labels): an open licence is not appropriate because changed code might output wrong results, so people could buy the wrong item.

Common ways to lose marks

  • Naming a licence without reasons that fit the scenario (paying customers, a business that edits the code).
  • Saying "open source is free" and stopping. The key idea is that the source code is available and can be edited and redistributed.
  • Giving 'to protect the program' as the only benefit of copyright. Say it identifies the owner and allows legal action.
  • Writing general statements about ethics, such as "act honestly", with no link to colleagues or the public.
  • Mixing shareware (trial then pay) with freeware (free, no trial).

Top tips

  • Match the licence to the scenario. Pay and private: commercial. Edit: open.
  • Shareware: trial then pay. Not 'free'.
  • Open source: edit and redistribute. Name OSI and FSF.
  • Ethics: colleagues, public, client. Give a reason for each.
  • Ethical body: four benefits. Guidelines, reputable, support, training.
  • Copyright: owner and legal action. Two marks.
