A Level Economics · 9708 · Syllabus 11.4.3

Economic Structure and the Pattern of Trade

11.4.3: employment in the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors, how the pattern of trade changes with development, primary product dependence and the terms of trade. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

As an economy develops, employment shifts from the primary sector (farming, mining, fishing) to the secondary sector (manufacturing and construction) and then to the tertiary sector (services), because farming productivity rises and the demand for manufactures and services grows faster than the demand for food. The pattern of trade changes too: low-income countries export primary products and import manufactures, newly industrialising countries export labour-intensive manufactures, and high-income countries export capital-intensive goods and services. Dependence on a few primary exports exposes a country to price volatility and a long-run fall in its terms of trade, which is why many governments try to diversify.

1 · Employment composition by sector

SectorActivitiesTypical share of employment
PrimaryAgriculture, forestry, fishing, mining and oil extractionLarge in low-income countries (often over half), tiny in high-income countries
SecondaryManufacturing, construction, utilitiesRises in the middle of development, then flattens or falls (de-industrialisation)
TertiaryRetail, banking, health, education, tourism and other servicesSmall in low-income countries, largest by far in high-income countries

Why the shares change

  • Rising farm productivity (better seeds, machinery, irrigation) means fewer workers are needed on the land, so labour is released to other sectors.
  • Income elasticity of demand: as incomes rise, spending on food rises slowly (it is a necessity, income inelastic: Engel's law), while spending on manufactures and especially services rises faster (income elastic). Output and jobs follow demand.
  • Urbanisation and investment in infrastructure, education and technology make non-farm jobs more productive and attractive.
  • In rich countries automation and offshoring have reduced manufacturing jobs while services grow, which is sometimes called de-industrialisation.
  • Implications: moving workers from low-productivity farming to higher-productivity industry and services raises average output per worker (structural change contributes to growth). But rapid shifts can cause structural unemployment and a large informal sector if jobs are not created fast enough.

  • Most trade by value is among high-income countries and between rich and poor (North-South). Trade among developing countries (South-South) has been growing, especially with China and other emerging economies.
  • Comparative advantage explains specialisation: land and labour-rich countries export primary and labour-intensive goods; capital- and skill-rich countries export capital- and knowledge-intensive goods.

3 · Dependence on primary exports: the problems

  • Price volatility: demand for many primary goods is income and price inelastic and supply is unpredictable (weather, harvests). Small shifts in supply or demand cause large price swings, so export earnings, farmers' incomes, tax revenue and investment fluctuate.
  • Long-run decline in the terms of trade (Prebisch-Singer): as world incomes rise, demand for manufactures grows faster than for primary goods, so primary prices tend to fall relative to manufactured prices. Terms of trade = (index of export prices ÷ index of import prices) × 100. A fall means a country must export more to buy the same imports.
  • Low value added and limited linkages: few jobs and little technology transfer compared with manufacturing.
  • Dutch disease and resource dependence: a booming natural resource sector can raise the exchange rate and hurt other exports.
  • Policies: diversify into manufactures and services, add value locally (processing), invest in education and infrastructure, stabilise prices (buffer stocks, hedging), use trade agreements and export-led growth, and protect infant industries temporarily. Each has costs and time lags.
Evaluation pointReliance on primary products is a cause of low growth in many developing countries, but it is not the only one: low investment, poor infrastructure, weak education, conflict and governance also matter, and some countries with primary exports have grown strongly. Also, rich resource exporters (such as oil producers) can have high incomes, so the answer depends on the product and the policy.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Coffee, cocoa and copper

Countries that depend on one or two crops or minerals see their export earnings rise and fall with world prices. When coffee or copper prices drop sharply, farm incomes, government revenue and the exchange rate all fall, which makes planning and investment hard.

Exam use: price volatility and why diversification is a common policy aim.

South Korea and Taiwan

Both economies moved from agriculture and light industry to electronics, ships, cars and later services, with exports of manufactures driving growth. The share of workers in farming fell sharply over a few decades while productivity and incomes rose.

Exam use: structural change and export-led growth as part of development.

Bangladesh and garments

Bangladesh has built a large clothing industry, which now supplies most of its exports and employs millions of workers, many of them women. It moved the economy away from agriculture, though it depends heavily on a single sector and on buyers in rich countries.

Exam use: a pattern of trade at an early stage of industrialisation, with the risk of dependence.

Oil exporters and diversification

Many oil-exporting countries have high incomes but rely on oil for most export revenue and government income. Plans to build tourism, finance and manufacturing aim to reduce the exposure to oil price swings.

Exam use: resource dependence versus diversification, and why income and development can differ.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/42 · Q5 25 marks Many developing countries rely heavily on the production and export of primary products. This is the main cause of low growth in developing countries and therefore the main explanation for their low standard of living. Discuss the extent to which you agree.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Three elements: first, many developing countries do rely on primary products, which have inelastic demand (food is a necessity) and supply that varies with the weather, giving price and income volatility.
  • Second, reliance on primary goods with small secondary and tertiary sectors links to low growth through low investment and low productivity in the primary sector, and low saving from low incomes.
  • Third, other factors may contribute (for example education and training), and the link between growth and living standards is not automatic: high growth can reduce living standards (negative externalities), and living standards depend on more than goods and services (use alternative measures).
  • Level 4 (18 to 25) analyses the links and assesses how far each is supported, with a conclusion.
PlanPrimary dependence and volatility. Link to low growth (investment and productivity). Other causes of low growth. Growth versus living standards. Conclude.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Many developing countries rely on primary products. Demand for food and raw materials is inelastic and supply varies with the weather, so prices and export earnings are volatile. Volatile income may discourage investment, and low incomes in the primary sector mean low saving and low productivity, which could keep growth low. Over the long run the terms of trade could fall as demand for manufactures grows faster than demand for primary goods. Low growth then limits the rise in incomes and so living standards.

However, this may not be the main cause. Low levels of education and training, weak infrastructure, poor governance and conflict also reduce growth, and some countries with primary exports have grown quickly. In addition, the link from growth to living standards is not automatic, because the gains may be unequally shared and growth can damage the environment, and living standards depend on health and education as well as income. So primary dependence is probably an important but not the only cause.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/41 · Q5 25 marks With the help of a circular flow diagram, discuss how a policy of export-led growth might affect the standard of living in a developing economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Export-led growth uses an outward-looking strategy relying on openness and trade; a successful policy raises national income, output and employment.
  • Circular flow diagram with all injections and withdrawals: an increase in exports (an injection) raises output and income by a multiple through the multiplier, so real GDP per head rises and access to goods and services improves.
  • Evaluation: a developing economy is likely to rely on primary goods with income-inelastic demand and volatile supply, so the growth effect may be small; the effect on living standards depends on the distribution of income, loss of leisure and effects on long-term growth of focusing on primary products.
PlanDefine export-led growth. Circular flow diagram: exports as an injection, with a multiplier. Standard of living. Primary goods and volatility. Distribution. Conclude.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Export-led growth is a strategy of expanding output for sale abroad. In the circular flow, exports are an injection, so the national income equilibrium moves to a higher level, with a multiplier effect that raises output, employment and income by more than the original rise in exports. Higher real GDP per head could give people access to more goods and services, so the material standard of living may rise. Extra export earnings also help to pay for imported capital goods and technology, which may raise productivity.

However, a developing economy may export mainly primary goods with income-inelastic demand and volatile prices, so the gain in income may be small and unstable. The standard of living also depends on how the extra income is distributed, on working conditions and on the environment, so exports could raise GDP without much improvement for the poorest.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/43 · Q5(a) 12 marks Consider, with the help of a circular flow diagram, why an export-led growth policy is likely to be successful when a developed economy is in a recession.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Describe export-led growth and show on a labelled circular flow diagram an increase in injections (exports), with a larger effect on output, income and employment through the multiplier.
  • Recession: GDP falling for two successive quarters, rising unemployment from falling aggregate demand.
  • Compare alternatives: lower interest rates may be ineffective with low confidence, and higher government spending is limited if public debt is high; a policy that boosts exports avoids those problems and may protect the currency and the balance of payments; its success depends on the exchange rate regime and policy used, for example lowering interest rates to depreciate a floating currency.
PlanDiagram with exports as an injection. Recession and unemployment. Why it beats other policies when rates are low and debt is high. Depends on exchange rate.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

In a recession, falling aggregate demand reduces output and raises unemployment. Export-led growth boosts demand from abroad: in the circular flow exports are an injection, so national income, output and employment increase through the multiplier. Other policies may be limited in a recession. Lower interest rates may not work if confidence is low, and a larger budget deficit may be hard to finance if debt is already high. Raising exports does not depend on domestic confidence or on public borrowing, and it improves the balance of payments.

It is more likely to succeed if the exchange rate can fall, for example in a floating system after interest rate cuts, and if trading partners are growing. If they are also in recession, demand for exports may be weak.

Top tips

  • Name the three sectors. Primary, secondary and tertiary, and what each includes.
  • Explain why the shares change. Farm productivity, income elasticity (Engel's law) and technology.
  • Compare patterns of trade. Low-income: primary exports. Newly industrialised: manufactures. High-income: capital goods and services.
  • Give the volatility reason. Inelastic demand plus supply that varies with the weather means price swings.
  • Quote the terms of trade. (Export prices ÷ import prices) × 100. A fall is a deterioration.
  • Use the circular flow for exports. Exports are an injection. Show the multiplier effect on income.
  • Evaluate the link to living standards. Growth may not reach the poor; other factors also matter.
