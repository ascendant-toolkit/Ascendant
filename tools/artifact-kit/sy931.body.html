Full Employment and Types of Unemployment

9.3.1 to 9.3.3: full employment, equilibrium and disequilibrium unemployment including hysteresis, and voluntary and involuntary unemployment. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Full employment means everyone who is willing and able to work at the going wage can find a job; it does not mean zero unemployment, because some frictional and structural unemployment always remains. Equilibrium unemployment exists even when the labour market is in balance (frictional, structural, seasonal). Disequilibrium unemployment exists because the labour market is not in balance: wages are above the equilibrium level (real wage or classical unemployment) or demand for goods is too low (demand-deficient or cyclical unemployment). Hysteresis is when a rise in unemployment feeds on itself and stays high after its cause has gone, because workers lose skills and contacts.

1 · Measuring employment and unemployment

Labour force

Everyone of working age who is employed or actively seeking work. People who are retired, in full-time education, caring at home or have given up looking are economically inactive and are not in the labour force.

labour force = employed + unemployed

Rates

unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100
employment rate = employed ÷ working-age population × 100

2 · Full employment (9.3.1)

  • Everyone willing and able to work at the current wage can find a job; the labour market is in equilibrium (labour demand equals supply).
  • There is still frictional unemployment (people between jobs), structural unemployment (skills or location mismatch) and seasonal unemployment. Some economists put full employment at about 3% to 5% unemployment; the figure depends on the country and the period.
  • Full employment is closely linked to potential output: when the economy is at full employment, output is at the LRAS.

3 · Equilibrium and disequilibrium unemployment (9.3.2)

Labour marketIn balance: demand equals supply at the going wageNot in balance: more people willing to work than jobs on offer
TypesFrictional, structural, seasonal, voluntary (the natural rate)Real wage (classical) and demand-deficient (cyclical, Keynesian)
CauseSearch time, skills mismatch, seasonal patterns, benefits that discourage workWages above equilibrium (minimum wage, union power), or low aggregate demand and sticky wages
DurationPersists even when the economy is at full capacityFalls if wages adjust or demand recovers

Real wage (classical) unemployment

The wage is held above the equilibrium by a minimum wage or union bargaining. Firms demand less labour than workers supply, so there is excess supply of labour. Classical economists say the answer is to let wages fall or remove the floor.

Demand-deficient (cyclical) unemployment

A fall in aggregate demand (recession) reduces demand for goods, so firms cut output and jobs. Keynesians say wages are sticky downwards, so unemployment can persist until demand recovers. The answer is to raise AD.

Frictional

Short-term unemployment while people search for a new job or enter the labour market. Poor information about vacancies lengthens it. It exists even in a healthy economy.

Structural

A long-term mismatch between the skills or location of workers and the jobs available, caused by technology, changes in demand, foreign competition or the decline of an industry. It can be regional or technological.

Seasonal unemployment follows the time of year (tourism, farming, construction).

Hysteresis

After a recession, unemployment may not fall back to its previous level. Hysteresis means the equilibrium rate is raised by the experience of unemployment:

  • Long-term unemployed workers lose skills and work habits.
  • Employers see long spells as a signal of low quality (stigma).
  • Workers become discouraged and stop searching, or become outsiders to the wage bargain (insider-outsider).
  • Industries close and capital is scrapped, so jobs do not come back where workers live.
Why it mattersHysteresis turns cyclical unemployment into structural unemployment and raises the natural rate. It is why governments try to prevent long recessions and help the long-term unemployed early with training.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Coal mining closures

When mines close because power stations switch to gas and renewables, miners often have skills that no local employer needs and live in towns with few other jobs. Many stay unemployed for years.

Exam use: structural unemployment, regional immobility and hysteresis. Link to retraining and regional investment as the policy response.

Youth unemployment in southern Europe after 2010

After the financial crisis and the euro-zone recession, youth unemployment in Spain and Greece reached 50% or more. Many young people spent years without work, losing skills and experience.

Exam use: demand-deficient unemployment that risked becoming hysteresis. Use it to say why a long recession costs more than lost output.

Summer jobs on the coast

A seaside town employs many hotel and cafe staff between May and September, and many of them are out of work in winter. Employers hire again every year.

Exam use: seasonal unemployment, which is predictable and part of the equilibrium rate. It is hard to cut with demand policy.

A graduate searching for the right job

A new graduate turns down jobs they see as below their skills while waiting for a better offer, supported by savings and family. Some weeks later they take a job in their field.

Exam use: frictional and voluntary unemployment. Better job information and shorter search time reduce it.

Hysteresis means that a rise in unemployment, for example in a recession, does not fully reverse when the cause disappears, because the experience of unemployment itself reduces the chances of re-employment. Workers who are out of work for a long time lose skills and work habits, so employers find them less employable. Some employers may treat a long spell of unemployment as a sign of low quality, and some workers become discouraged and stop searching. When demand recovers, vacancies appear but many of the unemployed do not fit them, so cyclical unemployment has become structural.

This raises the natural rate of unemployment, because there are more workers in the economy who cannot easily be matched to jobs even when demand is high. A rise in demand may therefore push up wages and prices sooner, rather than reduce unemployment. However, hysteresis can be reduced by early intervention, for example retraining programmes and temporary wage subsidies for the long-term unemployed, so the effect depends on policy and on how long the recession lasts.

Top tips

  • Full employment is not zero unemployment. It means everyone willing and able to work at the going wage can find a job. Frictional and structural unemployment remain.
  • Use the rate formulas. Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force. Know who is in the labour force and who is inactive.
  • Equilibrium versus disequilibrium. Say whether the labour market is in balance. Real wage and demand-deficient unemployment are disequilibrium.
  • Draw the labour market. A wage floor above equilibrium shows real wage unemployment as the gap between supply and demand.
  • Match type to cause to cure. Frictional: information. Structural: retraining and mobility. Cyclical: raise AD. Real wage: weaken the floor. Seasonal: diversify.
  • Explain hysteresis with a mechanism. Skills decay, stigma and discouragement are the usual ones.
  • Voluntary does not mean 'their own fault'. It means they choose not to accept jobs at the going wage, often because of benefits, search time or a high reservation wage.
