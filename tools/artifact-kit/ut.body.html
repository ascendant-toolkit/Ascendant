Utility

How economists measure satisfaction, why each extra unit gives less, how a rational consumer splits a budget between goods, and how all of that builds the downward-sloping demand curve. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions, with mark scheme points.

The chapter in four sentences

Utility is the satisfaction a consumer gets from consuming a good. Total utility (TU) is all of it, marginal utility (MU) is the extra from one more unit, and MU diminishes as consumption rises. A rational consumer with a limited income maximises total utility by choosing the bundle where MU ÷ price is the same for every good (the equi-marginal principle). When a price falls, MU per dollar of that good rises, the consumer buys more, and that gives the downward-sloping demand curve.

1 · Total utility and marginal utility

Total utility (TU)

The total satisfaction from consuming a given quantity of a good. It rises as you consume more, but at a decreasing rate, up to a maximum point.

Marginal utility (MU)

The extra satisfaction from consuming one more unit.

MU = change in TU ÷ change in quantity

Take slices of pizza. Utility is measured in made-up units called utils.

Three facts to learnWhile MU is positive, TU rises. When MU = 0, TU is at its maximum. When MU is negative, TU falls. The law of diminishing marginal utility says MU falls as you consume more of a good in a given time.

2 · Why the law of diminishing marginal utility matters

It explains why people will pay less for the next unit than for the last. If MU falls, the price a consumer is willing to pay for each extra unit falls too. That is the first step towards a downward-sloping demand curve. It also explains the paradox of value: water is cheap but diamonds are dear because water is plentiful (low MU of the last unit) and diamonds are scarce (high MU).

3 · Consumer equilibrium: the equi-marginal principle

A consumer has a limited income, so choosing more of one good means less of another. To get the most total utility from a budget, the consumer should compare utility per dollar for each good.

MUx ÷ Px = MUy ÷ Py = … = MU of the last dollar spent

If MUx/Px > MUy/Py

The last dollar on X gives more utility than on Y. Switch spending from Y to X. As X rises, its MU falls, and Y's MU rises, until the ratios are equal.

If the ratios are equal

No switch can add utility. The consumer is in equilibrium: total utility is maximised for this income and these prices.

Example: income $24, price of X is $4, price of Y is $2. Buying 4 units of X and 4 of Y gives MUx = 12 and MUy = 6, so 12 ÷ 4 = 6 ÷ 2 = 3 utils per dollar. See the animation on the Watch tab.

CarefulThe rule is "equal MU per dollar", not equal MU and not equal total utility. A common mistake in Paper 3.

4 · Deriving the demand curve from marginal utility

  1. Start in equilibrium: MUx ÷ Px = MUy ÷ Py.
  2. The price of X falls. Now MUx ÷ Px is higher than MUy ÷ Py.
  3. The consumer switches spending towards X, so quantity of X rises. MUx falls as more X is bought, until the ratios are equal again.
  4. Plot the price of X against the quantity of X at each equilibrium. Joining the points gives the individual demand curve, and adding all individuals gives the market demand curve.

Marshall's version: the price a consumer is willing to pay for a unit equals the MU of that unit (in money terms). So the demand curve is the MU curve, and the area between the demand curve and the price is consumer surplus.

5 · Assumptions and limitations

Assumptions

  • The consumer is rational: aims to maximise utility
  • Complete information about prices and satisfaction
  • Utility can be measured (cardinal utility, in utils)
  • MU diminishes
  • Limited income, and the marginal utility of money is constant
  • Ceteris paribus: tastes, income and other prices do not change

Criticisms

  • Satisfaction cannot really be measured in utils
  • People lack information, follow habits and are swayed by advertising (bounded rationality)
  • It cannot separate income and substitution effects, so it cannot explain Giffen goods
  • Hard to apply to one-off purchases such as a house
  • A dollar means more to a poor person than a rich one, so MU of money is not constant

The alternative, indifference curve analysis (syllabus 7.2), needs only a ranking of preferences ("ordinal" utility) and can separate income and substitution effects.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. The numbers are simple on purpose, to show the idea.

The all-you-can-eat buffet

The first plate is very satisfying. By the fourth, extra food adds little, and a fifth plate may make you feel ill. Marginal utility falls, reaches zero, and could turn negative. A buffet owner can charge one price because diners are likely to stop when MU is low, not when they have eaten everything.

Exam use: a ready example of diminishing marginal utility, including the zero and negative stages. A 2021 multiple choice question used a buffet's final course.

Water and diamonds

Water is essential but cheap. Diamonds are not essential but costly. Total utility from water is huge, but because it is plentiful the MU of the last unit is low. Diamonds are scarce, so the MU of one more diamond is high.

Exam use: the paradox of value. Say that price reflects marginal utility, not total utility.

A student with $24 for the week

The student splits money between coffee and snacks. If the last dollar on snacks gives more satisfaction than the last dollar on coffee, the student may switch spending until the two are about equal.

Exam use: the equi-marginal principle in words. Use "utility per dollar of the last unit" and state that the consumer could keep adjusting until the ratios are equal.

A discount on one good

When a supermarket cuts the price of a fruit, a shopper may buy more of it and less of something similar. The fruit now gives more utility per dollar than before, so spending could move towards it.

Exam use: derive one point on the demand curve: a fall in price raises MU ÷ P, so quantity demanded rises.

A house, a wedding, a university degree

These are one-off purchases. You cannot buy "a bit more" of them and compare MU per dollar easily. Marginal utility theory may fit everyday goods better than big decisions.

Exam use: an evaluation point in the 2024 essay: the theory has difficulty with one-off goods.

Advertising and habit

People do not always know the utility they will get. Advertising, fashion and habit can change choices without any change in price or income. This is one reason economists question the rational consumer.

Exam use: evaluation: imperfect information, bounded rationality, impact of advertising.

Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/42 · Q2 20 marks Evaluate whether marginal utility theory can fully explain the link between the changing price of a good and quantity demanded of that good.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge and analysis (14): definitions of marginal and total utility; total utility and diminishing marginal utility; the effect of a price change through the equi-marginal principle, illustrated with a diagram; the downward-sloping demand curve, which can apply to normal and inferior (non-Giffen) goods but the theory does not tell them apart.
  • Evaluation (6): validity of the rational consumer (imperfect information, bounded rationality, advertising); it cannot separate income and substitution effects so it does not explain Giffen goods; ceteris paribus is hard to keep, so each change needs a new demand schedule; the assumption that money has constant utility; difficulty with one-off goods such as a house.
Diagram to drawTwo panels. Left: MUx ÷ Px bars or curve before and after the price fall. Right: demand curve with two points, price falling and quantity rising. Label the equilibrium conditions.
KANE

Total utility is the total satisfaction from consuming a quantity of a good and marginal utility is the extra satisfaction from one more unit. Marginal utility is assumed to diminish as consumption rises. A consumer with a limited income is in equilibrium when the marginal utility per dollar is the same for every good, so that MUx divided by Px equals MUy divided by Py.

Marginal utility theory can explain why the quantity demanded of a good rises when its price falls. If the price of X falls, MUx divided by Px becomes greater than MUy divided by Py, so the last dollar spent on X now gives more satisfaction than the last dollar spent on Y. The consumer is likely to switch spending towards X. As more X is bought, MUx falls because of diminishing marginal utility, until the two ratios are equal again at a higher quantity. Plotting the price against the quantity at each equilibrium traces a downward sloping demand curve. This explanation holds for goods where the consumer can adjust quantity in small steps, but it may not work for a good that is bought only once.

However, the theory may not fully explain the link. It cannot separate the income effect from the substitution effect, so it does not distinguish a normal good from an inferior good and cannot account for the rare case of a Giffen good, whose demand may rise when its price rises. It also assumes that the marginal utility of money is constant, but a dollar is likely to be worth more to someone earning $100 than to someone earning $10,000, so a price change could affect people differently.

The theory relies on the rational consumer with full information. In practice consumers may be influenced by advertising, habit and imperfect information, and may use rules of thumb (bounded rationality), so the expected response to a price change could be different. Other things rarely stay equal, so each change in price may need a new estimate of the demand schedule. Overall, marginal utility theory is likely to give a useful explanation of why demand slopes downward for everyday goods, but it cannot fully explain the link, because it ignores income and substitution effects and relies on strong assumptions about consumer behaviour.

KANE

Total utility is the total satisfaction from consuming a good and marginal utility is the extra satisfaction from one more unit. Marginal utility diminishes as more units are consumed. A rational consumer with a limited income maximises total utility when the marginal utility per dollar is equal across goods, so MUx divided by Px equals MUy divided by Py. If MUx divided by Px were higher, the consumer could gain by spending more on X and less on Y.

Suppose the price of X is $4 and the price of Y is $2, and a consumer with $24 is in equilibrium buying 4 units of X and 4 of Y, where MUx is 12 and MUy is 6, so each ratio is 3. If the price of X falls to $2, MUx divided by Px at the old quantity doubles to 6 while MUy divided by Py stays at 3. The consumer is likely to buy more X and less Y until the ratios are equal again, which happens at a larger quantity of X because MUx falls as more X is bought. Plotting the price of X against the quantity of X at each equilibrium gives points on the individual demand curve, which slopes downward.

Adding the quantities that every individual would buy at each price gives the market demand curve, which also slopes downward. This holds only if tastes, income and the prices of other goods stay unchanged. If the marginal utility of other goods changes, the demand curve could shift.

Top tips

  • Define both TU and MU. TU is total satisfaction from a quantity. MU is the extra satisfaction from one more unit.
  • Remember the three stages: MU positive means TU rises, MU zero means TU is at its maximum, MU negative means TU falls.
  • The equi-marginal rule uses prices: MUx/Px = MUy/Py. Never write MUx = MUy on its own.
  • Always use two goods when explaining equilibrium. One good is held at Level 3 or below.
  • Show the change: price falls, MU per dollar of that good rises, consumer buys more, MU falls, new equilibrium. Then plot the demand point.
  • Go from individual to market demand by adding the quantities. The mark scheme caps individual-only answers at 9.
  • Evaluate with the assumptions: measurable utility, rationality and information, constant MU of money, ceteris paribus, one-off goods, Giffen goods.
  • Draw it. Two panels: MU per dollar before and after, and the demand curve.
