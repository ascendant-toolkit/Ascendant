Demand and Supply Curves and Their Determinants

2.1.1 to 2.1.4: effective demand, individual and market demand and supply, and the determinants of each. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Effective demand is the quantity people are willing and able to buy at each price, so a want with no money behind it is not effective demand. Individual demand is one consumer's demand; market demand is the horizontal sum of all the individual demand curves, and the same is true of supply. Demand slopes downward because a lower price makes people able and willing to buy more (income and substitution effects), and supply slopes upward because a higher price makes extra output more profitable and covers rising costs. A change in the price of the good itself moves along the curve, while a change in any other determinant shifts the curve.

1 · Effective demand (2.1.1)

  • Wants and needs are not demand. A student wants a sports car, but without the income to buy it there is no effective demand.
  • Demand needs willingness (the preference) and ability to pay (income, savings or credit), at a given price and in a given time period.
  • A demand curve shows the quantity demanded at each price, ceteris paribus (other things equal). It slopes down: the law of demand.

2 · Individual and market demand and supply (2.1.2)

  • Market demand = the sum of everyone's quantity demanded at each price, which is a horizontal addition of individual curves.
  • Market supply = the sum of all firms' quantities supplied at each price.
  • More buyers (or firms) shift the market curve to the right, but do not change an individual's curve.
  • Some factors affect an individual curve more than the market curve, for example a change in one person's tastes or income.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A student and a sports car

A student wants a luxury car but cannot afford one, so adds nothing to market demand. When the student gets a well-paid job, the want becomes effective demand. The wish was always there; the ability to pay arrived.

Exam use: effective demand needs willingness and ability to pay.

Coffee prices and tea

A poor coffee harvest raises coffee prices. Many drinkers switch to tea, so demand for tea shifts to the right, while demand for coffee mugs and filter paper (complements) may fall.

Exam use: substitutes, complements and the difference between a movement and a shift.

Solar panels

Better technology and economies of scale have cut the cost of making solar panels. Supply shifts to the right, the price falls and the quantity sold rises. Government subsidies in some countries have pushed supply further.

Exam use: technology and subsidies shifting the supply curve.

Drought and wheat

A drought cuts the wheat harvest, so supply shifts to the left and the price rises. Farmers who expect higher prices next year may store grain, which can reduce supply further.

Exam use: weather as a supply determinant and the role of expectations.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/22 · Q3(a) 8 marks Explain three reasons, associated with costs of production, why the supply curve for a particular market may shift to the right and consider the extent to which government microeconomic policy may also shift the supply curve for that market to the right.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): three explanations, such as a fall in wage rates, an increase in worker productivity, a fall in raw material costs, a fall in distribution costs or improved technology, each explaining why the supply curve shifts to the right (1 each).
  • Analysis (up to 3): government policy targeted at the market, such as subsidies, indirect taxes, price controls, regulation, provision of information or buffer stocks; at least one analysed for how it may shift supply to the right (for example a subsidy to education providers).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): the extent depends on the size of the subsidy, how firms respond and other factors.
PlanThree cost reasons, each linked to a right shift. Then a policy such as a subsidy, with its limits.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Supply could shift to the right if wage rates fall, because unit costs fall and firms are willing to supply more at each price. Greater worker productivity has a similar effect, since output per worker rises. A fall in raw material prices also lowers costs, so firms supply more at every price.

Government policy might also shift supply to the right. A subsidy for each unit lowers the producer's cost, so the supply curve moves to the right by the amount of the subsidy. A government could also reduce regulation or provide training grants.

However, the extent depends on the size of the subsidy and on whether firms pass the lower costs into more output, and a tax on the same market would work in the opposite direction.

Based on Feb/March 2020 · 9708/22 · Q2(a) 8 marks Explain what determines the change in equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity of a good when there is a rise in incomes in an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): income elasticity of demand (1) and how it is measured (1).
  • Application (up to 6): impact on equilibrium price and quantity for a normal good (2), a necessity (2) and an inferior good (2).
PlanIncome elasticity of demand decides the shift. Normal: right, price and quantity up. Necessity: small shift. Inferior: left.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Income elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of demand to a change in income, as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in income.

For a normal good, income elasticity is positive, so demand shifts to the right and both equilibrium price and quantity rise. For a necessity, the elasticity is positive but below one, so demand rises only a little and price and quantity increase slightly. For an inferior good, the elasticity is negative, so demand shifts to the left and both equilibrium price and quantity fall.

Top tips

  • Effective demand needs money. Willing and able. A want alone is not demand.
  • Market curve = horizontal sum. Add quantities at each price.
  • Price of the good moves along. Every other determinant shifts the curve.
  • Link every factor to cost or income. Say why the curve shifts, then which way.
  • Name normal and inferior. Rising income can lower demand for inferior goods.
  • Substitute up, complement down. A rise in a substitute's price raises demand for this good.
  • Supply shifts with costs. Lower costs, technology, subsidies and more firms shift supply right.
