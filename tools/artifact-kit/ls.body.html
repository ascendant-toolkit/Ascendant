The Supply of Labour, Wage Determination and Wage Differentials

Why people work the hours they do, what decides the number of workers in a job, how demand and supply set the wage, and why wages differ between jobs. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions, with mark scheme points.

This part in four sentences

An individual's labour supply balances pay against leisure: a higher wage has a substitution effect (work more) and an income effect (buy more leisure), so the supply of hours may bend backwards. The supply of labour to one occupation slopes upward because a higher wage attracts workers from other jobs. In a competitive market the wage is set where the demand for labour (MRP) meets the supply. Wages differ because demand and supply differ between jobs (skills, training, productivity), because jobs differ in non-wage advantages (compensating differences), and because of market imperfections.

This is part 2 of syllabus 8.3. Part 1 covers the demand for labour, part 3 economic rent, part 4 monopsony and trade unions, and part 5 the minimum wage.

1 · The individual's supply of labour

A worker divides time between work (which earns income) and leisure. The wage is the opportunity cost of leisure: each hour not worked gives up one hour's pay.

Substitution effect

When the wage rises, leisure becomes more expensive, so the worker may substitute work for leisure and supply more hours.

Income effect

When the wage rises, the worker earns more income for the same hours and may choose to buy more leisure (leisure is a normal good), supplying fewer hours.

At low wages the substitution effect usually dominates, so the supply curve slopes upward. At high wages the income effect may dominate, so hours supplied fall as the wage rises: a backward-bending supply curve of labour.

2 · The supply of labour to an occupation (the market)

A higher wage in one occupation attracts people from other occupations, from unemployment, and from outside the labour force, so the supply curve for an occupation slopes upward and does not bend back. How elastic it is depends on:

  • Skills and training time: the longer and more costly the training, the less elastic the supply (surgeons versus shop assistants).
  • Occupational and geographical mobility: if workers can move easily between jobs and areas, supply is more elastic.
  • Spare labour: unemployment makes supply more elastic.
  • Time: supply is more elastic in the long run, once people can retrain.

4 · Wage determination in a competitive market

The market wage is where the demand for labour (the sum of firms' MRP curves) meets the supply of labour. A rise in demand (higher product demand, higher productivity) raises the wage and employment; a rise in supply (more workers) lowers the wage and raises employment. Each firm is a wage taker and hires where MRP equals that wage (part 1).

5 · Why wages differ

Differences in demand and supply

  • High MRP: workers whose output is highly valued (a top surgeon, a star footballer) have high demand.
  • Scarce skills and long training: supply is small and inelastic, so the wage is high.
  • Jobs needing few skills have elastic supply, so wages stay low even if demand rises.

Compensating differences

Workers compare the net advantage of jobs: pay plus non-pay features (danger, unsocial hours, location, security, enjoyment). Unpleasant or risky jobs must pay more to attract workers, and pleasant jobs can pay less.

Net advantage = wage + non-wage benefits − non-wage costs

Market imperfections

  • Discrimination by gender, race or age keeps some groups' pay lower.
  • Imperfect information: workers do not know about better-paid jobs.
  • Immobility: geographical (housing, family) or occupational (lack of skills).
  • Trade unions and monopsony employers (part 4), and government pay-setting in the public sector.
  • Minimum wages (part 5).

6 · Evaluating the theory

  • The competitive model assumes identical workers, perfect mobility and perfect information. These rarely hold, so real wages often differ from the model.
  • MRP is hard to measure for jobs whose output is not sold (nurses, teachers) or is shared in a team (a chief executive).
  • Economic rent explains part of very high pay: see part 3.
  • Differences between workers in the same job in different firms may come from local supply, firm size, profitability or bargaining power.
  • Wage differentials may be fair or unfair: that is a value judgement (equity, syllabus 8.2).

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting figures.

Surgeons and shop assistants

Becoming a surgeon takes many years of costly training, so few people qualify and supply is small and inelastic. The skills have a high value to patients and hospitals, so demand is high. A shop assistant needs little training, so supply is large and elastic and the wage stays low.

Exam use: the standard wage differential: compare both markets on one pair of diagrams, with the MRP and the elasticity of supply.

Night shifts and offshore work

Night nurses and workers on oil platforms usually receive extra pay for unsocial hours or risk. Without it, fewer people would choose these jobs and supply would be too small.

Exam use: compensating differences or "net advantage": the premium makes the whole package equal to pleasanter jobs.

Migration and wages

When many workers move into a country or region, the supply of labour shifts right. On a standard diagram the wage in that market could fall, while more people are employed. If the migrants have different skills, the effect on existing workers depends on which jobs they compete for.

Exam use: the Oct/Nov 2021 data response asked for an MRP diagram of the effect of migration on wages. Draw the supply shift and the lower wage.

The gender pay gap

On average, women in many countries are paid less than men. Part of the gap reflects differences in occupation and hours, but some may reflect discrimination, which is a market imperfection.

Exam use: discrimination as a reason why wages differ from what the competitive model predicts.

Chief executive pay

The pay of the heads of large firms can be hundreds of times the pay of average staff. Supply of people able to run huge firms is small, and a good decision may add a very large amount to revenue (high MRP). Pay may also be set by the board the chief executive influences.

Exam use: the Oct/Nov 2020 25-mark question on bank bosses. Combine MRP, inelastic supply, and imperfections.

Teachers and nurses in the public sector

In many countries the government sets teachers' and nurses' pay nationally. It does not move automatically with local supply and demand, so shortages may appear in expensive areas.

Exam use: a case where pay is set by something other than the market, and why that can leave shortages or surpluses.

(ii) Same job, different firms: different MRP (firm productivity, product price), local supply, mobility and information. Then imperfections. Conclude: the theory explains much but not all.
KANE

The theory of wage determination says that the wage in a competitive labour market is set where the demand for labour meets the supply of labour. Demand comes from the marginal revenue product (MRP) of workers, which depends on their marginal physical product and the price the firm receives. Supply comes from workers' choices between work and leisure and the number of workers who choose each occupation.

The theory could account for the difference between a director and a general worker. A director's decisions can affect the revenue of the whole company, so the MRP is likely to be much higher. There are also few people with the skills, experience and training to be a director, so the supply of labour is small and inelastic. A high demand meeting an inelastic supply gives a high wage, and much of it may be economic rent. However, a director's MRP is very hard to measure because success depends on a team, and a director's pay is often set by a board that the director may influence, so it may not follow the competitive model closely.

The theory could also account for differences between two workers doing the same job in different companies. A programmer at a very productive firm that sells high-priced software has a higher MRP than a programmer at a firm with lower prices or lower productivity, so the first firm is likely to pay more. Local labour supply also matters: a firm in a city with few suitable workers may need to offer more. But in a perfectly competitive market workers could move to the higher-paying firm until wages equalised. Persistent differences suggest imperfections such as poor information, geographical immobility, discrimination, or a monopsony employer in one area.

Trade unions and minimum wage laws can also keep pay different from the market level, and workers may value non-wage benefits differently, so the same wage may not mean the same net advantage. Overall, the theory is likely to explain much of the difference between a director and a general worker through MRP and scarcity, and part of the difference between similar workers in different firms. The remaining differences are likely to come from market imperfections, so the theory is a useful but incomplete account.

Top tips

  • Explain both effects of a wage rise on hours. Substitution (work more) and income (more leisure). Say which dominates at low and at high wages.
  • Occupation supply is upward sloping. The backward-bending curve is for an individual's hours, not for a whole occupation.
  • Give the determinants of elasticity of supply: training time, mobility, spare labour, time period.
  • Separate shifts from movements. A change in the wage moves along; migration, training costs, attitudes and other wages shift.
  • Draw demand and supply for each occupation when asked about wage differentials. Mark the wage and employment in each.
  • Use "net advantage" for compensating differences. Pay plus non-pay features, not just the wage.
  • Name the imperfection: discrimination, imperfect information, immobility, trade unions, monopsony, minimum wage, government pay-setting.
  • Evaluate: the model assumes identical workers and perfect markets; MRP is hard to measure; "unjust" is a value judgement. Always conclude.
