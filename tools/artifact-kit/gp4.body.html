Government Failure and Choosing the Right Policy

Why intervention can leave society worse off, how to match each tool to the failure it fits, and how to weigh the free market against government action. Animated diagrams built from numbers, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Intervention is justified when a market fails, but government failure occurs when intervention leaves resources allocated no better, or worse. It has several causes: poor information, time lags, unintended consequences, the cost of administration and enforcement, and political pressure. Choosing the right tool means matching it to the type of failure and its cause and then weighing it against these risks. The final judgement compares the size of the market failure with the likely success and cost of the policy.

1 · Sources of government failure

Intervention can leave the economy worse off. The causes:

  • Information failure: the government cannot measure the external cost, so the tax is wrong.
  • Time lags between recognising a problem and the policy taking effect.
  • Unintended consequences: black markets, avoidance, new distortions.
  • Administration and enforcement costs.
  • Political pressure and regulatory capture: policy may serve special interests.

Always conclude by comparing the size of the market failure with the risk of government failure.

Four questions to ask before choosing the policy

  1. Is the problem on the production side or the consumption side?
  2. Is the good over-provided (negative externality, demerit good) or under-provided (positive externality, merit good, public good)?
  3. Does the government need to change the price, the quantity or what people know?
  4. Is a market-based tool enough (tax, subsidy, permit, property right), or does it need command and control (regulation, ban, quota, licence) or state provision?

How the tools group

GroupToolsHow it works
Market-basedIndirect taxes, subsidies, pollution permits, property rights, minimum and maximum pricesChange the price signal or ownership, and leave decisions to buyers and sellers
Command and controlRegulation, prohibitions, licences, production quotasSet a rule or limit that must be obeyed, with penalties
Ownership and provisionDirect provision, nationalisation, privatisationChange who supplies the good
PersuasionProvision of information, behavioural insights and nudgesChange what people know or how choices are presented, and leave choice free

A question on "how far a government should rely on market forces" is asking you to compare the first group with the others. Market-based tools tend to be cheaper to run and more flexible, but they need good estimates and may not stop the worst harm quickly. Command and control is certain and fast, but costly to enforce and blunt.

Wrong tool for the failure: the traps

  • A subsidy for a good with a negative externality moves output the wrong way.
  • A maximum price to cut consumption of a demerit good. It makes the good cheaper, so a shortage appears and consumption may not fall.
  • Information alone for a large pollution problem. Nudges work best alongside other policies.
  • A subsidy to private firms to supply a public good. Free riders can remain and the firm may still not produce it.
  • Privatising a natural monopoly and doing nothing else. Regulation is still needed.

4 · Free market or intervention?

The case for the free market

Prices carry information, so resources may flow to their most valued uses without anyone planning it. Profit gives firms an incentive to be efficient and to innovate, and consumers have choice. Intervention costs money and can be captured by special interests.

The case for intervention

Markets may ignore external costs and benefits, under-supply public goods, leave monopolies unchecked, suffer from poor information and produce an unequal distribution of income. In each case government action may raise welfare.

Almost every economy is mixed: the question is how much intervention, and of what kind. Good answers avoid "the market always works" and "the government always helps".

5 · A checklist for evaluating a policy

  • Effectiveness: does it deal with the cause of the failure, and by how much?
  • Cost: to the government (taxes, administration) and to those affected.
  • Side effects: black markets, avoidance, new distortions.
  • Fairness: who gains and loses, and is it regressive?
  • Time: how long before it works, and does the effect last?
  • Information: does the government know enough to set the right level?
  • Alternatives: would another tool do the job at lower cost?

8 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Sugar taxes and reformulation

Several countries have taxed soft drinks by sugar content. Some manufacturers cut the sugar in their drinks to avoid the tax, so revenue was lower than forecast and consumption of sugar fell, though the government raised less money than it expected.

Exam use: a policy that works better than expected on the aim but worse on revenue. Forecasting is hard, which is one cause of government failure.

Food subsidies and land clearance

Subsidies to grow fresh fruit and vegetables can encourage farmers to clear land. The merit good is encouraged, but habitats may be lost, an unintended external cost.

Exam use: an unintended consequence. A Paper 3 question asked which result of such a subsidy would be a government failure: the loss of wildlife.

Fuel subsidies

Some governments hold fuel prices low with subsidies. They can help poorer households but are often used mostly by richer households with more vehicles, they cost a lot, and they encourage use of fossil fuels.

Exam use: poor targeting and high cost as sources of government failure.

Rent control

Some cities cap rents below the market level. Existing tenants may gain, but the supply of rental housing could shrink and shortages, waiting lists and black market payments may follow.

Exam use: the standard maximum price example. Draw the ceiling below equilibrium, mark the shortage, then evaluate who gains and who loses.

B · Practice question with a model answer

Practice (not a past paper question) 12 marks Evaluate the view that government intervention in markets always makes society better off.
PlanSay when intervention helps (market failure) and give one example with a diagram or numbers. Then give causes of government failure and an example. Conclude with a judgement on "always".
KANE

Government intervention means actions such as taxes, subsidies, regulation and provision that aim to correct market failure and raise society's welfare.

Intervention could make society better off where the market fails. If a firm ignores an external cost, such as pollution, output is above the social optimum; a tax equal to the external cost could raise the firm's costs by that amount and move output towards the optimum, removing a welfare loss. Similarly, state provision of street lighting could supply a public good that private firms would not.

However, intervention may not always help. The government may lack the information to set the tax at the right level: a tax well above the external cost could cut output too far and cause a welfare loss as big as no tax at all. Policies may also have time lags, administration costs and unintended effects, such as a rubbish tax leading to fly-tipping. Overall, intervention is likely to improve welfare when the failure is large and the government is well informed, but it is unlikely to do so in every case, so the claim that it always makes society better off is too strong.

Top tips

  • Always include government failure in evaluation: poor information, time lags, unintended effects, cost, lobbying.
  • Give a specific cause, then a specific example. "Poor information, so the tax may be set too high" beats "the government may fail".
  • Compare the size of the market failure with the risk of government failure in your conclusion.
  • Test strong words: "always", "all", "neither possible nor desirable". Weigh both sides and conclude.
  • Name the failure before the policy. Production or consumption, over- or under-provided, then the tool (see the table in the Learn tab). A policy that does not fit the failure scores little.
  • Match tool to failure before evaluating: negative externality (tax, permits, regulation), positive externality or merit good (subsidy, provision), public good (provision), monopoly (regulation), information failure (labels, nudges).
  • Mention mixed economies. Real economies use markets and government together.
