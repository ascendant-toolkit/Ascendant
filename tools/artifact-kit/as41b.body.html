Gross National Income and Net National Income

4.1.2: how GDP becomes GNI through net primary income from abroad and NNI through capital consumption. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and past paper questions.

This part in five sentences

Gross National Income (GNI) is the income earned by a country's residents, wherever in the world it is earned: GNI = GDP + net primary income from abroad. Primary income is income from factors of production: wages of residents working abroad, and profits, interest and dividends from investments overseas; net primary income is what residents receive from abroad minus what is paid to foreign owners and workers at home. GNI is above GDP if a country receives more than it pays, and below GDP if foreign-owned firms send large profits home. Net National Income (NNI) is GNI minus capital consumption (depreciation), the value of capital worn out in producing the output. NNI is therefore the income that could be consumed in a year without reducing the economy's capacity to produce.

1 · From GDP to GNI

  • GDP measures output produced inside the country (including by foreign-owned firms). GNI measures the income of the country's residents (including from abroad).
  • Primary income received from abroad includes: wages of citizens working temporarily overseas, profits and dividends from firms owned overseas, and interest on foreign investments.
  • Primary income paid abroad includes: profits sent home by foreign-owned firms, interest paid on foreign loans, and wages paid to foreign workers.
  • Net primary income from abroad = income received − income paid. It can be positive or negative.
  • Secondary income (transfers such as aid and remittances sent as gifts) is a different part of the balance of payments and is not part of GNI as defined here.

2 · From GNI to NNI

  • Capital consumption (depreciation) is the value of capital goods worn out, damaged or made obsolete during the year.
  • NNI = GNI − capital consumption. It is the more accurate measure of income that can be spent without reducing the capital stock.
  • A gross figure counts all output, including that needed to replace worn-out capital. A net figure deducts it.
  • Because depreciation is hard to measure accurately, GDP and GNI are used more often in practice.

3 · Why the difference matters

  • Living standards: GNI (per head) shows the income actually available to residents, so it may be a better guide than GDP in countries with large foreign ownership or large overseas earnings.
  • Sustainability: NNI shows whether output is being used to replace capital. If capital consumption is high, a lot of production is only maintaining the existing capital stock.
  • Limits: primary income data can be unreliable; none of the measures covers inequality, the informal economy or the environment.
Exam tipWrite the steps: GDP + net primary income from abroad = GNI; GNI − capital consumption = NNI. Watch the sign of net income (it can be negative), and ignore items such as national debt, which are not in the calculation.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A country with many foreign factories

Overseas firms own large factories and send their profits home. GDP counts all the output made inside the country, but some income leaves, so net primary income from abroad is negative and GNI is smaller than GDP.

Exam use: GDP versus GNI and net primary income.

A country with many overseas workers

Many citizens work abroad and send wages home. Those earnings count as primary income from abroad, so GNI exceeds GDP.

Exam use: net primary income from abroad being positive.

A country that has invested overseas

A country whose firms and investors own assets abroad receives profits, interest and dividends, which raise GNI above GDP.

Exam use: primary income from investment abroad.

Replacing worn-out machines

A firm spends $10m a year replacing machinery that wears out. National income after allowing for this wear and tear is lower than the gross figure, which is why NNI is lower than GNI.

Exam use: capital consumption.

Top tips

  • GNI is about residents. Where the income is earned does not matter.
  • Net primary income. Received from abroad minus paid abroad; may be negative.
  • NNI subtracts depreciation. Capital consumption.
  • Write the steps. GDP, then GNI, then NNI.
  • Ignore irrelevant items. National debt is not in the calculation.
  • Say what it shows. NNI: income that can be spent without running down capital.
  • Compare with GDP. Foreign ownership lowers GNI; overseas earnings raise it.
