Policy Conflicts and Government Failure in Macroeconomic Policy

10.3.2 to 10.3.3: the problems and conflicts that arise from macroeconomic policies, and the causes of government failure in macroeconomic policy. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Policies aimed at one objective often damage another: cutting inflation may raise unemployment, faster growth may worsen the current account, and tighter environmental rules may slow output. These conflicts mean governments choose trade-offs and rarely meet every objective at once. Government failure in macroeconomic policy happens when intervention makes things worse or fails to improve them, because of time lags, poor information and forecasting, political motives, wrong theories, crowding out and unintended effects. A strong answer names the conflict or failure, shows the mechanism and judges how serious it is.

Conflicts in the policy mix

Governments sometimes use policies that pull in opposite directions. A tax cut (expansionary fiscal) together with higher interest rates (contractionary monetary) may give higher demand from consumers but dearer borrowing for firms and a stronger currency: investment and exports could fall while inflation is only partly controlled. Coordination between fiscal and monetary authorities matters.

Can a government meet all objectives at once?

  • Usually not in the short run. The Phillips curve, the growth-inflation link and the growth-current account link all imply trade-offs.
  • Sometimes in the long run: supply-side improvements that shift LRAS can raise growth and cut unemployment without inflation. Good luck (falling world prices), credibility and flexible markets can help.
  • It depends on spare capacity: with a large negative output gap, expansion helps growth, jobs and has few costs.

2 · Government failure in macroeconomic policy (10.3.3)

Government failure occurs when intervention fails to achieve its aim or leaves the economy worse off than if the government had done nothing.

CauseHow it leads to failure
Time lagsRecognition, decision, implementation and effect lags mean policy takes effect after the problem has changed, so it may be pro-cyclical.
Poor information and forecastingOutput gaps, the natural rate and multipliers cannot be measured exactly, so policy may be too weak or too strong.
Political motivesA political business cycle: expansion before elections, cuts afterwards; short-termism; pressure from interest groups such as unions.
Wrong model or theoryA policy based on a Laffer curve peak or on a stable Phillips curve may not produce the expected effect.
Crowding outGovernment borrowing raises interest rates and cuts private investment.
ExpectationsIf people anticipate policy, behaviour changes and the policy's effect is smaller or cancelled (rational expectations).
Unintended consequencesPrivatisation creates private monopolies; benefit cuts increase poverty; protectionism triggers retaliation.
Lack of credibility and time inconsistencyIf governments are known to change policy, announcements do not change expectations.
External constraintsGlobal shocks, capital flows and the exchange rate limit what a single government can do.
Evaluating government failureIt does not follow that intervention should stop: markets also fail, and doing nothing in a deep recession can be costly. Better design reduces failure: independent central banks, rules or targets for inflation and debt, automatic stabilisers (no lags), transparent forecasts and policy tested against evidence. Compare the cost of failure with the cost of the original problem.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

The UK 'Lawson boom' and bust, 1980s

Tax cuts and low interest rates fuelled rapid growth and falling unemployment in the late 1980s, but inflation and the current account deficit rose. Interest rates were then raised sharply and the economy fell into recession in 1990 to 1992.

Exam use: a conflict between growth, inflation and the current account, and policy mistakes caused by misjudging the output gap.

Austerity in the euro area after 2010

Several governments cut spending and raised taxes to reduce debt. Output fell, unemployment rose and debt ratios rose in some countries because GDP was falling. Critics said the multiplier had been underestimated.

Exam use: fiscal consolidation conflicting with growth and jobs, and a forecasting failure about the multiplier.

Tax cuts and rate rises together

In 2018 the US cut taxes (raising demand) while the central bank raised interest rates (reducing demand). The mix raised the dollar and worsened the trade deficit even as growth stayed strong.

Exam use: a policy mix that pulls in two directions, and a conflict with the current account.

Elections and the political business cycle

Governments have been accused of cutting taxes or raising spending just before elections, then tightening afterwards. Economists have found some evidence in some countries, but it is hard to separate from other causes.

Exam use: a source of government failure from political motives. Say that independent central banks reduce the problem for monetary policy.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/42 · Q5 25 marks A government cuts taxes at the same time as its central bank announces that it will raise interest rates. Discuss how these policies may cause conflicts for the government in trying to achieve its macroeconomic aims.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Name the aims: growth, inflation, unemployment, the balance of payments and development.
  • Tax cut: higher disposable income, consumption and investment, higher government borrowing; the multiplier raises output and jobs but may raise inflation and imports.
  • Higher interest rates: lower consumption and investment, more attractive for savers, a higher exchange rate which cuts export competitiveness and import prices.
  • Conflicts: demand pushed up by one policy and down by the other; growth and the current account; inflation and investment. Level 4 (18 to 25) needs at least three aims, both policies developed and a conclusion (maximum 21 without one).
PlanThree aims. Tax cut route. Interest rate route. Show the two pull against each other. Conclude on which effect dominates.
Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/43 · Q7 25 marks A lender such as the IMF lends to developing countries but requires them to reduce import restrictions, focus on exporting primary goods and accept a devaluation. Assess whether developing countries should continue to borrow from it.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Examine each condition: removing import restrictions could worsen the balance of payments and hurt infant industries; focusing on primary goods exposes the country to unstable prices and low productivity; devaluation could cause cost-push inflation.
  • Benefits of the loan: finance for investment, stability, a confidence signal. Weigh the negative effects against the benefits.
  • Level 4 (18 to 25) needs analysis of each requirement, an evaluation of their combined effect, comment on the benefits and a conclusion.
PlanThree conditions, each with benefit and cost. Benefits of the loan. Weigh them and conclude for a typical developing economy.
Based on Feb/March 2022 · 9708/42 · Q7(b) 13 marks Discuss whether there is likely to be a conflict when a government tries to fix its exchange rate while introducing policies to reduce inflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explain a fixed exchange rate and how the central bank defends it: buy the currency with reserves if it is falling, sell it if it is rising. Interest rates then have to serve the exchange rate.
  • Conflict: reducing inflation requires higher interest rates or tight demand, which attracts capital and pushes the currency up, so the central bank must sell currency (expanding the money supply) or accept a revaluation; if the currency is under pressure, defending it requires higher rates, which may conflict with growth.
  • Credit that a fixed rate can help cut inflation by importing low inflation from the anchor currency, so conflict is not certain.
PlanDefine the fixed rate. Interest rate dilemma. Case where there is no conflict. Judge.
Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/42 · Q7(b) 13 marks Discuss whether monetary policy alone is sufficient for a government to achieve its macroeconomic aims at the same time.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Identify the aims: employment, inflation, growth and the balance of payments. Explain monetary policy and its limitations (lags, liquidity trap, conflicts).
  • Impact of monetary policy changes on at least two aims, for example higher interest rates cut inflation but raise unemployment.
  • Compare an alternative: fiscal policy or supply-side policy, which can address problems monetary policy cannot (structural unemployment, long-run growth).
  • Level 4 (9 to 13) needs analysis of monetary policy on two aims, comparison with an alternative and an evaluation of the statement.
PlanMonetary policy on two aims. Its limits. Alternative policy. Judge: one instrument rarely meets several aims.
Practice (not a past paper question) 12 marks Explain why government intervention in the macroeconomy may fail to achieve its aims.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Time lags: policy takes effect after the problem has changed and may add to the cycle.
  • Poor information: forecasting errors about the output gap, the natural rate and the multiplier.
  • Political motives: policies timed for elections or influenced by interest groups.
  • Other causes: crowding out, expectations, unintended consequences, external constraints.
  • Evaluation: markets also fail, and automatic stabilisers, independent central banks and rules reduce the problem.
PlanThree causes, each with a mechanism. A limit on the argument: government failure is not a reason to do nothing.

Government failure in macroeconomic policy occurs when intervention fails to achieve its objective or makes the situation worse than it would have been without it. One cause is time lags. It takes time to recognise a problem, decide on a response and for the policy to work, so a stimulus decided in a recession may arrive during the recovery and push inflation up, making the cycle worse. A second cause is poor information: the output gap and the natural rate cannot be observed, so a government that overestimates spare capacity may stimulate demand too much, leading to inflation.

A third cause is political motivation. Governments may cut taxes or raise spending before an election and tighten afterwards, which creates a political business cycle that does not reflect the needs of the economy. Policies can also fail because of crowding out, where higher government borrowing raises interest rates and reduces private investment, and because people's expectations may offset policies, such as workers who raise wage demands when they expect inflation. However, government failure does not mean that intervention should be avoided, because markets can also fail and a deep recession can be costly. Independent central banks, rules for inflation and the use of automatic stabilisers, which have no lag, can reduce the risk of failure.

Top tips

  • Name the conflict explicitly. For example: reducing inflation with higher interest rates conflicts with growth and employment.
  • Use the Phillips curve and the AD and AS model. They show conflicts: AD policy moves along the SRPC; AS policy can help both.
  • Say it depends on spare capacity. With a negative output gap the conflict is smaller; near capacity it is larger.
  • Show both policies in a mix. Explain what each does and how they pull against each other.
  • For government failure, give mechanisms. Time lags, forecasting errors, political motives, crowding out, expectations, unintended effects.
  • Evaluate: market failure too. Government failure does not mean that doing nothing is better.
  • Conclude on a mix. A set of policies matched to the causes usually works better than one instrument.
