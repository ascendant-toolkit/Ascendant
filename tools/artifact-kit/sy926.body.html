Sustainable Economic Growth

9.2.6: what sustainable growth means, using and conserving resources, the environmental cost of growth and climate change, and policies to reduce it. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Sustainable economic growth is growth that meets the needs of people today without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs: in terms of the earlier definitions, it raises productive potential without reducing future productive potential. It depends on how resources are used and conserved, because non-renewable resources run out and renewable ones can be harvested faster than they regrow. Growth can damage the environment and contribute to climate change through pollution, emissions and loss of habitats, which are negative externalities. Governments can respond with taxes, tradable permits, subsidies, regulation and international agreements, each with costs and limits.

1 · What sustainable growth means

  • Intergenerational equity: fairness between present and future generations. Using up natural capital for today's output may leave less for later.
  • Productive potential: growth is sustainable if the stock of capital (including natural capital such as forests, fish stocks, soil and a stable climate) is maintained or replaced.
  • GDP is not enough: GDP does not subtract depletion or pollution. Measures such as MEW (Measure of Economic Welfare) and green GDP adjust for environmental costs and unpaid work.
Sustainable growth versus sustainable developmentSustainable growth is about real output over time without eroding future capacity. Sustainable development is wider: long-run improvement in living standards (health, education, equity) without damage to the environment.

2 · Using and conserving resources

Non-renewable

Fossil fuels and minerals. Once used they are gone, so extraction today reduces what is left. Sustainability needs substitutes, recycling, efficiency and investment in other forms of capital.

Renewable

Fish, forests, soil, water. They regenerate, so a sustainable yield exists: harvest no more than regrowth. Overfishing and deforestation harvest faster than regrowth, so the stock collapses.

  • Why markets overuse resources: the full social cost is not paid (external costs), property rights are weak (the tragedy of the commons), and people discount the future.
  • Conserving resources: quotas and licences, property rights, recycling and reuse, efficiency standards, renewable energy and technology that cuts resource use per unit of output.

3 · Growth, the environment and climate change

Cost of growthLink to economic theory
Greenhouse gas emissions and climate changeA negative externality: the social cost of carbon is not in the market price, so output is above the social optimum (resource misallocation).
Air, water and land pollutionExternal costs fall on third parties; MSC is above MPC.
Deforestation, loss of biodiversityNatural capital is used up; the habitat has no market price.
Congestion and urban sprawlCongestion is a classic negative externality.

But growth also gives resources and technology to protect the environment: richer countries can afford cleaner technology and regulation. Some argue that pollution first rises then falls as income per head rises (an environmental Kuznets curve), though this is not automatic, and the poorest countries often bear the worst effects of climate change.

4 · Policies to reduce the impact of growth on the environment

PolicyHow it worksEvaluation
Carbon tax / pollution taxRaises the private cost to the social cost, so output falls to the efficient levelHard to set the right rate, may be regressive, may hurt competitiveness, but raises revenue
Tradable permits (emissions trading)Caps total emissions; firms buy and sell the right to emitCap is certain but the permit price varies; needs monitoring; cap may be set too high
Subsidies for renewables and R&DLower the cost of clean energy and technologyCost to taxpayers, may back the wrong technology, does not penalise polluters
Regulation (emission limits, bans)Directly sets limits on pollution or activitiesCertain outcome but inflexible, costly to enforce, no incentive to do better than the limit
Property rights and conservationOwners have an incentive to preserve the resource; protected areas, quotasHard to apply to the atmosphere or oceans
International agreementsCountries commit to emission cuts (for example the Paris Agreement)Free-rider problem, enforcement is weak, developing countries argue rich countries should do more
Education and information, nudgesChange behaviour and preferencesSlow, limited effect on its own
Evaluation checklistCost of implementation, environmental effectiveness, effect on production costs and jobs, fairness (regressive effects on poorer households), international spillovers and free riding, time lags and political feasibility.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Overfishing and the collapse of the Grand Banks cod

Heavy fishing off Newfoundland caught fish faster than they could breed. The cod stock collapsed in the early 1990s and the fishery was closed, leaving thousands without work. Recovery has been slow.

Exam use: a renewable resource harvested above the sustainable yield. Use it for intergenerational equity, weak property rights and the case for quotas.

The EU Emissions Trading System

Large emitters in the EU must hold permits for each tonne of carbon dioxide. The number of permits is capped and falls over time; firms can trade them. The permit price has varied widely.

Exam use: tradable permits as a market-based policy. Evaluate on certainty of the cap against uncertainty of the price, and on carbon leakage if firms move abroad.

Renewable energy subsidies

Governments have supported solar and wind power through subsidies and guaranteed prices. Costs have fallen sharply as output has grown, and the share of renewables in electricity has risen in many countries.

Exam use: subsidies to speed up clean technology. Evaluate: cost to taxpayers, risk of backing the wrong technology, and no penalty on polluters.

The Paris Agreement and free riders

Almost all countries agreed to limit warming, but each sets its own targets and there are no strong penalties. A country gains from others cutting emissions even if it does not, which weakens commitment.

Exam use: international action and the free-rider problem. Mention that poorer countries argue that richer ones caused most past emissions.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/42 · Q4(a) 12 marks Explain how climate change may be seen as a result of resource misallocation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Burning fossil fuels creates a negative externality: the social cost of emissions (climate damage, health costs) is not paid by producers or consumers.
  • Show with marginal private cost and marginal social cost: the market equilibrium is where MPC meets demand, and output is above the social optimum where MSC meets demand. This is overproduction and misallocation.
  • Level 4 (9 to 12) links externalities, private and social costs and benefits, and efficiency. A labelled diagram strengthens the answer.
  • Add that missing property rights over the atmosphere and the long time horizon make the problem worse.
PlanDefine the externality. Draw MPC, MSC and demand. Show the gap and the welfare loss. Link to misallocation.

Top tips

  • Define sustainable growth in terms of future generations. Meeting needs now without reducing the ability to meet future needs, or raising potential without reducing future potential.
  • Use the externality diagram for climate questions. MPC, MSC and demand. Market output is above the social optimum. A tax shifts MPC to MSC.
  • Separate renewable and non-renewable. Renewable resources have a sustainable yield. Non-renewable resources are depleted whatever the rate, so substitutes and recycling matter.
  • Name two policies and compare them. A market-based tool (tax or permits) and a regulatory or subsidy tool. Analyse each then judge.
  • Mention the global problem. Climate change crosses borders, so free riding and fairness between rich and poor countries are key evaluation points.
  • Include the costs of policy. Higher prices, jobs lost in polluting industries, effect on poorer households, competitiveness.
  • Growth is not always the enemy. Richer countries may afford cleaner technology. Say growth could be made more sustainable, not just that it harms the environment.
