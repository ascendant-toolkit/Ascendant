Maximum and Minimum Prices

3.1.3 and 3.2.4: why governments control prices and the effects of maximum and minimum prices, including shortages, surpluses, black markets and government buying. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

A maximum price (price ceiling) is a legal limit on how high a price can go, used to make essentials affordable; to be effective it must be set below the equilibrium price, which creates excess demand (a shortage), so goods have to be rationed and a black market may appear. A minimum price (price floor) is a legal limit on how low a price can fall, used to protect producers' incomes or discourage consumption of demerit goods; to be effective it must be above the equilibrium price, which creates excess supply (a surplus) that the government may need to buy or that wastes resources. In both cases the price mechanism stops clearing the market, some consumers or producers gain and others lose, and total surplus usually falls.

1 · Maximum prices (3.2.4)

  • Reasons (3.1.3): make food, housing (rent control) or fuel affordable for low-income households; limit inflation in key goods; prevent price gouging in an emergency.
  • Effect: quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls, so there is a shortage. The quantity traded is the smaller amount supplied.
  • Problems: rationing by queues, waiting lists or favouritism; black markets where goods sell above the legal price; lower quality; less incentive to supply, and in the long run less investment (for example fewer rented homes).
  • Winners and losers: consumers who still get the good pay less; consumers who cannot buy and producers lose.
  • A maximum price at or above the equilibrium has no effect.

2 · Minimum prices

  • Reasons (3.1.3): support farmers' incomes and ensure the long-run supply of food; discourage consumption of demerit goods such as alcohol; a minimum wage is a minimum price for labour.
  • Effect: quantity supplied rises and quantity demanded falls, so there is a surplus. The quantity traded is the smaller amount demanded.
  • Problems: the government may need to buy up the surplus (cost to taxpayers) or destroy or export it; consumers pay more and consumer surplus falls; producers may become inefficient; the policy may encourage illegal selling below the minimum.
  • Producers gain if demand is inelastic, because revenue rises; if demand is elastic, sales fall by more and some producers lose.
  • A minimum price at or below the equilibrium has no effect.

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Rent control

A cap on rents below the market level makes housing cheaper for tenants who find a home. But landlords supply fewer homes and spend less on upkeep, so there is a shortage, long waiting lists and sometimes illegal side payments.

Exam use: a maximum price, shortage, rationing and a black market.

Minimum alcohol price

A minimum price per unit of alcohol targets the cheapest, strongest drinks. It cuts consumption among heavy drinkers if demand is elastic, but if demand is inelastic spending rises and producers gain.

Exam use: a minimum price on a demerit good and the role of elasticity.

Farm price support

A guaranteed minimum price for grain encourages farmers to produce. The state must buy or store the surplus, which costs taxpayers and may be dumped abroad at low prices.

Exam use: a surplus and the cost of buying it.

Minimum wage

A legal minimum wage is a price floor in the labour market. Low-paid workers who keep their jobs gain, but if the minimum is above the equilibrium wage employers may hire fewer workers.

Exam use: a minimum price applied to labour.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/21 · Q3(a) 8 marks With the aid of a diagram, explain the consequences for consumers and producers of introducing a minimum price in the market for a product.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): a correct diagram with price and quantity axes labelled (1); a minimum price shown above the equilibrium, with demand and supply labelled (1).
  • Application (up to 3): consequences for consumers, who pay a higher price, which discourages consumption and is beneficial if the good is a demerit good.
  • Application (up to 3): consequences for producers, whose incomes may rise, but who face excess supply because consumers do not buy at the higher price, so government action may be needed to buy up or restrict the surplus.
PlanDiagram: minimum price above equilibrium. Consumers pay more, buy less. Producers: higher price but a surplus, government may buy it.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A minimum price is a legal floor below which the price cannot fall. On a diagram it is a horizontal line above the equilibrium price, where quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded.

Consumers face a higher price than in the free market, so quantity demanded falls, which would be a benefit if the product were a demerit good such as alcohol, but a cost to consumers who lose consumer surplus. Producers receive a higher price, which may raise their incomes if demand is inelastic, but not all they supply is bought, so there is excess supply. The government may have to buy the surplus, restrict output or accept waste.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/24 · Q3(a) 8 marks With the help of a diagram, explain the impact of introducing an effective minimum price for a product and consider the effect on the consumer surplus for that product.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): an accurately drawn supply and demand diagram (1); a minimum price above the equilibrium, with supply exceeding demand (1); an explanation that this leads to a surplus in the market (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): what is meant by consumer surplus (1); use of the diagram to show the fall in consumer surplus (1); an explanation of why consumer surplus will fall (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): the overall effect on consumer surplus is that it will fall, but the extent depends on factors such as price elasticity of demand, with a justified conclusion (1).
PlanDiagram with a minimum price above equilibrium. Surplus. Consumer surplus: smaller triangle. Evaluate: depends on PED.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

On a supply and demand diagram, an effective minimum price is set above the equilibrium price. At that price quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, creating a surplus.

Consumer surplus is the difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they pay. The higher price and the fall in quantity bought shrink the consumer surplus triangle on the diagram, so consumers are worse off.

Consumer surplus will fall, but by how much depends on the price elasticity of demand: if demand is inelastic, quantity falls little but consumers pay much more, while if it is elastic, quantity falls a lot. So the extent of the loss depends on elasticity and on the level of the minimum price.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/21 · Q3(b) 12 marks Governments sometimes fix maximum prices on goods for different reasons. Assess whether the use of a maximum price is always advantageous.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): advantages, such as limiting the price of essential foods, making them more affordable and reducing poverty (up to 4); disadvantages, such as excess demand in the market, queuing or rationing, and the creation of a black or illegal market (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): the balance of advantages and disadvantages, the effect on producers and consumers, and a conclusion on whether the policy will be effective and always advantageous.
PlanAdvantages: affordability, poverty. Disadvantages: shortage, rationing, black market. Evaluate: who gains, whether it works.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A maximum price below equilibrium can make essentials such as bread or rent more affordable, which helps low-income households and could reduce poverty. But it cuts the incentive to supply and raises quantity demanded, so there is excess demand. Goods must be rationed by queues or waiting lists, and a black market may appear in which the goods sell far above the legal price, harming the poorest.

A maximum price is not always advantageous. Those who obtain the good gain, but producers lose, and those who are unable to buy are worse off. It works best as a short-term measure in an emergency, while alternatives such as subsidies or income support might be better in the long run.

Based on Feb/March 2023 · 9708/22 · Q3(b) 12 marks With the help of a diagram, assess whether the introduction of a minimum price in a market can be justified.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): a correctly labelled diagram of a minimum price above the equilibrium; the advantages, such as making demerit goods dearer to discourage consumption and protecting producers' incomes; the disadvantages and limitations, such as excess supply, reduced efficiency of producers, and why a minimum price might be ineffective. A missing diagram caps at Level 2, and a one-sided response also caps at Level 2.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): an evaluation of the potential effectiveness of a minimum price, which may include alternative policies if the advantages and disadvantages have been discussed.
PlanDiagram. Advantages: cut demerit use, support incomes. Disadvantages: surplus, inefficiency, may be ineffective. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

On the diagram a minimum price lies above the equilibrium price. It can be justified for a demerit good, because the higher price should reduce consumption, and for farm goods, because it protects producers' incomes and secures long-run supply. But at that price there is excess supply, which the government might have to buy, and producers may become less efficient because they are protected. The policy could also be ineffective if demand is inelastic, since people keep buying, or if illegal sales appear below the minimum.

A minimum price can be justified where the aim is clear and demand is elastic enough to change behaviour, but taxes or information may do the job at lower cost. The overall justification depends on the good and on how well the price is enforced.

Top tips

  • Put the line on the right side. Maximum below equilibrium, minimum above it.
  • Say effective or not. A control on the wrong side has no effect.
  • Name the result. Maximum: shortage. Minimum: surplus.
  • Quantity traded is the smaller side. Maximum: Qs. Minimum: Qd.
  • Give winners and losers. Consumers and producers are affected differently.
  • Add an unintended consequence. Black market, rationing, government buying, waste.
  • Evaluate with elasticity. Inelastic demand: a minimum price raises producers' revenue.
