Monetary Policy: Effects and Limitations

5.3.3 to 5.3.4: expansionary and contractionary policy in AD/AS analysis, which problems it suits, and its limits. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and past paper questions.

This part in five sentences

Expansionary monetary policy (lower interest rates, a larger money supply, looser credit) raises consumption, investment and sometimes net exports, so AD shifts right and real output, employment and the price level rise; contractionary monetary policy (higher rates, a smaller money supply, tighter credit) does the reverse, so AD shifts left and inflation falls at the cost of lower output and employment. It is used to control inflation (contractionary), to end deflation or support recovery from a recession (expansionary), and to keep growth steady. How well it works depends on how responsive spending is to interest rates, confidence and expectations, time lags, the cause of the problem, the exchange rate and whether rates are already close to zero. It is best judged against fiscal and supply-side alternatives, and against the effects on different groups and on other objectives.

2 · Using AD and AS diagrams

  • Contractionary: AD shifts left along SRAS, so the price level and real output fall. This lowers demand-pull inflation, but also lowers output and employment in the short run.
  • Expansionary: AD shifts right. If there is spare capacity (the flat part of the AS curve), output rises with little effect on prices; near full capacity (the steep part), mostly the price level rises.
  • Long run: monetary policy shifts AD, not LRAS, so in the long run it cannot permanently raise output; sustained growth needs supply-side policies. Stable, low inflation can create a good environment for investment.

Housing bubbleTighter credit regulationTargets lending directly rather than all spending

4 · Evaluating monetary policy

  • Strengths: quick to decide and adjust, often independent of politics, can be changed in small steps, works through the market, and does not need the government to borrow or tax.
  • Time lags: it can take 12 to 18 months or more for a rate change to affect spending and inflation fully.
  • Responsiveness: if households and firms are not sensitive to interest rates (interest-inelastic), or many borrow at fixed rates, the effect is smaller.
  • Confidence and expectations: pessimistic households will not borrow even when rates are low; if people expect inflation to rise, they may spend sooner, offsetting higher rates.
  • Zero lower bound: rates cannot fall much below zero, so other tools such as QE may be needed.
  • Exchange rate: higher rates may raise the currency and hurt exporters; lower rates may weaken it and raise import costs.
  • Unequal effects: borrowers and savers, indebted firms and exporters are affected differently.
  • Conflicts: reducing inflation may cost growth and jobs, and expansion may risk inflation and asset bubbles. Compare with fiscal policy (more targeted but slower to decide) and supply-side policy (long-term).
Exam tipGive the full chain: base rate rises, borrowing dearer and saving more attractive, consumption and investment fall, AD shifts left, inflation falls. Evaluate with time lags, responsiveness, the cause of inflation (cost-push is harder to cure) and the effects on growth and unemployment, then compare with another policy.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Raising rates to fight inflation

Inflation is well above target, so the central bank raises its base rate. Mortgage and loan costs rise, households cut spending and firms delay investment, AD falls, and inflation eases, though growth may slow and unemployment rise.

Exam use: contractionary monetary policy.

Rates cut but nobody borrows

After a financial crisis rates fall to near zero, but households fear job losses and banks lend cautiously, so spending barely rises. This shows the limits of monetary policy when confidence is low.

Exam use: why monetary policy may be ineffective.

Cost-push inflation from energy prices

When inflation comes from higher energy costs, raising rates reduces spending but does not lower energy prices. Output and jobs may fall while inflation stays high.

Exam use: why monetary policy suits demand-pull better than cost-push inflation.

Deflation and falling prices

When prices fall, consumers delay purchases in expectation of lower prices. Lower interest rates may not change this, so governments may also use spending or QE.

Exam use: limits of expansionary policy in deflation.

Time lags in practice

A central bank raises rates today, but many borrowers are on fixed-rate loans that reset only months later, so the full effect takes a year or more.

Exam use: time lags.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/23 · Q5(a) 8 marks Explain how monetary policy may be able to reduce the rate of inflation in an economy and consider the likely success of this policy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): an understanding of monetary policy (1); the tools of monetary policy: interest rates, the supply of money and regulations (1); the nature of a contractionary monetary policy (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): an explanation of how the tools can be applied to reduce inflation (1); analysis of the changes on aggregate demand and the price level (1); analysis of the effects on output and employment (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): the effectiveness depends on the response of consumers and firms, in particular to higher interest rates (1), and may also depend on the rate of inflation and its causes (1), with a justified conclusion (1 reserved).
PlanDefine monetary policy and its tools. Contractionary: higher rates, AD falls, price level falls, output and jobs fall. Evaluate: responsiveness, causes of inflation.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Monetary policy is the use of interest rates, the money supply and credit regulation, normally by the central bank, to influence the economy. A contractionary policy involves raising interest rates, restricting the money supply or tightening credit.

Higher interest rates make borrowing dearer and saving more attractive, so consumption and investment fall and AD shifts to the left. The price level falls, reducing inflation, but real output and employment also fall in the short run.

How successful it is depends on how strongly consumers and firms respond to higher rates and on the cause of inflation. It works best against demand-pull inflation; against cost-push inflation it may cut output without lowering costs, so it may be less successful.

Based on Feb/March 2024 · 9708/22 · Q5(a) 8 marks With the help of an aggregate demand and aggregate supply diagram, explain how a decrease in interest rates could cause economic growth and consider whether economic growth will always result in inflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): a clear understanding of what is meant by economic growth (1), inflation (1) and interest rates (1), and an accurate diagram with correctly labelled axes and the equilibrium price level and real GDP (1; a maximum of 3 marks in total).
  • Analysis (up to 3): using the diagram to explain how a decrease in interest rates affects saving and borrowing (1), causing an increase in consumption, investment or net exports (1), which causes an increase in economic growth or real GDP (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a rise in inflation assumes the economy is operating at full capacity; the growth may be focused on capital-intensive industries, or the source of the growth may be long term in nature (1); the second mark is for a justified conclusion.
PlanDiagram: AD right. Lower rates: less saving, more borrowing, C and I up, real GDP up. Evaluate: only inflation if near full capacity; supply-side growth does not.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Economic growth is an increase in real GDP. Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. The interest rate is the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving. The AD/AS diagram has the price level and real GDP on the axes, with AD, SRAS and the equilibrium shown.

A decrease in interest rates reduces the reward for saving and makes borrowing cheaper, so consumption and investment rise, and a weaker currency may raise net exports. AD shifts right on the diagram, so real GDP increases: economic growth.

Growth will not always cause inflation. If there is spare capacity, output can rise with little change in prices, and if the growth comes from improved productivity, SRAS and LRAS also shift right, holding prices down. Inflation is more likely when the economy is near full capacity.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/23 · Q4(b) 12 marks Discuss whether a policy of increasing interest rates is the best way of controlling a rising rate of inflation in an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): how higher interest rates reduce AD and so inflation: dearer borrowing, more saving, lower consumption and investment, a stronger exchange rate lowering import prices and net exports (up to 4); at least one alternative, such as contractionary fiscal policy or supply-side policy, with how it would work, and the limits of each (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether higher interest rates are the best method, depending on the cause of inflation, time lags, responsiveness and costs to growth and unemployment, with a conclusion.
PlanRates up: borrowing dearer, AD left. Limits: lags, expectations, cost-push. Alternatives: fiscal, supply-side. Judge by cause of inflation.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A rise in interest rates makes borrowing more expensive and saving more attractive, so consumption and investment fall and AD shifts left, reducing demand-pull inflation. A higher exchange rate may also reduce import prices. But the effect takes time, households with fixed-rate loans may not respond, and higher rates raise firms' costs and slow growth. Contractionary fiscal policy could cut AD more directly, and supply-side policy could address cost-push inflation by raising productivity, but each has its own costs and lags.

Raising interest rates is probably the most flexible tool against demand-pull inflation, but it is less effective against cost-push inflation, so it is not always the best. The most effective response probably combines monetary policy with supply-side measures, depending on the cause.

Based on Feb/March 2020 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Discuss whether monetary policy measures are always effective in correcting a high rate of inflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): monetary policy must be deflationary: a reduction in the money supply, a rise in the rate of interest or a rise in the exchange rate, aiming to control AD (up to 4); reasons why it might be ineffective, such as time lags, consumers' and firms' limited response, expectations of inflation and cost-push causes (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether monetary policy is always effective, with a conclusion.
PlanMeasures: interest rates, money supply, exchange rate. Limits: lags, response, expectations, cost-push. Judge: not always effective.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Monetary policy tackles inflation by controlling AD: a rise in interest rates or a reduction in the money supply makes credit dearer and reduces spending, and a higher exchange rate lowers import prices and demand for exports. But it may be ineffective if consumers and firms do not respond to interest rates, if there are time lags, if people expect inflation to continue, or if inflation is caused by rising costs rather than strong demand.

Monetary policy is therefore not always effective. It works best against demand-pull inflation when the central bank is credible, but it can be slow and costly for growth, and other policies may be needed for cost-push inflation.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Discuss whether an expansionary monetary policy will be successful in correcting deflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): how expansionary monetary policy works against deflation: lower interest rates, more money and credit, raising consumption, investment and AD, and so the price level (up to 4); why it may fail, such as consumers expecting prices to fall further, low confidence, banks unwilling to lend, interest rates already near zero (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether it will succeed, taking into account the cause of deflation, confidence and alternative policies such as fiscal stimulus, with a conclusion.
PlanLow rates, more money: AD up. Limits: expectations, low confidence, rates near zero. Alternatives: fiscal policy. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Expansionary monetary policy cuts interest rates and increases the money supply, so borrowing becomes cheaper, consumption and investment rise, AD shifts right and the price level rises, ending deflation. But in deflation consumers expect prices to fall further and delay spending, banks may be reluctant to lend, and interest rates may already be close to zero so cannot be cut further, so the policy may not work.

Expansionary monetary policy is more likely to succeed if confidence is restored and banks lend, but in a deep deflation it may need to be combined with fiscal policy, such as higher government spending, to raise AD directly.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/23 · Q3(b) 12 marks Discuss whether a contractionary monetary policy is the best way to reduce the rate of inflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the advantages and disadvantages of monetary policy in reducing inflation (up to 4; a maximum of 3 if only advantages or only disadvantages); and of at least one other policy, such as fiscal or supply-side policy (up to 4 per policy; a maximum of 3 if one-sided). In theory a rise in the interest rate encourages saving and reduces funding for investment, reducing inflation; fiscal policy raises taxes or cuts spending, at the cost of higher unemployment; supply-side policies may help where there are structural weaknesses.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): consideration of which is the best way (up to 3) and a justified conclusion (1). There are no marks for merely defining terms in the question.
PlanMonetary: rates up, AD down; lags, cost-push. Fiscal: taxes up, spending down; unemployment. Supply-side: costs down; slow. Judge by cause and time.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A rise in the interest rate encourages saving and reduces borrowing and investment, so AD falls and inflation eases. It is flexible and independent of politics, but it works with a lag, may be offset by expectations and does little against cost-push inflation. Fiscal policy, raising taxes or cutting spending, also reduces AD and can be targeted, but cuts output and employment and takes time to legislate. Supply-side policy can lower costs and tackle structural causes, but takes years.

Contractionary monetary policy is probably the best short-run tool for demand-pull inflation because it can be changed quickly, but for cost-push or structural inflation supply-side measures may be better. A combination is likely to be best.

Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/21 · Q4(b) 12 marks Discuss how likely it is that monetary policy would be successful in reducing inflation in an economy with a floating exchange rate.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the likely success of monetary policy in an economy with a floating exchange rate (up to 4), through a rise in interest rates or a reduction in the money supply; for cost-push inflation, an appreciation lowers import prices of finished products and raw materials; for demand-pull inflation, higher rates or a smaller money supply reduce AD and appreciate the currency; and the likely difficulties (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on how likely it is that monetary policy would succeed, with reference to elasticities, the J-curve effect and the Marshall-Lerner condition, and a reserved mark for a conclusion.
PlanHigher rates: AD down, currency up, import prices down. Floating rate helps: appreciation lowers costs. Difficulties: exporters hit, elasticities, lags. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

With a floating exchange rate, higher interest rates attract funds, so the currency may appreciate. This lowers the price of imported goods and raw materials, which helps against cost-push inflation, and the fall in AD reduces demand-pull inflation. But a stronger currency makes exports dearer and reduces net exports, which may lower output and employment, and the effects take time.

Monetary policy is likely to be fairly successful with a floating exchange rate, because the currency effect reinforces the fall in AD, but the success depends on the elasticities of demand for exports and imports, time lags and the cause of the inflation.

Top tips

  • Give the full chain. Rate change, borrowing, C and I, AD, output and prices.
  • Use AD/AS diagrams. Left shift for contractionary, right for expansionary.
  • Match policy to problem. Demand-pull versus cost-push.
  • Mention time lags. Effects can take 12 to 18 months.
  • Mention the limits. Zero bound, confidence, expectations.
  • Use the exchange rate. It can reinforce or offset the policy.
  • Compare and conclude. Fiscal and supply-side alternatives.
