Globalisation: Causes and Consequences

11.6.1: what globalisation means, why it has grown, and its effects on consumers, workers, firms and countries at different levels of development. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Globalisation is the growing integration of economies through trade in goods and services, flows of capital and foreign direct investment, migration of labour, and the spread of technology, information and culture. It has been driven by falling transport and communication costs, trade liberalisation, multinational companies and global supply chains, deregulated capital markets and the opening up of large economies such as China and India. Its consequences include lower prices, more choice, faster growth and technology transfer, but also job losses in some industries, rising inequality, environmental damage, loss of sovereignty and greater exposure to shocks elsewhere, so the gains are real but not shared equally.

1 · The meaning of globalisation

  • Trade: world trade has grown faster than world output, so trade makes up a larger share of GDP in most countries (a rise in openness).
  • Capital flows: FDI and portfolio investment move across borders in large amounts, and financial markets are linked worldwide.
  • Labour: migration and remittances connect labour markets, although labour is less mobile than capital.
  • Technology and information: ideas, patents and know-how spread quickly; production is organised in global supply chains (a product may be designed in one country, made from parts from several others and assembled in another).
  • Culture and consumption: the same brands, media and consumer tastes appear worldwide.

2 · Causes of globalisation

  • Lower transport and communication costs: containerisation, cheaper air freight, the internet and mobile phones.
  • Trade liberalisation: reductions in tariffs and quotas through the GATT and World Trade Organization, regional agreements and unilateral reforms.
  • Multinational companies and FDI: firms locate production where costs are lowest and sell worldwide.
  • Deregulation of capital markets and the end of exchange controls, which allowed large capital flows.
  • Political change: the opening of China, the end of the Soviet bloc and market reforms in India and elsewhere added large labour forces and markets.
  • Technology and rising incomes: new products, automation and growing demand in emerging economies.

High-income and low-income countries

  • High-income countries gain cheaper goods and larger markets for high-value goods and services, but face job losses in manufacturing and inequality.
  • Low-income countries may gain markets, FDI, jobs and technology; growth and tax revenue may help living standards. But they can suffer from dependence on primary exports, exploitation of labour, competition that destroys local industries, volatile capital flows and a lack of bargaining power. The gains depend on whether they can diversify and have good institutions.

The backlash

Concerns about jobs, inequality and security have led to protectionism (higher tariffs and trade wars), calls to bring production back home, and tensions between major economies. These are threats to the continued growth of globalisation.

Evaluation pointWhether globalisation raises living standards depends on whether the gains in GDP reach the poor, on the extent of environmental costs, on how well countries support displaced workers (retraining, social protection) and on the type of country. Gains are real in aggregate, but they are not automatically or equally shared, so the answer is usually "it depends".

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

The shipping container

Standardised steel containers made loading and unloading cargo much cheaper and faster from the 1960s. Together with bigger ships and ports they cut the cost of moving goods across oceans, which made it profitable to make parts in one country and assemble them in another.

Exam use: falling transport costs as a cause of globalisation and global supply chains.

China and the 'China shock'

China's entry into the world trade system after 2001 made many consumer goods cheaper in rich countries but hit towns that made furniture, clothing and electronics, where jobs disappeared and some workers found it hard to retrain. China's incomes and exports rose very rapidly and hundreds of millions of people moved out of absolute poverty.

Exam use: benefits for a developing economy and costs for workers in high-income countries.

The Suez Canal blockage and supply chains

In 2021 a container ship stuck in the Suez Canal delayed hundreds of ships and caused shortages around the world. The event showed how interdependent supply chains have become and how a shock in one place can spread.

Exam use: interdependence and vulnerability to shocks.

Trade tensions and tariffs

In recent years some governments have raised tariffs on imports to protect jobs and national security and have encouraged firms to bring production home. Supporters point to jobs and resilience, while critics point to higher prices and retaliation.

Exam use: protectionism as a threat to globalisation.

Based on Feb/March 2023 · 9708/42 · Q5 20 marks Assess the impact of globalisation on the standard of living in low-income countries.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Globalisation promotes free trade, free movement of capital and labour and the transfer of technology; cultural and political ties are strengthened.
  • Low-income countries have low productivity, high dependency ratios, low GDP per capita and depend on primary goods; living standards are linked to poverty, inequality, housing, education and health.
  • An improvement in living standards is closely linked to improved economic performance (GDP per head, productivity); trade and technology transfer create more real wealth; tax revenue may fund health, housing and education.
  • Evaluation: the links from globalisation to GDP and living standards are not clear; labour may be exploited; economic dependency and instability; effects may be unequal.
PlanDefine globalisation. Benefits: trade, FDI, technology, tax revenue to fund services. Link GDP to living standards. Costs: exploitation, dependency, inequality, volatility. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Globalisation is the growing integration of economies through trade, investment, labour and technology flows. A low-income country typically has low productivity, a large primary sector and a high dependency ratio, so its living standards are low. Globalisation could raise living standards by giving access to world markets, bringing FDI and technology and creating jobs. Higher output and exports could raise real income per head, and more tax revenue may let the government spend more on health, education and housing.

However, the link to living standards is not automatic. Workers may be exploited in low-wage factories, the gains may go to a small group, and dependence on primary exports or on a few foreign firms leaves the economy exposed to price swings and shocks. Local industries may be destroyed by cheaper imports. Overall, globalisation could raise living standards in the long run if the country diversifies and shares the gains, but the effect may be uneven and uncertain.

Based on Feb/March 2024 · 9708/42 · Q5 20 marks Globalisation will help to achieve economic growth in high-income economies and this will automatically improve living standards. Evaluate this statement.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Globalisation promotes free trade, free movement of capital and labour, and technology transfer.
  • High-income countries have high productivity and GDP per head and rely on secondary and tertiary output; high living standards go with low poverty, advanced infrastructure, good housing, education and health, and low infant mortality.
  • Growth is measured by changes in real GDP per head; an improvement in living standards is closely linked to economic performance.
  • Evaluation: the link from growth to living standards is not automatic; globalisation brings job losses and inequality in some sectors, and non-monetary aspects of living standards matter.
PlanHow globalisation raises growth in rich countries. Link to living standards. Why it is not automatic: inequality, unemployment, environment. Conclude.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

In high-income economies globalisation could raise growth through specialisation, access to larger markets and cheaper imports, which lowers costs and prices. Real GDP per head may rise, and higher incomes and tax revenue could improve health, education and infrastructure. Consumers gain from lower prices and a wider choice, and exporters of high-value goods and services gain from larger markets.

However, growth does not automatically improve living standards for everyone. Workers in industries that face cheap imports may lose jobs, which raises structural unemployment and inequality. The environment may be damaged and the economy is more exposed to shocks from elsewhere. Living standards also depend on non-monetary factors such as leisure and the environment. So the statement is too strong; globalisation could help, but the effect depends on how the gains are shared.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/41 · Q5 20 marks To what extent do you agree with the view that globalisation benefits high-income countries at the expense of low-income countries?
What the mark scheme wants
  • Globalisation raises trade, FDI and technology flows for both groups; high-income countries have advantages in capital, technology and bargaining power.
  • Possible ways high-income countries gain: cheaper goods, markets for high-value exports, profits from MNCs, access to resources; possible losses for low-income countries: dependence on primary exports with falling terms of trade, low wages, profit repatriation, environmental damage.
  • Evaluation: low-income countries also gain (exports, jobs, technology, growth as in East Asia), and high-income countries can lose (jobs, inequality), so it is not a simple transfer.
PlanCase for. Case against. Examples of gains for both. Judge: depends on country, policy and the type of trade.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Globalisation could benefit high-income countries because their firms have more capital, technology and brands, so they may capture a larger share of the gains. MNCs based there can send profits home, and rich countries can protect their own farmers while selling manufactured goods to poorer countries. Low-income countries often export primary products with volatile prices and a falling terms of trade, and may have weak bargaining power, so a larger share of the gains could go to the rich.

However, it is not simply at the expense of low-income countries. Several East Asian economies grew rapidly by exporting, attracting FDI and learning technology, and many people moved out of poverty. High-income countries also lose, for example when jobs in manufacturing move abroad. The extent depends on the country's policies, the type of trade and the terms of investment, so the statement is only partly true.

Top tips

  • Define it with examples. Trade, capital, labour and technology flows, with global supply chains.
  • Give three causes. Cheaper transport and communication, trade liberalisation, multinationals and FDI.
  • Split high-income and low-income. Effects differ. Say which group is affected and why.
  • Link to living standards carefully. GDP per head is not the whole story: distribution, health, environment.
  • Use both sides. Benefits and costs for consumers, workers, firms and governments.
  • Mention the backlash. Protectionism and trade wars may threaten globalisation.
  • Judge with 'depends on'. Policies, institutions, flexibility and support for displaced workers.
