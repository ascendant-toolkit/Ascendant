AO1 Knowledge and Understanding: How Paper 4 Marks Are Earned

What earns AO1 marks on Paper 3 and Paper 4: precise definitions, formulae, labelled diagrams, using the data, and what Level 1, 2 and 3 knowledge looks like. Animations, labs and practice.

This part in five sentences

AO1 is knowledge and understanding: recalling facts, formulae and definitions, showing understanding with appropriate explanations and examples, and applying that knowledge to economic information in written, numerical and diagrammatic form. It is 35% of the A Level, 47% of Paper 3 and 33% of Paper 4. On Paper 4 it is not marked on its own: in an essay it is combined with AO2 on the 14-mark Table A scale, and in Section A it earns most of the marks in the early parts. The most common reasons for losing it are imprecise terms, a diagram that is missing or wrong, and knowledge that is correct but never applied to the question's context. This page shows what earns it, with examples.

1 · Where AO1 marks are

WhereWhat AO1 looks like
Paper 3 (30 questions, 30 marks)Definitions, relationships, formulae and calculations; reading a table or a diagram and applying a concept to it.
Paper 4 Section A, early parts (usually 2 to 4 marks)Identify, define, describe the data. One mark per accurate point, so short and precise answers score best.
Paper 4 Section A, later partsQuoting the source accurately and using it. The source is there to be used, and doing so is application, which counts as AO1.
Paper 4 essay (20 marks)Part of the 14-mark Table A scale, shared with AO2: accurate concepts, correct and labelled diagrams, and examples that support the explanation.

2 · The three strands of AO1

Recall

Facts, formulae, definitions. Know the exact meaning of terms such as allocative efficiency, the multiplier and the terms of trade, and what each formula measures.

Understanding

An explanation or example that shows you know why, not just what. "A negative externality is a cost to a third party" is recall. Adding "so the market price is too low and output too high" shows understanding.

Application

Using knowledge on the information in front of you: the passage, the table, the country, the good. This is the strand most students leave out, and it is the difference between a topic answer and an answer to this question.

3 · What the three levels of Table A look like for knowledge

In our words, from the pattern of the Paper 4 mark scheme. Markers award a best-fit level for the whole answer, with AO2.

Level (of the 14 marks)The knowledge looks like
Level 3 (11 to 14)Accurate concepts throughout, a correct and fully explained diagram, and examples or context that support the explanation.
Level 2 (6 to 10)Mostly accurate, but examples are limited or over-generalised, the diagram is thin or lightly used, or one required element is missing.
Level 1 (1 to 5)Some relevant knowledge with errors, no diagram or a wrong one, and little link to the question.
Hard capIf the question says "with the help of a diagram" and there is no correct, labelled diagram of the required type, the 14-mark scale is capped at Level 2. Draw the diagram the question names.

4 · Definitions that score

A precise definition has the term, the feature that matters and, if it helps, the contrast with its neighbour. Say it once, early, and move on.

TermImprecisePrecise
Allocative efficiency"Using resources well.""Resources are allocated to the goods and services that society values most, where price equals marginal cost (or marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost)."
Productive efficiency"Producing a lot.""Output is produced at the lowest possible average cost, at the minimum point of the average cost curve."
Negative externality"Pollution.""A cost imposed on a third party that is not reflected in the market price, so social cost exceeds private cost."
Economic growth"The economy getting bigger.""An increase in real GDP over time, or in potential output when the PPC shifts outwards."
Structural unemployment"People with no jobs.""Unemployment caused by a mismatch between workers' skills or location and the jobs available, usually after a long-term change in the structure of demand."
Absolute and relative poverty"Being poor.""Absolute poverty is income below the level needed for basic needs; relative poverty is income below a proportion of the typical income in that society."

5 · Formulae: what to write and what to avoid

MeasureFormulaMarks lost by
Price elasticity of demand% change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in priceForgetting that the sign is negative for most goods, or using absolute changes.
Income elasticity of demand% change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in incomeNot saying what a negative value means (inferior good).
Cross elasticity of demand% change in quantity of A ÷ % change in price of BReversing A and B.
The multiplier1 ÷ (1 − MPC), or 1 ÷ (MPS + MPT + MPM)Using the wrong marginal propensity or forgetting leakages.
Terms of trade(index of export prices ÷ index of import prices) × 100Inverting the ratio.
Percentage change(new − old) ÷ old × 100Dividing by the new value; saying "%" when it is percentage points.

6 · Diagrams: the marks are in the details

  • Axes: label both, with the right names (price level and real GDP for AD/AS, not P and Q).
  • Curves: name each one (AD1, SRAS, MSC, MSB) and make the shape and slope correct.
  • Equilibrium: mark the original and the new points and project them to the axes.
  • Shift: use an arrow, say which curve moves and why.
  • Context: use the good, country or market in a label ("Market for coal-fired electricity").
  • Match the model to the wording: a consumption externality is not a production externality, a monopsony is not a competitive labour market, and an injections and withdrawals graph is not AD/AS.

7 · Application is AO1

"Higher interest rates reduce investment and consumption."
"With household debt at a high share of income, a higher interest rate could raise repayments quickly, so consumption in this economy may fall more than the textbook suggests."

Two habits make this automatic. First, quote the data with number, unit and year: "GDP per head rose from $9,000 to $11,500 between 2015 and 2020". Second, run the swap test: change the country or the good in a paragraph. If it still reads correctly, it is not applied.

8 · Common ways to lose AO1 marks

  • Confusing neighbours: equity and equality, production and productivity, free trade area and customs union, takeover and merger, a rise and a fall in the interest rate.
  • No diagram, or the wrong one, or one that is drawn and never mentioned.
  • Defining everything, so the first third of an essay earns nothing new.
  • Leaving out part of the stem: both markets, both income groups, the named policy.
  • Quoting without using: copying a figure from the data and not saying what it shows.
  • Percent and percentage points mixed up, or a missing unit.
Exam tipFor a short Section A part, write exactly what the command word asks for and stop. "Identify two" needs two short named points. "Define" needs the meaning, plus one extra feature if there are two marks. Spend the time you save on the last part.

Terms get mixed up

Examiner reports for Paper 4 list the same confusions each year: free trade area and customs union, takeover and merger, production and productivity, equity and equality, and a rise and a fall in interest rates.

Exam use: defining the key term in your opening sentence, precisely, and checking the stem for a near neighbour.

Diagrams are the common cap

Reports describe AD and AS curves labelled D and S, a negative output gap drawn as positive, a production externality drawn for air travel, and a competitive diagram used for a monopsony.

Exam use: matching the diagram to the exact wording of the question and labelling it with the context.

Context gets ignored

Reports describe an answer that treated cost-push inflation as demand-pull, and answers that left out the low-income country or the standard of living element of the question.

Exam use: putting the context nouns in every paragraph and running the swap test.

Data is copied, not used

In Section A, answers that quote the source without saying what it shows earn only part of the data marks. Answers that ignore a passage when the stem says 'using the information' lose marks.

Exam use: quoting the number, unit and year, and adding a few words on what it shows or cannot show.

Missing parts of a long stem

A question on productivity in perfect and imperfect labour markets was often answered for one market only, and mark schemes capped it.

Exam use: ticking off each element of the stem in your five-minute plan.

Practice question 2 marks Identify two ways in which a government could measure a country's economic development.
What the mark scheme wants
  • One mark for each valid measure, for example the Human Development Index, real GDP per head, the Gini coefficient or life expectancy.
  • No explanation is needed, and no credit for repeating one measure in other words.
PlanWrite two short items on two lines. Do not explain. Two marks need two different measures.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The Human Development Index. Real GDP per head.

Practice question 3 marks Distinguish between absolute poverty and relative poverty.
What the mark scheme wants
  • One mark for a correct meaning of absolute poverty, for example income below the level needed for basic needs.
  • One mark for a correct meaning of relative poverty, for example income below a proportion of the typical income in that society.
  • One mark for the distinction: relative poverty can exist in a rich country and changes as incomes in society change, while absolute poverty relates to a fixed standard.
PlanDefine each, then add the contrast in one sentence. Three marks, three sentences.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Absolute poverty is income below the level needed to meet basic needs such as food and shelter. Relative poverty is income below a set share of the typical income in that society. Relative poverty could remain in a wealthy country, whereas absolute poverty relates to a fixed standard of living.

Practice question 4 marks Table: real GDP was $80bn in 2020 and $84bn in 2021; the population was 10 million in 2020 and 10.2 million in 2021. Calculate the percentage change in real GDP per head between 2020 and 2021.
What the mark scheme wants
  • One mark for real GDP per head in 2020 ($8,000) and one for 2021 (about $8,235).
  • One mark for the correct percentage change formula and one for the answer, about 2.9%.
  • Marks are for working, so show each step.
PlanDivide, then apply (new minus old) over old, times 100. State the unit.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

GDP per head in 2020 = $80bn ÷ 10m = $8,000. In 2021 = $84bn ÷ 10.2m = about $8,235. The change is (8,235 − 8,000) ÷ 8,000 × 100 = about 2.9%.

Practice question 20 marks (AO1 and AO2 on the 14-mark scale) With the help of a diagram, assess whether a tax on a good with a negative externality in production will make a market allocatively efficient.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Hard cap: a correct, labelled diagram of a production externality (MPC, MSC, demand) is needed to reach Level 3 on the 14-mark scale.
  • Accurate definition of a negative externality in production and of allocative efficiency.
  • Application to a named market, such as coal-fired electricity or a chemical plant.
  • Evaluation (6 marks): the size of the tax against the external cost, elasticity, the difficulty of measuring the cost, and alternatives.
PlanOpening: define the externality and efficiency. Diagram with MPC, MSC, D. Chain: tax raises costs, supply shifts, output falls to the social optimum. Then evaluate and conclude.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A negative externality in production is a cost imposed on third parties that is not reflected in the firm's private cost, so the market price is too low and output is too high. Allocative efficiency is reached where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost. Take a coal-fired power market. The firm's marginal private cost (MPC) leaves out the local air pollution, so the marginal social cost (MSC) curve lies above it. In the diagram, with price and quantity on the axes, the free market output Q1 is where demand (MSB) meets MPC, but the socially efficient output Q2 is where demand meets MSC, which is lower.

Producing between Q2 and Q1 means each extra unit costs society more than it is worth to buyers, which creates a welfare loss triangle. A tax equal to the external cost per unit raises the firm's cost, so MPC shifts up to MSC. Price rises from P1 to P2 and output falls to Q2, so the firm now pays for the pollution and the welfare loss could disappear. The tax also raises revenue that could be used to clean up damage or reduce other taxes.

However, the tax makes the market allocatively efficient only if it is set equal to the external cost at the efficient output, and measuring that cost is difficult. If the tax is too low, some welfare loss remains; if it is too high, output falls below the optimum. Effectiveness also depends on elasticity: if demand for electricity is price inelastic and there are few substitutes to coal, output may fall by little, so emissions fall little even though the tax raises prices for households. Over a longer period, firms could switch to cleaner fuel, so the tax may be more effective in the long run than the short run. Regulation or tradable permits could guarantee a fixed level of pollution but would not raise revenue. On balance, a tax is likely to move this market towards allocative efficiency, but only to the extent that it is set close to the external cost and substitutes for coal exist, which is why it may be best paired with investment in alternatives.

B · Multiple choice questions (Paper 1)

Choose an answer, then read the reason. Reworded from the paper and question number shown.

Top tips

  • Say it precisely, once. One definition, early, with the feature that matters.
  • Draw what the stem names. Right model, right axes, labelled with the context.
  • Use the data. Number, unit and year, then what it shows.
  • Check the units. Percent against percentage points.
  • Cover every element of the stem. Both markets, both groups, the named policy.
  • Short parts need short answers. Write what the command word asks for and stop.

Sentences you can use

You want to sayUse this sentence
Defining a term"X is [what it is], which means [the feature that matters]."
Using the data"The source shows that [figure, unit, year], which means [what it shows]."
Referring to the diagram"In the diagram, the shift from X1 to X2 raises [variable] from A to B."
Stating a limit"The data cover only [period or group], so they cannot show [what is missing]."
