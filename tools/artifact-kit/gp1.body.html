Maximum and Minimum Prices

Price ceilings and floors: what a binding control does to quantity demanded and supplied, who gains and loses, how black markets appear and what it costs to buy up a surplus. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

A maximum price (ceiling) is a legal limit set below the equilibrium price, so quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied and there is a shortage. A minimum price (floor) is set above equilibrium, so supply exceeds demand and there is a surplus. Neither works unless it is binding, meaning it sits on the wrong side of the market price. Both create winners and losers, so the evaluation is about who gains, who loses and what the government does next.

1 · Price controls and the diagram

Market: demand P = 100 − Q, supply P = 20 + Q, so the equilibrium is Q = 40, P = $60.

Maximum price below equilibrium

At $40, consumers want 60 but firms supply only 20: a shortage of 40. Rationing by queue, favouritism or a black market replaces the price mechanism. Used for rents, essential goods and regulated utilities.

Minimum price above equilibrium

At $80, consumers buy only 20 but firms supply 60: a surplus of 40. Someone must buy up, store or destroy it. Used for agricultural prices and minimum unit pricing of alcohol.

A price control only works if it bitesA maximum price above the equilibrium price (or a minimum price below it) has no effect. Check where the control line sits compared with the market price.

3 · Evaluating price controls

  • Size of the gap. The further the control is from equilibrium, the bigger the shortage or surplus.
  • Elasticity. The more elastic demand and supply, the larger the shortage or surplus. Supply is usually more elastic in the long run, so a rent ceiling may do more damage over time as landlords sell up or stop building.
  • Time. A temporary ceiling in an emergency is easier to defend than a permanent one.
  • Alternatives. A subsidy, a cash benefit or more supply can help the same people without a shortage.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Rent control

Some cities cap rents below the market level. Existing tenants may gain, but the supply of rental housing could shrink and shortages, waiting lists and black market payments may follow.

Exam use: the standard maximum price example. Draw the ceiling below equilibrium, mark the shortage, then evaluate who gains and who loses.

Minimum unit price for alcohol

Scotland introduced a minimum price per unit of alcohol in 2018. It aims to cut cheap, strong drink consumption. It acts like a floor above the market price for some products.

Exam use: a minimum price used to reduce demerit good consumption. Evaluate: effect on heavy drinkers, loss of consumer choice, extra revenue for retailers rather than the government.

Price caps on energy bills

In energy crises several governments capped what suppliers could charge households. Bills were held down, but the cap meant the government (and so taxpayers) or the suppliers carried the gap between the cap and wholesale costs, and the lower price gave households less reason to cut back.

Exam use: a ceiling used to protect incomes. Evaluate: who pays the difference, and the weaker incentive to save energy.

Agricultural guaranteed prices

Guaranteeing farmers a price above the market level encouraged over-production in some farm policies, leaving stocks that had to be stored, sold abroad cheaply or destroyed.

Exam use: the standard minimum price example. Draw the surplus, then say who pays for it.

A · Practice question with a model answer

Practice (not a past paper question) 12 marks Explain, with the help of a diagram, the likely effects of a maximum price set below the equilibrium price in the market for rented housing.
PlanDefine the control. Diagram: demand, supply, ceiling below equilibrium, Qs and Qd, shortage. Effects on tenants, landlords and the market. One evaluative point on elasticity or alternatives.
KANE

A maximum price is a legal limit on the price that may be charged, set here below the equilibrium rent so that it is binding.

At the lower rent, the quantity of housing demanded could rise as renting becomes cheaper, while the quantity supplied is likely to fall because landlords earn less and some may sell or convert their properties. Demand is then greater than supply, so there is a shortage equal to the gap between Qd and Qs. Without the price mechanism, flats may be rationed by waiting lists or by who the landlord prefers, and some tenants may pay extra payments to secure a home.

Tenants who keep a flat at the capped rent gain, but people who cannot find a home lose, and the quality of housing may fall if landlords spend less on repairs. The size of the shortage may depend on elasticity: supply of housing is likely to be more inelastic in the short run, so the shortage may be smaller at first and grow over time. Subsidies for tenants or building more homes could help low-income households without a shortage.

Top tips

  • Check where the control sits. A ceiling only bites below the equilibrium price and a floor only above it. Say so, and show it on the diagram.
  • Label Qd, Qs and the gap. The shortage or surplus is the horizontal distance at the controlled price, not at equilibrium.
  • Name the rationing method. Queues, waiting lists, favouritism and black markets are the application marks for a ceiling.
  • For a floor, say who pays for the surplus. Government buying costs surplus times price, funded by taxpayers.
  • Pick winners and losers. Consumers who get the good against those who do not; producers who sell against those who cannot.
  • Evaluate with elasticity, time and alternatives. Subsidies or income support can help the same people without a shortage.
