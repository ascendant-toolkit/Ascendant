Effects of Fiscal Policy on the Aims

How fiscal policy measures could help a government meet its macroeconomic aims, the conflicts between them and the limits of fiscal policy. Syllabus 4.2.6.

The big idea

Fiscal policy can help a government meet its macroeconomic aims, but each measure helps some aims and may harm others. Expansionary policy tends to help growth and jobs but risks inflation, a bigger deficit and a worse trade balance. Contractionary policy tends to help prices and the budget but risks growth and jobs. Good answers say which aim, how, and what depends on the economy's state.

Aim by aim

Fiscal policy and the budget

Fiscal policy directly changes the budget balance. If a balanced budget is followed by higher spending or a tax cut, a deficit appears in the short run. The long run can differ: more spending on education may raise productivity and output and so, in time, tax revenue; a cut in corporation tax may attract firms and raise revenue.

Why fiscal policy may not work as planned

  • Time lags. It takes time to recognise a problem, pass a budget and for spending to reach the economy.
  • Size of the multiplier. If people save or spend a lot on imports, the effect is smaller.
  • Spare capacity. With little spare capacity, extra AD mostly raises prices.
  • The deficit. Borrowing adds to the national debt and interest payments.
  • Confidence and expectations. If households expect higher taxes later, they may save a tax cut.
  • Conflicts. Growth and jobs against inflation and the current account; spending against the deficit.

Recessions and automatic stabilisers

When the economy slumps, firms lay off workers. Income tax revenue falls and spending on unemployment benefit rises automatically, so the deficit widens without any new policy and support for spending is maintained.

Exam use: a clean example of automatic stabilisers. Explain the mechanism, then evaluate: it limits the damage but does not remove the recession.

Stimulus after a crisis

After the 2008 financial crisis and during the COVID-19 pandemic, many governments raised spending (support payments, wage subsidies, infrastructure) and some cut taxes temporarily such as lowering VAT rates. Deficits and national debt rose sharply as a result.

Exam use: expansionary fiscal policy in action. Cover AD, growth and jobs, then the cost: bigger deficit, higher debt and interest payments.

Austerity and the cost of tightening

Some countries with large deficits raised taxes and cut spending to reduce borrowing. Public finances improved, but critics argued the cuts slowed growth and raised unemployment, which cut tax revenue.

Exam use: contractionary fiscal policy, and a standard evaluation point: reducing the deficit may take longer or cost more output than hoped.

Tourism and the Maldives budget

The Maldives gets government revenue from taxes and from leasing islands for resorts, fisheries and agriculture, and a big share of spending goes on public sector wages. When tourism falls, revenue falls while wage bills do not, so the deficit widens.

Exam use: a budget-position question. Calculate spending as revenue plus the deficit, then explain how a fall in tourist numbers cuts tax and leasing income.

Q1 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not an increase in income tax rates will improve economic performance.
Reminder"Whether or not" means two sides and a judgement. Define economic performance in your own words (growth, employment, inflation, equity, the budget) so the answer is about the right thing. Finish with "it depends on…".
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

Economic performance is judged by how well the government meets aims such as economic growth, low unemployment, stable prices and a fair distribution of income, and income tax is a direct tax on earnings.

An increase in income tax rates could improve economic performance if the extra revenue is spent well. The revenue could fund education, healthcare or infrastructure that may raise productivity and long-run growth, and a progressive income tax may reduce inequality. If the economy is overheating, higher taxes could also cut disposable income and consumption, which may ease inflationary pressure. However, these gains depend on how the money is used and on how long the benefits take to arrive.

On the other hand, higher rates could reduce performance. Lower disposable income is likely to reduce consumption and aggregate demand, which may slow growth and raise unemployment. Workers may be discouraged from overtime or promotion and entrepreneurs from expanding, and some highly skilled people might move abroad or work in the informal economy, which could shrink the tax base. This effect may be weaker for workers who cannot choose their hours, or who have fixed commitments such as a mortgage and may work more.

Overall, the effect is likely to depend on the size of the increase, the state of the economy (a boom or a recession), and how the revenue is spent. A modest rise on higher earners during a boom, used to fund productive spending, is more likely to improve performance than a large rise during a recession.

Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain two conflicts between macroeconomic aims that could result from expansionary fiscal policy.
ReminderTwo pairs of aims, each with a chain. Employment and prices; growth and the current account.
One conflict is between full employment and stable prices. Higher spending raises AD so firms hire more workers, but demand could rise faster than supply, causing demand-pull inflation. A second is between economic growth and the balance of payments. Higher incomes could lead to more spending on imports, so the current account deficit could widen.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a government could use fiscal policy to reduce unemployment.
ReminderPick expansionary measures; chain to AD, output and jobs. Mention one limit.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A government could use expansionary fiscal policy: increase spending and/or cut taxes. For example, building roads or cutting income tax. Higher G adds directly to AD and a tax cut raises disposable income and consumption, so AD could shift right. Firms need to produce more, so they are likely to hire more workers, and the multiplier could lead to further jobs. However, the effect may be slow and the deficit may rise.
Q4 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not a government should reduce its budget deficit by cutting spending.
ReminderBoth sides. For: debt interest, confidence, inflation. Against: lost jobs, growth, services, lower revenue. End with a judgement.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A budget deficit exists when spending is higher than revenue, and cutting spending is contractionary fiscal policy. Cutting spending could reduce borrowing, so the national debt and interest payments grow more slowly, leaving more of future revenue for services. It could also reduce demand-pull inflationary pressure. These gains are larger when the economy is near full capacity. However, spending cuts reduce AD, so output and employment could fall, which lowers tax revenue and raises benefit spending, so the deficit may fall by less than hoped. Cuts to health, education or benefits can hurt vulnerable groups and long-run growth. Overall, cutting spending is more likely to be suitable in a boom than in a recession. The best approach depends on the state of the economy, the size of the debt and which spending is cut.

Top tips

  • Name the aim, then follow the chain. Growth, employment, prices, balance of payments, redistribution, environment.
  • Always give a cost as well as a gain. Expansionary: inflation, deficit, imports. Contractionary: jobs, growth.
  • Use conflicts. Pairs the syllabus names: employment and prices; growth and the environment; employment and the balance of payments.
  • Evaluate with lags, multiplier, spare capacity and the deficit. Spare capacity decides whether output or prices respond.
  • Judge: 'it depends on…' The state of the economy, the size of the policy and how it is funded.
