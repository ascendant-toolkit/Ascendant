Direct Provision and Provision of Information

3.2.3 and 3.2.6: when governments supply goods directly, the problems of free provision, and how information can correct information failure. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Direct provision means the government itself produces and supplies a good or service, usually free or below cost at the point of use and paid for through taxation; examples are state schools, public hospitals, defence, police and flood defences. Governments use it for public goods (which the market will not supply), for merit goods (to make sure everyone can use them) and for equity, but it has an opportunity cost, may be inefficient without competition, and a free service can cause over-use and queues. Provision of information tackles information failure directly, through campaigns, warnings, labels and education, to raise demand for merit goods and cut demand for demerit goods. It is cheap and respects choice, but it works slowly, may be ignored, and is rarely enough on its own.

1 · Direct provision (3.2.3)

Why governments do itProblems with it
Public goods such as defence and street lighting would not be supplied by the market (free riders)Opportunity cost: funded by taxes, so less for other uses or higher taxes
Merit goods such as education and health care are under-consumed if left to the marketFree at the point of use means demand rises, so queues and waiting lists
Equity: everyone can use essential services regardless of incomeInefficiency: no profit motive or competition, so higher costs or lower quality

  • Direct provision is not only for public goods: health care and education are private goods (rival and excludable) provided by the state.
  • It means the price mechanism is not used for those goods: they are allocated by need, waiting lists or administrative rules.

2 · Provision of information (3.2.6)

  • Merit goods: campaigns on the benefits of vaccination or education raise awareness, so demand shifts to the right.
  • Demerit goods: health warnings, plain packaging, calorie labels and advertising bans make people appreciate the harm, so demand shifts to the left.
  • Advantages: tackles the cause of the problem (information failure), keeps freedom of choice, usually cheaper than tax or direct provision, and has no black-market risk.
  • Disadvantages: results are slow and hard to measure; people may ignore or misunderstand it; advertising by firms may counter it; addicts may not respond; it does not help if cost is the barrier.
Exam tipLink each policy to the cause of the market failure. Information fixes information failure; direct provision fixes non-provision of public goods and under-provision of merit goods. Evaluate with cost, time and effectiveness.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A national health service

Treatment is paid for through taxes and is free when used. Everyone can get care regardless of income, but demand exceeds capacity, so waiting lists form, and the system has a large opportunity cost.

Exam use: direct provision of a merit good, queues and opportunity cost.

Flood defences

A private firm cannot charge each household for a sea wall, because no one can be excluded and one household's protection does not reduce another's. Governments build them with tax money.

Exam use: direct provision of a public good.

Cigarette health warnings

Large pictorial warnings tell smokers about the harm. Awareness rises and some smokers cut down, but heavy smokers who are addicted may not respond, so warnings usually accompany taxes.

Exam use: information provision for a demerit good and its limits.

Vaccination campaigns

Adverts and local clinics explain the benefits of vaccination. Demand shifts to the right as people understand them better, and the campaign costs far less than providing every dose without explanation.

Exam use: information as a way of raising consumption of merit goods.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/21 · Q3(a) 8 marks Explain why a government might decide to increase its direct provision of essential goods and services and consider whether such a policy is always likely to be successful.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): an understanding of the role of the state in providing essential goods and services, such as merit goods (1); these could include education and health care (1); they could be financed out of money received from taxation (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): up to 2 marks for explaining each essential good or service that a government might decide to provide (2 for well explained, 1 for limited explanation). Maximum 3.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a valid judgement on whether an increase in direct provision will always be likely to be successful.
PlanDefine direct provision, name essential goods such as health and education, funded by tax. Evaluate: opportunity cost, queues, inefficiency.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Direct provision is when the government itself supplies goods and services, such as education and health care. They are merit goods, financed from taxation, and the government may increase provision to make sure everyone can use them, whatever their income.

Education raises skills and productivity, with benefits for the whole economy, but low-income households may under-consume it if they have to pay. Health care keeps the workforce healthy and has external benefits. If left to the market, both would be under-provided, so the state steps in.

However, more provision will not always succeed. It has an opportunity cost, because the money could be spent elsewhere or taxes raised, free services can lead to over-use and waiting lists, and state providers can be inefficient. Success depends on how well the extra funding is targeted.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/21 · Q2(a) 8 marks Explain, with the help of examples, how imperfect information among consumers affects their consumption of merit goods and demerit goods.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): knowledge of imperfect information in relation to a merit good and a demerit good, with suitable examples (such as education or health care and alcohol or tobacco).
  • Application (up to 6): imperfect information and the under-consumption (and under-production) of a merit good because consumers do not fully appreciate its value (up to 3); imperfect information and the over-consumption of a demerit good because consumers do not appreciate the harm (up to 3).
PlanMerit: benefits not fully appreciated, under-consumed. Demerit: harm underestimated, over-consumed. Use examples for each.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A merit good, such as education or health care, is a private good that has benefits for the individual and society. A demerit good, such as alcohol or tobacco, is a private good that is damaging to the individual.

With imperfect information, consumers may not appreciate the full value of a merit good, so they under-consume it: for example, some do not see the long-term gain from preventive health care. The market then under-produces it too. For a demerit good, consumers may not understand the damage caused, so they consume more than if they knew, and the market over-produces. Both mean the free market output is not the best for society.

Based on Feb/March 2024 · 9708/22 · Q2(b) 12 marks Assess whether the improved provision of information is likely to be the best method to reduce the consumption of demerit goods.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): how information (labels, warnings, campaigns) corrects information failure and shifts demand to the left; a comparison with at least one alternative, such as an indirect tax, a minimum price, regulation or a ban, with the advantages and disadvantages of each. Indicative content may include elasticity, time lags and cost.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether information is likely to be the best method, supported by reasons, with a conclusion.
PlanInformation: targets the cause, slow, may be ignored. Alternatives: tax (inelastic demand), minimum price (black markets), ban (enforcement). Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Demerit goods are over-consumed because consumers underestimate the harm. Better information tackles this directly: warnings and campaigns could shift demand to the left without raising prices, respect consumer choice and cost less than other methods. But information works slowly, may be ignored by addicted users and can be countered by advertising. An indirect tax raises the price, but with inelastic demand consumption may fall only a little. A minimum price or a ban could cut consumption but risk black markets and are hard to enforce.

Information is unlikely to be the best method on its own, particularly for addictive goods, but it deals with the cause of the problem. A mix of information and a tax is likely to be the most effective, and the best choice depends on the good and on elasticity.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/23 · Q1(e) 6 marks With the help of a diagram, assess how the market for sugary foods might be affected by a ban on online advertising.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 5): a ban on advertising reduces awareness and lowers demand, shifting the demand curve to the left, with a labelled diagram showing a fall in price and quantity; explanation of the effect on producers and revenue; consideration of what limits the effect, such as other forms of promotion, brand loyalty or addiction.
  • Judgement (1): a valid conclusion about the likely overall effect.
PlanDiagram: demand shifts left, price and quantity fall. Limits: other promotion, taste, price elasticity.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Advertising raises demand for sugary foods. A ban on online advertising would reduce awareness and temptation, so demand would shift to the left. On a supply and demand diagram the equilibrium price and quantity would both fall, so producers lose revenue and may cut production. But companies may switch to other forms of advertising, and many consumers have strong taste preferences.

The overall effect is likely to be a small fall in consumption, because the ban affects only one channel, so it would probably work best alongside other policies such as taxes and labelling.

Top tips

  • Say what direct provision is. The state supplies the good or service itself, paid for by tax.
  • It is not only for public goods. Health and education are private goods provided by the state.
  • Always mention opportunity cost. Tax money has other uses.
  • Free means queues. At a zero price demand exceeds capacity.
  • Information targets the cause. Information failure, so it shifts demand.
  • Say which way demand shifts. Right for merit goods, left for demerit goods.
  • Evaluate with time and cost. Information is slow and may be ignored.
