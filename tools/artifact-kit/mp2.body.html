Monetary Policy Measures

Changes in the interest rate, the money supply and the foreign exchange rate: how each works and who it affects. Syllabus 4.3.2.

The big idea

The syllabus lists three monetary policy measures: changes in the interest rate, the money supply and the foreign exchange rate. Each can be expansionary or contractionary. For each one, follow the chain: what changes, who is affected, and what happens to aggregate demand.

Interest rates

An interest rate is the price of borrowing money and the reward for saving it. The central bank sets its own rate (the base or policy rate) and commercial banks usually move their own lending and saving rates in the same direction.

A cut in interest rates

  • Households: loans, credit cards and mortgages cost less and saving pays less, so consumption is likely to rise.
  • Firms: borrowing is cheaper, more investment projects look profitable, so investment could rise.
  • Exchange rate: lower returns on deposits may reduce demand for the currency, so it could fall.
  • Result: AD shifts right.

A rise in interest rates

  • Households: borrowing costs more and saving pays more, so consumption is likely to fall. Mortgage holders have less left to spend.
  • Firms: fewer projects clear the higher cost of borrowing, so investment could fall.
  • Exchange rate: higher returns can attract foreign savers, so the currency could rise.
  • Result: AD shifts left.

Rate changes also reach the government (it pays more to borrow when rates rise, which raises the cost of the national debt) and savers (who gain when rates rise and lose when they fall).

The money supply

A central bank can change the amount of money available. Ways it may do this:

  • Buying or selling government bonds. Buying bonds from banks and investors puts money into the banking system, so banks have more to lend. Selling bonds takes money out.
  • Quantitative easing (QE). A large-scale purchase of bonds to push the money supply up when interest rates are already very low.
  • Changing the rules on banks. For example, how much cash banks must hold back instead of lending.
  • Printing money to finance government spending. This is risky: too much money chasing the same goods can lead to inflation.

A bigger money supply means banks have more to lend, which tends to push interest rates down and make credit easier to get. A smaller one does the reverse. This is why money supply and interest rates are two sides of one policy: the central bank can mainly choose one or the other.

The foreign exchange rate

The foreign exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. In a floating system it is set by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market; in a fixed system the central bank holds it at a set value, using its reserves or interest rates to do so. Governments and central banks can influence it by changing interest rates, buying or selling currency from reserves, or changing the fixed rate (a devaluation or revaluation).

A lower exchange rate (depreciation)

  • Exports are cheaper in foreign currency, imports are dearer in home currency.
  • Exports may rise and imports may fall, so X − M could improve and AD shifts right.
  • Output and jobs in export and import-competing firms could rise.
  • Risks: imported raw materials and goods cost more, which could push up costs and prices (imported inflation); less incentive for firms to cut costs.

A higher exchange rate (appreciation)

  • Exports are dearer abroad, imports are cheaper at home.
  • Exports may fall and imports may rise, so X − M could worsen and AD shifts left.
  • Helps against inflation: imported inputs and goods cost less, and firms face more competition.
  • Risks: exporters lose sales and jobs, and the current account could move towards deficit.

How big the change in X − M is depends on price elasticity of demand for exports and imports. If demand is inelastic, a lower exchange rate may not improve the trade balance by much, at least in the short run.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Central banks fighting inflation, 2022–23

After prices rose quickly in many countries, central banks such as the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and the Bank of England raised interest rates in a series of steps. Mortgage payments rose and borrowing slowed.

Exam use: contractionary monetary policy against demand-pull and cost-push inflation. Evaluate: time lags, higher unemployment risk, and pain for borrowers.

Rates near zero after 2008 and COVID-19

Central banks cut rates to very low levels after the 2008 financial crisis and again during the pandemic, and several bought large amounts of government bonds (quantitative easing) to increase the money supply.

Exam use: expansionary monetary policy in action. Evaluate: effects depend on confidence and on whether banks lend, and savers earn less.

Jordan's peg to the US dollar

Jordan fixes its currency against the US dollar, so it tends to follow US interest rate moves to keep the peg. When the US central bank changes rates, Jordan's central bank usually follows.

Exam use: a fixed exchange rate gives certainty for traders but takes away independent monetary policy. A past paper uses this case.

The Swiss franc surge, 2015

Switzerland's central bank had held the franc down against the euro. When it stopped doing so, the franc rose sharply. Swiss exporters and tourism firms found their prices suddenly higher abroad.

Exam use: a higher exchange rate making exports less competitive. Link to jobs in export industries.

A weaker currency and imported inflation

When a currency falls sharply, as the Turkish lira did in several years of the late 2010s and early 2020s, imports such as fuel and machinery cost more in the home currency. Firms and households may see their costs rise.

Exam use: the cost of a lower exchange rate. Evaluate any depreciation argument with imported inflation.

Housing markets and rate changes

In countries where most home loans have variable rates, a change in the central bank rate changes household mortgage payments quickly. Where loans are fixed for several years the effect is slower. This was noted in a recent past paper stimulus.

Exam use: why the effect of monetary policy depends on the type of borrowing and on how the housing market is working.

Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain how a cut in interest rates could increase consumption and investment.
ReminderTwo sides: households (consumption) and firms (investment). One developed chain for each.
A cut in interest rates lowers the cost of borrowing and reduces the reward for saving. Households are likely to find loans and credit cards cheaper and mortgage payments lower, and saving less attractive, so they may spend more and consumption could rise. For example, a family with a variable rate mortgage could have more disposable income each month. Firms could find that more investment projects now earn more than the cost of borrowing, so they may buy machinery or build new premises and investment could rise.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a fall in a country's foreign exchange rate could increase employment. (May/June 2021, P22)
ReminderOne long chain, in order: price of exports, price of imports, competitiveness, demand, firms, workers. Hedged words (could, may, likely).
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

A fall in the exchange rate means the currency buys less foreign currency. Exports become cheaper for foreign buyers and imports become dearer for domestic buyers, so home-produced goods are likely to be more price competitive. Demand for exports could rise and demand for imports could fall, which increases total demand for domestic output. Firms are likely to receive more revenue and profit, and may increase output. To produce more they could take on extra workers, so employment may rise.

Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain how a central bank could increase the money supply.
ReminderName a method and follow it through: bonds, banks' lending, interest rates.
A central bank could buy government bonds from banks and investors. It pays for them with new money, so banks have more funds available to lend. More lending increases the amount of money in the economy and tends to push interest rates down, which could raise spending. Large-scale bond buying of this kind is called quantitative easing.
Q5 Explain 4 marks Explain how a rise in the interest rate may affect households and firms.
ReminderBoth groups, each with a chain. Borrowers and savers.
A rise in the interest rate makes borrowing more expensive and saving more rewarding. Households with loans or variable-rate mortgages have higher payments and less income to spend, so consumption could fall, though savers earn more. Firms face higher borrowing costs, so fewer investment projects look profitable and investment may fall.

Top tips

  • Three tools only: interest rate, money supply, exchange rate. Name the one in the question.
  • Interest rate chains need both sides. Borrowers and savers, households and firms.
  • Money supply up usually means interest rates down. Link the two.
  • Exchange rate chains start with prices. Export price abroad, import price at home, competitiveness, demand, jobs.
  • Elasticity decides how big the effect is. Inelastic demand means a lower exchange rate may not improve trade much.
