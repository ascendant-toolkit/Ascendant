Cost-Benefit Analysis

Counting all the private and external costs and benefits of a project, discounting future amounts to present value, using net present value as the decision rule, and why the discount rate and valuation make CBA hard. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Cost-benefit analysis (CBA) counts all the private and external costs and benefits of a project, gives them money values and compares them. Because costs and benefits arrive at different times, future amounts are discounted to present value: PV = future value ÷ (1 + r)t. The project is worth doing if the net present value (NPV), the discounted benefits minus the discounted costs, is positive. CBA is useful but hard in practice, because external effects are hard to value and the answer depends on the discount rate.

1 · Cost-benefit analysis (CBA)

A way for governments to decide whether a project (a new airport, hospital or road) is worthwhile by counting all the costs and benefits to society, in money terms.

  1. Identify the private costs and benefits, then the external ones (pollution, congestion, jobs, time saved).
  2. Give them money values. Hard for things such as a lost view or a human life.
  3. Discount future costs and benefits to present values, because $100 now is worth more than $100 in ten years. Present value = future value ÷ (1 + r)t.
  4. Decide: go ahead if the net present value (benefits minus costs, discounted) is positive, or compare projects.

Benefits of CBA

  • Takes account of externalities and wider effects
  • Helps to compare projects and ration public money
  • Makes the decision more open and consistent

Problems

  • Valuing external costs and benefits is difficult and may be biased
  • Uncertain future outcomes
  • The choice of discount rate can change the answer
  • Does not show who gains and who loses (distribution)

2 · Discounting step by step

A project costs $600 now and gives $120 a year of private benefit for ten years, with $36 a year of external cost. At a discount rate of 5%:

  • $120 in year 1 is worth 120 ÷ 1.05 = $114.3 now; $120 in year 10 is worth 120 ÷ 1.0510 = $73.7 now. Later money counts for less.
  • The present value of ten years of $120 is about $927, so private NPV = 927 − 600 = +$327.
  • Net of external cost, the yearly benefit is $84, with present value about $649, so social NPV = 649 − 600 = +$49: still worth doing, but much less so.

3 · Decision rules

  • Single project: go ahead if NPV is greater than zero.
  • Several projects with a limited budget: rank by NPV, or by the ratio of discounted benefits to discounted costs.
  • The discount rate matters. A higher rate shrinks distant benefits more, which favours projects with quick payoffs and can turn a positive NPV negative. Use the Watch and Explore tabs to see the sign flip.

4 · Difficulties in practice

  • Valuing intangibles: noise, a view, national pride, a life saved. Any figure is open to argument.
  • Forecasting: costs often overrun and future benefits are uncertain.
  • Choosing the discount rate can decide the result.
  • Distribution: a positive net benefit hides who gains and who loses.
  • Judgement: a project with a positive NPV may still not go ahead if money is limited or other projects have a better return.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. The numbers are simple on purpose, to show the idea.

Building a hospital

A new hospital has large private costs. It also creates negative externalities (traffic, effects on wildlife) and positive ones (health, jobs, skills). A cost-benefit analysis counts them all and discounts benefits that arrive many years later.

Exam use: the Oct/Nov 2022 25-mark question. Explain CBA, apply to the hospital, then evaluate the difficulties of valuing and discounting.

A new railway line

A rail project has large construction costs and benefits that arrive over decades: time saved, fewer car journeys, more jobs near stations, but also noise and land use. CBA adds these up and discounts the future ones.

Exam use: a project with long-term benefits. A high discount rate may make it look less attractive.

Hosting a major sports event

A stadium for a major event has high private and external costs, and benefits that are hard to value, such as national pride and the use of the stadium afterwards.

Exam use: a Paper 3 question asked why CBA of a stadium is difficult: valuing national pride is hard.

A bridge to an island

A bridge cuts travel time and may raise incomes on the island, but ferry workers may lose their jobs and carbon emissions may rise.

Exam use: a Paper 3 question asked what could be included in a CBA of a bridge: all of these, both costs and benefits.

Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/43 · Q2(a) 12 marks Consider why it is important for a government to use cost benefit analysis when deciding how much of a public good to provide.

Public goods are not provided by the market, so the government must choose the quantity. CBA counts social costs and benefits (including those people would not reveal because of free riding), compares marginal social benefit with marginal cost, and helps avoid over- or under-provision. Add the limits: valuing benefits that have no market price, and discounting.

KANE

Cost-benefit analysis compares all the private and external costs and benefits of a project in money terms. Discounting converts future costs and benefits to present value, using present value = future value ÷ (1 + r)t, because money received later is worth less than money received now.

A hospital has a large cost when it is built, but its benefits, such as better health, fewer working days lost and jobs, may arrive over many decades. Without discounting, distant benefits would count as much as present costs. With a discount rate of 5%, a benefit of $100 in 20 years has a present value of about $38, so later benefits count for much less. If the net present value of the discounted benefits minus the costs is positive, the project is worth doing.

The choice of discount rate could change the decision: a higher rate would reduce the value of long-term health benefits and could make the NPV negative. Valuing health, wildlife and traffic effects in money is also difficult. Overall, CBA is likely to be a useful guide but not the only factor, because the result depends on assumptions that are open to challenge.

Top tips

  • For CBA, say all costs and benefits, money values and discounting. Then give the difficulties.
  • Write the formula: PV = future value ÷ (1 + r)t. Say that NPV above zero means go ahead.
  • Include the external effects. A private NPV can be positive and the social NPV negative once pollution or congestion is counted.
  • Test the discount rate. A higher rate cuts long-term benefits more, which can reverse the decision.
  • Give distribution as an evaluation point: a positive net benefit does not show who gains and who loses.
  • Apply to the case. A hospital, airport or road has particular costs and benefits. Name them.
