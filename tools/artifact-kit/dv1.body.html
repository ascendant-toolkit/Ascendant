Differences in Economic Development Between Countries

Economic development, and how income, productivity, population growth, sector sizes, saving and investment, education, healthcare and natural resources differ between countries. Syllabus 5.4.1.

The big idea

Economic development is an improvement in the standard of living and quality of life of the people of a country: higher income per head, but also better education, healthcare and living conditions (measured by indicators such as the HDI). It is wider than economic growth, which is only a rise in real GDP. Countries differ widely in development. The syllabus gives eight areas of difference, which are both causes and consequences of development: income, productivity, population growth, the size of the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors, saving and investment, education, healthcare and natural resources.

Development compared with growth

Economic growth

A rise in real GDP over time. It is about the quantity of output. A country can grow without improving the lives of most people, for example if the gains go to a few.

Economic development

A rise in living standards and quality of life, measured by GDP per head, education, healthcare, life expectancy and the HDI. Growth is usually needed for development but is not enough by itself.

Countries are often grouped as high-income (more developed) and low-income (less developed) with middle-income countries in between. Development is the process by which low-income economies become high-income economies.

The three sectors

SectorWhat it doesExamples
PrimaryExtracts or grows natural resourcesFarming, fishing, forestry, mining, oil extraction
SecondaryProcesses raw materials into goods and builds thingsManufacturing (cars, clothes, food processing), construction
TertiaryProvides servicesBanking, retail, tourism, education, healthcare, transport

As a country develops, its economy tends to shift from the primary sector to the secondary and then the tertiary sector. Value added (the extra value created at each stage) is usually lowest in the primary sector, so countries that depend on raw materials often have lower incomes. Primary products also have unstable prices and may run out.

Causes of development, and why differences continue

  • Investment in capital and technology raises productivity.
  • Education and healthcare raise the quality of labour.
  • Government spending on infrastructure, schools and hospitals; stable institutions and low corruption.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI) from multinationals, and trade.
  • Making better use of natural resources, and shifting to higher-value sectors.

Differences often reinforce themselves. Low income means low saving and investment, so low productivity and low income again: a vicious circle. High income allows more spending on education and health and more investment: a virtuous circle.

Consequences of the differences

  • Big gaps in living standards, life expectancy and poverty between countries.
  • Migration of workers, often skilled, from poorer to richer countries (brain drain).
  • Dependence on a few exports, on aid or on other countries; vulnerability to price falls and shocks.
  • Trade patterns: poorer countries often export primary products and import manufactured goods and services.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

South Korea's rapid development

In the 1960s South Korea was a low-income, mainly agricultural country. High saving and investment, heavy spending on education and a shift to manufacturing and then services raised incomes more than tenfold in a few decades.

Exam use: Investment, education and a change of sectors raising development.

Mauritius

A past paper describes Mauritius moving from an economy based on sugar to one based on tourism and services, with rising life expectancy and falling infant mortality.

Exam use: A shift from primary to tertiary sector and healthcare improving.

Oil and diamonds

Countries such as Saudi Arabia and Botswana have used revenue from oil or diamonds, with varying success, to fund schools and infrastructure. Others have remained dependent on primary exports with limited diversification.

Exam use: Natural resources: a chance, not a guarantee.

Niger

Niger has one of the world's lowest HDI values, with a large primary sector, high population growth, few years of schooling and low productivity.

Exam use: Several differences combining in a vicious circle.

Bermuda

A past paper asks students to explain two reasons why Bermuda has a higher level of development than many countries.

Exam use: Linking development to sectors, education, healthcare and investment.

Q6 Explain 4 marks Explain two causes of differences in economic development between countries. (Oct/Nov 2020, P23)
ReminderTwo different causes with a link: income, education, healthcare, investment, resources, productivity, sectors, population growth.
One cause is differences in education. Countries with more years of schooling have more skilled workers, who are more productive and earn higher wages, so incomes and living standards are higher. Another cause is differences in saving and investment. A country that invests more in machinery and infrastructure raises productivity and output, so it can develop faster than one with low investment.
Q7 Explain 4 marks Explain two causes of an increase in a country's economic development. (Oct/Nov 2023, P23)
ReminderEducation, healthcare, investment, FDI, better use of resources.
A rise in spending on healthcare is one cause. Healthier people live longer and lose fewer days to illness, so the HDI and output both rise. Foreign direct investment is another. Multinationals bring capital, technology and jobs, raising productivity and incomes.
Q8 Analyse 6 marks Analyse the causes of differences in economic development between countries. (Oct/Nov 2024, P23)
ReminderSeveral differences, each with a link: income, education, healthcare, population growth, sector size, government spending and FDI, resources. Do not say growth is the same as development.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Countries differ in the level of income. Higher income allows more spending on goods and services, and more saving and investment. Education differs. More schooling gives workers skills, raising productivity and job opportunities. Healthcare differs. Better health means longer life expectancy and fewer days lost, so output is higher. Population growth differs: fast growth increases dependants and spreads resources thinly. A large primary sector usually means lower value added and lower income than a large secondary or tertiary sector. Government spending and foreign investment on infrastructure, education and healthcare also differ and can create jobs and reduce poverty.
Q9 Analyse 5 marks Analyse the relationship between the size of countries' primary sector output and GDP per head. (Feb/Mar 2020, P22 style)
ReminderState the overall pattern, quote data for a high and low example, explain why. Say if there is an exception.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Generally there is a negative relationship: countries with a larger primary sector share tend to have a lower GDP per head. For example, a country where primary output is 40% of GDP may have GDP per head of $1,500, whereas one with primary output of 2% may have $45,000. The primary sector has low value added and unstable prices, so countries that rely on it have lower incomes. Higher-income countries have a larger secondary and tertiary sector with higher productivity. There may be exceptions, such as oil exporters with a large primary sector and high income.
Q10 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not having a large primary sector is a disadvantage to an economy. (May/June 2022, P23)
ReminderDisadvantages: low value added, unstable prices, low productivity, risk of specialising, resources can run out. Advantages: jobs, exports, low start-up costs, high-value resources (oil, gold), funds for diversification.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
The primary sector extracts or grows natural resources, such as farming, mining and oil. A large primary sector could be a disadvantage because primary products usually have low value added and low prices, so incomes and living standards may be low. Prices of primary products are unstable, so incomes and export earnings fluctuate, and depending on one sector is risky. Resources may be used up, making growth unsustainable. This is more serious for low-skill, low-productivity farming. However, a large primary sector can provide jobs, especially for low-skilled workers, and low start-up costs. Some primary products such as oil and gold earn high export revenue and the money can be used to diversify into other sectors. Overall, a large primary sector is more likely to be a disadvantage when it is low-value and unstable, but it can be an advantage when resources are valuable and the revenue is invested wisely.
Q11 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not the growth of the primary sector is beneficial to a country. (Oct/Nov 2020, P21)
ReminderCompare jobs and exports with low value added, price instability and sustainability.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
The primary sector includes farming, mining and fishing. Growth of the primary sector could create jobs, raise output and export earnings, especially if the country has valuable resources, and can use the income to invest. It can raise incomes for rural workers and reduce inequality between urban and rural areas. The gains are larger when demand for the product is strong. However, primary products have low value added and volatile prices, and the sector may use up resources or damage the environment. It may also lead the country to depend on a few exports rather than developing manufacturing and services. Overall, primary sector growth is more likely to benefit a country in the short run than as a long-term development strategy, unless the proceeds are invested in other sectors and education.

Top tips

  • Development is not growth. Development means living standards and quality of life; growth is only real GDP.
  • Eight differences: income, productivity, population growth, sector size, saving and investment, education, healthcare, natural resources.
  • Give a link for each. 'More schooling raises skills, so productivity and wages are higher.'
  • Primary: low value added. Secondary and tertiary add more value, so incomes are higher.
  • Mention circles. Poverty reinforces itself; development can feed itself.
  • Evaluate with 'it depends'. On how resources are used, stability and shared gains.
