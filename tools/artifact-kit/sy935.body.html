Patterns and Trends in Employment and Unemployment

9.3.5: measures of employment and unemployment, patterns by group and region, trends over the cycle and how to read the data. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Employment and unemployment data show patterns across groups (age, gender, region, education) and trends over time, such as rising unemployment in recessions and the long-run shift from agriculture and manufacturing to services. Unemployment usually moves against output but lags behind it. National figures hide big differences between groups and regions, and different measures (survey-based or claimant count) can give different answers. Reading the data well means knowing who is counted, using rates and numbers correctly and not mistaking correlation for cause.

If unemployed people give up searching, the unemployment rate falls but nobody has found a job: check the employment rate and participation rate too.

2 · Patterns

  • By age: youth unemployment is usually 2 to 3 times the average, because of limited experience and fewer contacts. Older workers have lower rates but longer spells.
  • By gender: participation rates differ, and in many countries women are more often in part-time or informal jobs.
  • By region: areas that depended on declining industries have higher rates; capital cities usually lower.
  • By education: workers with fewer qualifications face higher unemployment and lower pay.
  • By country: differences in labour markets, benefits and wage-setting give very different rates, for example persistently lower rates in Germany than in Spain in the 2010s.
  • Developed and developing countries: developing countries often show low measured unemployment but high underemployment and informal work.

3 · Trends

  • The cycle: unemployment rises in a recession and falls in a boom. It lags output, because firms keep workers at first and hire late in a recovery.
  • Okun's law: a rough rule that a fall in output of about 2% below trend goes with about 1 percentage point more unemployment. It varies between countries and periods.
  • Structural change: employment shifts from the primary sector to manufacturing and then services as economies develop. Automation changes the mix of jobs.
  • Flexible work: more part-time, temporary, self-employed and gig work, which blurs the line between employed and unemployed.
  • Demographics: ageing populations shrink the labour force and may lower unemployment.
  • Long-term unemployment tends to rise after deep recessions (hysteresis).

4 · Reading data in the exam

  • Percentage points or percent? A rise from 4% to 6% is a 2 percentage point rise and a 50% rise.
  • Rate or number? A rate can fall while the number of unemployed rises if the labour force grows.
  • Quote figures with units and dates and compare like with like.
  • Trend, then exception, then explanation. Give the overall pattern, one specific figure, then a reason.
  • Correlation is not causation. Say that the data are consistent with a link, then bring in theory to explain it.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Youth unemployment in southern Europe

After 2010, unemployment among under-25s in Spain and Greece was often more than double the national rate and reached about half of young people in the labour force at the peak. Many emigrated or stayed in education.

Exam use: an example of a pattern by age. Use it with hysteresis and the cost of lost skills, and note that falling youth unemployment can reflect emigration or education, not jobs.

Germany's low unemployment

Germany's measured unemployment fell to historic lows in the 2010s, helped by wage restraint, short-time work schemes, apprenticeships and growth in part-time and low-paid jobs.

Exam use: country differences. Evaluate: a low rate can hide underemployment and low pay, and comparisons depend on measurement.

Informal work in developing economies

In many low-income countries most workers are in informal jobs such as street trading or small-scale farming. They count as employed but may work few hours for low pay.

Exam use: why measured unemployment is a poor guide in developing economies. Mention underemployment.

The pandemic and the participation rate

In 2020 many economies lost millions of jobs, but unemployment rates rose less than expected in some countries because many people left the labour force and some were supported by furlough schemes.

Exam use: a reminder to check the employment and participation rates and not only the headline unemployment rate.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/41 · Q1(a) 3 marks The table shows 2017 data: Country A had growth of 7.0% and unemployment of 5.9%. The EU average was growth of 2.4% and unemployment of 8.6%. Use the information to indicate how Country A's economy performed in 2017 compared with the EU as a whole.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Growth: Country A grew much faster than the EU average (7.0% against 2.4%), about three times as fast (1 to 2 marks).
  • Unemployment: Country A's rate was lower (5.9% against 8.6%), a difference of 2.7 percentage points (1 to 2 marks).
  • Credit a short link: strong growth is consistent with lower unemployment. Quote both figures with units.
PlanGrowth sentence with both numbers, unemployment sentence with both numbers, one link.
Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/41 · Q1(c) 6 marks A country's workforce fell after a large number of workers moved abroad: 600,000 left out of a population of 21.2 million, and the population is now 19.4 million. There is now a labour shortage. Describe the changes in the workforce and consider whether they have been a benefit to the country.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Describe: emigration of 600,000 people (about 2.8% of the population in the year they left), population down by 1.8 million, a shortage of labour despite low unemployment.
  • Benefits: lower unemployment, less pressure on public services and housing, remittances sent home, higher bargaining power and wages for those who stay.
  • Costs: labour shortages limit output (lower potential growth), loss of skilled and young workers (a brain drain), smaller tax base, ageing population.
  • A conclusion that weighs short-run gains against long-run costs.
PlanDescribe in two sentences with figures. Two benefits. Two costs. A judgement.
Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/41 · Q7(a) 12 marks Explain the economic costs of unemployment and consider which is the most important.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Waste of scarce resources: output is below potential, so living standards are lower than they could be.
  • Loss of tax revenue and higher spending on benefits, which widens the budget deficit.
  • Costs for individuals and society: lower income, loss of skills (hysteresis), poverty, crime and health costs, inequality.
  • Level 4 needs about three costs analysed, an assessment of each and a conclusion on which is most important (many answers say waste of resources, because the others follow from it).
PlanThree costs, each with cause and effect, then rank them and justify.
Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/43 · Q6(b) 13 marks 'Economic growth will always reduce unemployment, but this will usually create significant problems for other macroeconomic aims.' How far do you agree?
What the mark scheme wants
  • Growth raises demand for labour, so unemployment tends to fall. Use Okun's law and the lag between output and jobs. Say 'always' is too strong: jobless growth, productivity growth, automation, structural change and a rise in participation can all keep unemployment high.
  • Conflicts with other aims: faster growth can raise inflation, cause a current account deficit as imports rise, widen inequality and harm the environment.
  • Evaluation: the problems are not inevitable if growth comes from supply-side improvements, and the severity depends on spare capacity. Reach a judgement on both halves of the statement.
PlanGrowth and jobs, with the word 'always' tested. Then the conflicts. Then spare capacity and a conclusion.
Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/42 · Q6(b) 13 marks Discuss whether reducing unemployment should be the main policy objective for the government of a developing country.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Why it matters: wasted resources, poverty, lost output, social problems, and in developing countries more workers in low-productivity informal work.
  • Why not the main aim: measured unemployment may be low while underemployment is high; growth, development, inflation, debt and the balance of payments may matter more; policies for jobs may conflict with other aims.
  • A reasoned conclusion that links the aim to the country's circumstances.
PlanCase for. Case against, including underemployment. Compare with other aims. Judge.

B · Multiple choice questions (Paper 3)

Choose an answer, then read the reason. Reworded from the paper and question number shown.

Top tips

  • Quote figures with units and years. Say '5.9% in 2017 against 8.6% for the EU', not 'lower'.
  • Distinguish percentage points from percent. A rise from 4% to 6% is 2 percentage points, or 50%.
  • Check who is counted. The unemployment rate falls if people stop searching. Look at the employment rate and participation rate too.
  • Mention the lag. Unemployment usually rises after output falls and keeps falling after a recovery begins.
  • Break the average down. Age, region, gender and education can show very different rates from the national figure.
  • Do not say 'proves'. Data are consistent with a theory. Bring in the economic reason for the pattern.
  • For developing countries, talk about underemployment and the informal sector. Headline unemployment may be low even if many workers have very little work.
