Subsidies: Impact and Incidence

3.2.2: how a subsidy shifts supply, who gains, the cost to the government and the role of elasticity, with the advantages and disadvantages. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

A subsidy is a payment by the government to producers (or sometimes consumers) that lowers the cost of production, so the supply curve shifts to the right (down) by the amount of the subsidy per unit. The price consumers pay falls, the price producers receive rises (market price plus subsidy) and the quantity increases, so consumer surplus and producer surplus both rise. The incidence of the subsidy is shared between consumers and producers according to elasticity, and the cost to the government is the subsidy per unit times the quantity sold, which has an opportunity cost in other spending or higher taxes. Subsidies are used to encourage merit goods, support producers and keep prices of essentials low, but they can be costly, hard to target and difficult to remove.

1 · The impact of a subsidy (3.2.2)

  • The vertical gap between the old and new supply curves is the subsidy per unit.
  • Price paid by consumers falls (but by less than the subsidy); price received by producers rises above the old price; quantity rises.
  • Cost to the government = subsidy per unit × quantity sold after the subsidy.
  • It raises consumer and producer surplus, but the cost is more than the gain in private surplus, so unless there are external benefits there is a welfare loss from over-production.

2 · Incidence and the cost of a subsidy

  • Consumers gain more from a subsidy the more inelastic demand is relative to supply; producers gain more the more inelastic supply is.
  • The more elastic demand, the more quantity responds, so the larger the total cost of the subsidy for the same rate per unit.
  • Subsidising a merit good works best if demand is price elastic; if demand is inelastic, consumption barely rises.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Subsidised public transport

A government pays bus operators a fixed amount per journey. Fares fall, more people use buses and congestion may fall. The cost to taxpayers is large, and the opportunity cost is spending on other services.

Exam use: a subsidy on a merit good and its opportunity cost.

Electric vehicle grants

Grants lower the effective price of electric cars. Demand is fairly elastic, so sales rise by a lot, but some of the money goes to buyers who would have bought one anyway.

Exam use: elastic demand raising the cost, and poor targeting.

Farm subsidies

Payments to farmers raise their incomes and keep food cheap, but can lead to over-production and make farmers dependent on support. Removing them can cause hardship.

Exam use: producer incidence and the difficulty of removing a subsidy.

Free school meals

Meals are provided at no cost to the pupil. Some of the benefit is private, but better nutrition improves learning, an external benefit that justifies public spending.

Exam use: a subsidy on a merit good with external benefits.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q3(a) 8 marks With the help of a diagram, explain how changes in a subsidy can influence the price and quantity sold of a product in a market and consider how expenditure on a subsidy is affected by the price elasticity of demand for the product.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): a correct diagram with labelled axes, demand and supply (1); an increase in a subsidy shifting supply to the right, lowering price and raising quantity, with new equilibrium positions shown (1); a decrease in a subsidy shifting supply to the left, raising price and lowering quantity (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): a subsidy is a sum of money paid by a government to a producer (1); an increase lowers the price charged to consumers and raises quantity (1) and the explanation of how that affects the cost to the government.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): the cost of a subsidy depends on price elasticity of demand: the more elastic, the more quantity rises and the greater the expenditure, with a justified conclusion.
PlanDiagram: subsidy shifts supply right, price down, quantity up. Cost = subsidy × quantity. Elastic demand: quantity rises more, so cost is higher.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A subsidy is a payment by the government to producers per unit of output. On a diagram it shifts the supply curve to the right, by the amount of the subsidy, lowering the equilibrium price and increasing the quantity sold. A bigger subsidy shifts supply further, and removing it shifts supply back to the left, raising price and cutting quantity.

Because the subsidy lowers firms' costs, they supply more at each price, and the price consumers pay falls. The government pays the subsidy on every unit sold, so the cost is the subsidy per unit multiplied by the quantity sold after the subsidy.

The cost depends on the price elasticity of demand. If demand is elastic, the fall in price brings a large rise in quantity, so the total spent on the subsidy is larger. If demand is inelastic, quantity rises less, so the subsidy costs less but also does less to raise consumption.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/22 · Q3(a) 8 marks With the help of an example of each, explain the difference between a merit good and a demerit good and consider whether a subsidy given to a merit good will always be effective in increasing its consumption.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): a merit good is desirable but under-provided or under-consumed because of information failure or a lack of awareness (1); a demerit good is undesirable but over-consumed because of information failure (1); valid examples of both (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): a subsidy may encourage poorer households to consume more because it lowers the price (1); but demand may be price inelastic, so the rise in quantity demanded is less than the fall in price (1); the information failure may persist (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a judgement on whether a subsidy will always be effective.
PlanMerit: under-consumed. Demerit: over-consumed. Subsidy lowers price, but may not work if demand is inelastic or information is poor.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A merit good, such as vaccination, is under-consumed because people do not fully appreciate its benefits. A demerit good, such as tobacco, is over-consumed because people underestimate the harm.

A subsidy lowers the price of a merit good, so poorer households who could not afford it may consume more. But if demand is price inelastic, the rise in quantity will be smaller than the fall in price, and if the problem is a lack of awareness, a lower price may not change behaviour.

So a subsidy will not always be effective. It is most likely to work where cost is the barrier and demand is elastic, and less likely where information failure is the main problem, so information campaigns could do better.

Based on Feb/March 2025 · 9708/22 · Q3(b) 12 marks Assess the extent to which a subsidy is likely to be the best method to increase the consumption of a merit good.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the meaning of a subsidy and a merit good; how a subsidy reduces the costs of production, lowers the price and raises quantity demanded; potential problems such as the opportunity cost of government spending, dependency, incorrect targeting and when to remove provision; alternative methods, such as improved information, analysed for how they would increase consumption.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): an assessment of whether a subsidy is the best method, perhaps depending on price elasticity of demand, cost and the cause of under-consumption, with a justified conclusion.
PlanSubsidy: lowers price, but cost and targeting. Alternatives: information, direct provision. Judge using elasticity and cause of under-consumption.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A merit good, such as education, is under-consumed because consumers do not fully understand its benefits. A subsidy lowers production costs, so the price falls and quantity demanded rises, especially if demand is elastic. But it has an opportunity cost, because the money could be spent elsewhere, it may go to people who would have bought anyway, and it can be hard to remove. Alternative policies include information campaigns, which address the cause of the problem directly but work slowly, and direct provision by the state.

A subsidy is probably most effective where the barrier is cost and demand is elastic, but if under-consumption is mainly due to a lack of information, an information campaign may be better. A combination of a subsidy and information is probably the most effective.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/23 · Q1(a) 2 marks Explain what might be the opportunity cost if a government agreed to pay larger subsidies to its farmers.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Opportunity cost is the cost in terms of the best alternative forgone (1).
  • Other areas of government funding, such as healthcare or education, would have less spending allocated to them (1).
PlanOpportunity cost defined. Name what the money could have been spent on.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up. If the government pays more subsidies to farmers, it has less to spend on something else, such as health care or schools, which is the opportunity cost.

Top tips

  • Draw supply shifting down and right. By the subsidy per unit.
  • Label both prices. Paid by consumers, received by producers.
  • Cost = subsidy × new quantity. Shade the rectangle.
  • Use elasticity. Elastic demand: a bigger rise in quantity and a bigger cost.
  • Always mention opportunity cost. What else could the money buy?
  • Consider targeting. Some benefit goes to people who would have bought anyway.
  • Compare with other policies. Information, regulation, direct provision.
