Consequences of Changes in Foreign Exchange Rates

How a rise or fall in the exchange rate changes prices and the demand for exports and imports, and who gains and loses. Syllabus 6.3.4.

The big idea

A change in the exchange rate changes prices and so the demand for exports and imports. A fall (depreciation or devaluation) makes exports cheaper for foreigners and imports dearer at home: exports may rise, imports may fall, jobs may increase and the current account may improve, but import prices and inflation may rise. A rise (appreciation or revaluation) does the reverse: imports and holidays abroad are cheaper and inflation may fall, but exports become dearer, exporters may lose sales and jobs, and the current account may worsen.

Effects of a fall in the exchange rate

Possible benefits

  • Exports cheaper in foreign currency: exports could rise
  • Imports dearer: demand for imports could fall; domestic goods more competitive
  • Current account may improve; AD rises; output and jobs may rise
  • Tourism becomes cheaper for foreign visitors

Possible costs

  • Imported inflation: imported raw materials, fuel and food cost more; cost-push inflation
  • Firms using imports face higher costs
  • Debts and repayments in foreign currency cost more
  • Holidays abroad dearer; living standards may fall
  • The current account may worsen at first if demand for imports is inelastic

Effects of a rise in the exchange rate

Possible benefits

  • Imports cheaper: lower inflation, lower costs for firms using imports
  • Holidays and investment abroad are cheaper
  • Greater competition forces domestic firms to improve efficiency
  • Foreign debts easier to repay

Possible costs

  • Exports dearer abroad: exports may fall, exporters lose sales and jobs
  • Imports cheaper: domestic firms face more competition
  • Current account may worsen; lower AD, growth and employment
  • Tourism dearer for foreign visitors

How big is the effect? Elasticity

How much a fall improves the current account depends on how price elastic the demand for exports and imports is. If demand is elastic, cheaper exports and dearer imports bring large changes in quantity, so the trade balance improves. If demand is inelastic, quantities barely change, so the fall mainly raises the price of imports and the current account may not improve (at least in the short run).

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

The pound after 2016

The pound fell after the Brexit vote, boosting some exporters and tourism but raising the cost of imported food and fuel, adding to inflation.

Exam use: Both sides of a fall.

Swiss franc surge, 2015

When the Swiss central bank stopped capping the franc, it rose sharply, making Swiss exports and tourism dearer and hurting exporters.

Exam use: A rise hurting exporters.

Turkey's lira

After the lira fell sharply in 2018 and again in 2021, imported energy and goods became much dearer and inflation rose.

Exam use: Imported inflation.

Japan's weak yen

A weak yen has boosted Japanese exporters' profits and tourism, but raised the cost of imported energy and food.

Exam use: Winners and losers from a fall.

Q1 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a fall in a country's foreign exchange rate could reduce a deficit on the current account of its balance of payments. (Feb/Mar 2022, P22)
ReminderExports cheaper, imports dearer, quantities change, revenue and spending change. Elasticity.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A fall in the exchange rate lowers the foreign currency price of exports and raises the home currency price of imports. Exports become more price competitive, so demand for exports could rise and export revenue increase. Imports become dearer, so demand for imports could fall, reducing spending on imports. Higher exports and lower imports reduce the deficit. This depends on demand for exports and imports being elastic; if inelastic, the deficit might not narrow, at least in the short run.
Q2 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a fall in a country's foreign exchange rate could increase its inflation rate. (Oct/Nov 2022, P22)
ReminderImports dearer, raw materials, cost-push; exports cheaper raise demand; less pressure to cut costs.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A fall in the exchange rate makes imports dearer in the home currency. Imported raw materials, fuel and components cost firms more, so costs of production rise and AS could shift left, causing cost-push inflation. Imported consumer goods cost more, directly raising the price level. At the same time exports are cheaper, so demand for them could rise, increasing AD and causing demand-pull inflation. The effect depends on how much the country imports.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how an increase in a country's foreign exchange rate could reduce inflation. (Oct/Nov 2023, P23)
ReminderImports cheaper, input costs fall, competition, exports dearer reduce AD.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A higher exchange rate makes imports cheaper in the home currency. Imported raw materials and goods cost less, so firms' costs fall and prices may fall. More competition from cheaper imports may force domestic firms to hold prices down. Exports become dearer, so net exports and AD may fall, reducing demand-pull inflation. But exports and jobs may suffer.
Q4 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not a rise in a country's foreign exchange rate would benefit its economy. (May/June 2023, P21)
ReminderFor: lower inflation, cheaper imports, cheaper holidays, efficiency. Against: exports dearer, jobs, current account, growth.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A rise in the exchange rate means the currency buys more foreign currency. It could benefit the economy by making imports cheaper, lowering firms' costs and consumer prices, and reducing inflation. Holidays and investment abroad are cheaper, and competition from imports may raise efficiency. This is more likely if the country relies heavily on imports. However, exports become dearer abroad, so exporters may lose sales and cut jobs, and the current account may worsen. Lower net exports reduce AD, which could slow growth. Overall, a rise is likely to benefit consumers and importers but harm exporters, so it depends on how much the country exports, the size of the rise and the state of the economy.
Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not an appreciation of a country's domestic currency will have negative effects on its economy. (Oct/Nov 2021, P21)
ReminderNegative: exports fall, unemployment, current account. Positive: cheaper imports, lower inflation. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
An appreciation is a rise in the value of a floating currency. It could have negative effects because exports become dearer to foreign buyers, so export sales fall and exporting firms may cut output and jobs, reducing growth and worsening the current account. Domestic firms also face more competition from cheaper imports. The effect is larger where demand for exports is elastic. However, cheaper imports reduce costs and inflation, and consumers can buy more imports and travel abroad more cheaply. Overall, an appreciation is likely to harm exporters but help consumers and importers, so the effect on the economy depends on the size of the appreciation and how dependent the country is on exports.
Q6 Discuss 6 marks Discuss whether or not having a strong foreign exchange rate is a problem for a country such as Greece. (May/June 2020, P23)
ReminderStrong rate: dearer exports, fewer tourists; cheaper imports. Greece depends on tourism and exports.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A strong exchange rate means the currency buys a lot of foreign currency. It could be a problem for a country that depends on tourism and exports, because foreign visitors find it dearer and exports are less competitive, so earnings and jobs fall. The current account could worsen. This is more serious if demand is elastic. However, a strong currency makes imports cheaper and reduces inflation, and residents can buy foreign goods and holidays more cheaply. Overall, it is more likely to be a problem for a tourism and export-dependent economy, but benefits consumers.

Top tips

  • Fall: exports cheaper abroad, imports dearer at home. Rise: the reverse. Say 'in foreign currency' and 'in home currency'.
  • Link to the current account, jobs and inflation. Chains for each.
  • Elasticity decides the size. Inelastic demand may mean the current account does not improve.
  • Name who gains and loses. Exporters, importers, consumers, tourists, borrowers.
  • Evaluate with 'it depends on…'. Elasticity, size of change, how much the country trades.
