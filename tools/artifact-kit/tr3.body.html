Consequences of Trade Restrictions

The effects of trade restrictions on the home country and its trading partners, trade wars, and the advantages and disadvantages of restricting free trade. Syllabus 6.2.6.

The big idea

Trade restrictions affect the home country (the country that imposes them) and its trading partners. They help protected producers and workers and may raise government revenue, but they raise prices, reduce choice and efficiency, and invite retaliation, which can start a trade war where everyone loses. Good answers weigh the advantages and disadvantages of restricting free trade and say who gains and who loses.

Impact on the home country

GroupLikely effect of a tariff or quota
ConsumersPay higher prices for imports and (as competition falls) domestic goods; less choice
Protected domestic producersSell more, may expand; may become complacent and inefficient without competition
Firms that use imported inputsCosts rise, so they may cut output or jobs or lose competitiveness
WorkersJobs saved or created in protected industries; jobs lost in industries that import or export
The governmentTariff revenue (not from quotas); but may need to pay subsidies; may raise inflation
The economyImports fall, the current account may improve; but lower efficiency, higher prices (inflation) and lower consumer spending power

Impact on trading partners

  • Exporters lose sales in the country imposing the restrictions, so output and jobs fall in those industries.
  • Their export earnings and growth may fall; a current account deficit may widen.
  • They may retaliate with their own tariffs or quotas, hurting the first country's exporters.
  • Prices of the goods they buy from the first country may rise too.

Trade wars

If one country raises tariffs and others retaliate, trade between them falls, prices rise for everyone, supply chains are disrupted, and growth slows. A trade war can leave all countries worse off than under free trade.

Advantages of restricting free trade

  • Protects infant, declining and strategic industries; saves jobs.
  • Reduces imports and may improve the current account.
  • Raises government revenue (tariffs).
  • Prevents dumping and limits demerit goods.

Disadvantages of restricting free trade

  • Higher prices and less choice for consumers.
  • Lack of competition leads to inefficiency and complacency.
  • Higher costs for firms using imported inputs.
  • Retaliation and trade wars; lower exports.
  • Smuggling; cost of administration; may breach trade agreements.
  • Slower growth for both countries.

The overall effect often depends on the elasticity of demand for imports: if demand is inelastic, a tariff raises prices without cutting imports much, mainly raising revenue; if elastic, imports fall a lot.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

US-China tariffs, 2018 onwards

The US and China raised tariffs on hundreds of billions of dollars of each other's goods. US farmers lost sales, prices rose for some goods, and firms moved some production to other countries.

Exam use: Retaliation and a trade war.

Smoot-Hawley tariffs, 1930

US tariffs raised in 1930 were followed by retaliation by other countries, and world trade fell sharply during the Great Depression.

Exam use: A historic trade war.

Cars and EU protection

The EU's tariff on imported cars raises prices for European consumers but protects European car producers.

Exam use: Gains for producers, losses for consumers.

Chicken wars

Several African countries have restricted imports of cheap frozen chicken to protect local poultry farmers, raising prices for consumers.

Exam use: Protection of local producers against cheap imports.

Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain how a tariff on imports could affect consumers and producers in the country imposing it.
ReminderConsumers: higher price, less choice. Producers: more sales.
A tariff raises the price of imports. Consumers pay more and buy less, and may have less choice, so their living standards could fall. Domestic producers face less competition from imports. They can sell more and raise output, so profits and jobs could rise.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain two ways in which a country's trading partners may be affected when it imposes import restrictions.
ReminderLost exports; retaliation; lower growth.
Its trading partners' exporters lose sales. Imports become dearer in the country with the restrictions, so demand for foreign goods falls, reducing output and jobs in exporting industries. Trading partners may retaliate. If they impose their own tariffs, the first country's exports fall, so both countries lose trade.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a trade war could affect an economy.
ReminderRetaliation; lower trade; higher prices; supply chains; growth.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A trade war is when countries raise tariffs on each other's goods in turn. Exports to the other country fall, so exporters lose sales and workers may lose jobs. Imports become dearer, raising prices for consumers and costs for firms that use imported inputs, which could cause cost-push inflation. Supply chains are disrupted, firms cut investment and growth slows. Some protected industries might gain, but overall both economies are likely to be worse off.
Q4 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not protection in international trade benefits an economy. (May/June 2022, P23)
ReminderFor: current account, infant and sunset industries, strategic, demerit goods. Against: higher prices, retaliation, higher input costs, complacency, unemployment where imports are used.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Protection means measures such as tariffs, quotas and subsidies that restrict imports and help domestic firms. It could benefit an economy by reducing imports and improving the current account, protecting infant industries so they can grow and compete, and maintaining jobs in declining or strategic industries. It may also restrict harmful imports of demerit goods. These gains are larger if protection is temporary and targeted. However, higher prices reduce consumers' purchasing power, and firms that use imported raw materials face higher costs. Other countries may retaliate, making it harder to export, and protected firms may become complacent and produce low-quality goods. Overall, protection is likely to benefit particular industries but harm the economy as a whole if it is widespread and permanent, so it depends on how long, how much and which industries.
Q5 Discuss 6 marks Discuss whether or not an increase in its import tariffs would be likely to benefit the Pakistani economy. (May/June 2020, P22)
ReminderFor: reduce imports and deficit, protect industries, revenue. Against: prices, retaliation, input costs.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
An import tariff is a tax on imports. Higher tariffs could reduce imports and the current account deficit, protect domestic industries and raise government revenue. Domestic firms could expand and create jobs. The gain is greater if demand for imports is elastic. However, consumers would pay higher prices, firms using imported inputs would face higher costs, and trading partners may retaliate, reducing Pakistan's exports. Overall, tariffs could help some industries but their net effect on the economy is uncertain and depends on retaliation and elasticity.
Q6 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not an increase in government subsidies will reduce a deficit on the current account of the balance of payments. (Feb/Mar 2021, P22)
ReminderFor: lowers costs, exports and import substitutes. Against: cost to taxpayers, inefficiency, may not raise exports, imports of inputs, retaliation, time.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A subsidy is a payment to producers that lowers their costs. It could reduce a current account deficit by making domestic goods cheaper, so exports rise and consumers switch from imports to domestic products. Lower costs may also raise output and competitiveness. This works best if demand for the goods is elastic. However, subsidies cost taxpayers and may require higher taxes or borrowing, and other countries may retaliate or complain, reducing exports. Subsidised firms may become inefficient, and if they use imported raw materials, imports could rise. Overall, subsidies may narrow the deficit but the effect depends on elasticity and on how the subsidy is paid for.

Top tips

  • Give effects on the home country and trading partners. The syllabus asks for both.
  • Name who gains and who loses. Consumers, producers, workers, government, foreign exporters.
  • Trade wars harm everyone. Retaliation lowers trade and growth.
  • Evaluate with elasticity. A tariff only cuts imports if demand is elastic.
  • End with 'it depends on…'. Temporary or permanent, which industries, retaliation.
