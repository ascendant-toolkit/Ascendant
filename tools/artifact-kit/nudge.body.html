Nudge Theory

How governments steer choices without forcing them: the behaviour behind it, the types of nudge, examples from the UK and Vietnam, animated diagrams, labs, and every nudge question from the Paper 3 and Paper 4 past papers 2020 to 2025.

This page in four sentences

Nudge theory says people do not always choose rationally: they follow defaults, copy others, put things off and react to how a choice is framed. A nudge changes the way a choice is presented, so that people are steered towards the better option, without banning anything or changing prices. It is cheap and keeps freedom of choice, but its effect can be small, uncertain and short-lived. Governments therefore use nudges beside taxes, subsidies and regulation, not instead of them.

1 · Where it comes from

Traditional economics assumes consumers are rational: they weigh costs and benefits, have full information and maximise utility. Behavioural economics tests that assumption and finds systematic patterns of "irrational" behaviour. Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein used these findings in their book Nudge (2008), and Thaler won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2017. They called the idea libertarian paternalism: the state steers people towards choices that improve their own welfare (paternalism) while leaving them free to choose otherwise (libertarian).

A nudge counts only if it is easy and cheap to avoid. Putting fruit at eye level is a nudge. Banning chocolate is not. The syllabus words are behavioural insights and nudge theory, listed with the other tools for correcting market failure.

3 · The main types of nudge

  • Defaults: the option you get if you do nothing (opt-out schemes).
  • Social norms: telling people what others do.
  • Framing and labels: calorie labels, traffic-light food labels, wording of letters.
  • Placement: where an option sits on the shelf, the website or the form.
  • Reminders and commitment devices: messages that tell people when to act, or let them lock in a promise.

The exam trapA tax is not a nudge, even when its purpose is to change behaviour, because it changes the price. A ban is regulation. A health warning or campaign is information, which the syllabus lists separately but which often counts as a nudge when it uses persuasion or example. The Paper 4 mark scheme defined nudge theory as policies that influence behaviour by persuasion or example to achieve one of the government's main economic aims.

5 · How nudges correct market failure

  • Demerit goods (smoking, sugary food, gambling): consumers under-estimate the harm, so demand is too high. A nudge moves them back.
  • Merit goods (pensions, vaccination, education, organ donation): consumers under-value the benefit, so demand is too low. A default or reminder raises it.
  • Information failure: labels and simpler forms close the gap.
  • Negative externalities: social norm messages can reduce littering, energy use or tax evasion, where a fine would be costly to enforce.

6 · Evaluating nudges

ForAgainst
Cheap compared with subsidies or enforcement, and quick to runEffects may be small, especially where the good is addictive or demand is inelastic
Preserves freedom of choice, so it may be more acceptable politicallyEffects may fade as people get used to the nudge
Can be tested first on a small group (randomised trials), so waste is limitedHard to measure the long-run result; a trial may not scale up
Helps where taxes are unpopular or hard to collectEthical concerns: who decides what is "best", and is steering people a form of manipulation?
Can raise tax revenue or lower public spending (more tax paid on time, more people saving)Firms can use the same tricks against consumers (hidden fees, "sludge" that makes cancelling hard)

Evaluation points to use: the size and time of the effect, how elastic behaviour is, whether it works alone or only with a tax, subsidy or law, the cost against the benefit, the quality of the evidence, and whether it is acceptable to the public.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

United Kingdom

The Behavioural Insights Team ("Nudge Unit")

The UK government set up a small unit in the Cabinet Office in 2010 to apply behavioural economics to public policy and test ideas with trials before rolling them out. It later became an independent organisation, and similar units now exist in many countries. The Paper 4 extracts on this topic describe exactly these "Nudge Units" in the US, UK and Australia.

Exam use: evidence that governments take behavioural insights seriously. Evaluate: trials test small changes, and results may not scale to the whole economy.

Automatic enrolment into workplace pensions

Since 2012 UK employers must enrol eligible workers in a pension scheme automatically, and workers must actively opt out. Participation among eligible employees rose from roughly half to around nine in ten. Tax relief alone had not persuaded many people to save.

Exam use: a default for a merit good, countering present bias. This is the same mechanism as the "good luck" and saving examples in the Paper 4 extract. Evaluate: contribution rates are often the minimum, so the saving may still be too low for a comfortable retirement.

Social norm letters from HMRC

Trials by the tax authority added messages such as "most people in your town pay their tax on time" to reminder letters. More people paid on time than with the standard letter, at almost no extra cost.

Exam use: the standard answer to "how could a nudge raise tax revenue or reduce evasion?", which links to the Paper 3 questions on tax evasion. Evaluate: it helps the people who intend to pay, but does little against deliberate evasion.

Organ donation consent

Wales changed to an opt-out system in 2015, England followed in 2020 and Scotland in 2021. Families are still consulted, so the system is a soft default. Consent rates are only one factor in donation numbers, which also depend on hospital capacity and family decisions.

Exam use: default nudge with a merit good. Evaluate: the benefit depends on the whole system, not just the default.

Calorie labels on menus, and the 5p bag charge

Large restaurants in England must show calories on menus (since 2022), an information and framing tool. The 5p carrier bag charge (2015) cut single-use bag use sharply, but it works through a price, so it is closer to a tax than a nudge.

Exam use: a good way to show you know the boundary. A small charge is a price change, so say it is not a pure nudge, although its effect on behaviour is larger than the size of the charge would suggest.

Vietnam

The hand-washing song and the 5K message

In 2020 the Ministry of Health and the National Institute of Occupational and Environmental Health backed a catchy hand-washing song and the "5K" message (face masks, disinfection, distance, no gatherings, health declaration). The campaign used simple memorable wording, a shared social identity and a song that went viral online, with very low cost per person reached.

Exam use: persuasion and social norms working as an information nudge for a public good, with a positive externality. Evaluate: it was supported by testing, tracing and legal rules, so the nudge was not working alone.

QR code payments at the till

In Vietnam shops, street stalls and markets display a QR code, and banks and apps compete to make payment as easy as possible. Paying by phone is made the easiest option, and the state encourages cashless payment.

Exam use: choice architecture and salience: making one option easy raises its use without forbidding cash. Evaluate: the effect depends on phone and bank access, so rural and older people may be left behind.

Voluntary social insurance: a possible nudge

Many Vietnamese workers in the informal sector are not covered by social insurance because joining is a voluntary, active choice. Economists often suggest simpler sign-up, reminders and matching contributions from the state as ways to use present bias and inertia in reverse.

Exam use: applies the pension default to a developing economy. Evaluate: a default needs a formal employer, so it is harder to use where many people are self-employed.

Where Vietnam used law and tax instead

Helmets for motorcycle riders became compulsory in 2007 and drink-driving is banned outright with heavy fines, both regulation. Single-use plastic bags are being phased out under the 2020 environment law, and a special consumption tax on sugary drinks has been legislated to start in 2027 (check the current details). These are not nudges.

Exam use: shows the contrast. The helmet law worked where a nudge alone might not have, because the harm (death or head injury) is serious and enforcement was strong. A sensible answer says why a government might choose a ban, tax or nudge for a given problem.

Nudge theory is the idea that governments can influence people's behaviour, by persuasion, example or by changing how choices are presented, without forcing them or changing prices, so as to help achieve a policy aim such as higher employment, more tax revenue or better health.

May/June 2020 · 9708/41 · Q1(b) 6 marks The extract says that nudge policies were used to get people back to work, to pay their taxes on time and to save for pensions or study for better qualifications. Consider whether the information provides any evidence that nudge theory could increase an economy's GDP.
What the mark scheme wantsUse the evidence in the extract. Three routes to higher GDP are rewarded: taxes paid raise government revenue that could fund projects, more people working cuts unemployment and raises incomes, and saving for education could raise skills and productivity. A conclusion (hedged) earns the evaluation mark.
Command word: ConsiderLook at both the evidence for and its limits, then judge. Quote or refer to the extract.
KANE

The extract says nudge policies encouraged people to pay their taxes on time. More tax revenue could allow the government to spend more on infrastructure and projects, and that spending is a component of aggregate demand and could raise GDP.

It also says nudges helped to get people back to work. Falling unemployment means more output and more income, which may raise consumption and GDP through the multiplier. Nudging people to study for better qualifications could raise skill levels and productivity, which is likely to raise the productive capacity of the economy in the long run.

However, the extract only says nudges worked "with some success", and that they were not enough to remove the budget deficit, so the effect on GDP is likely to be small and may take years. The evidence is a claim rather than data, so it supports the view that nudge theory could increase GDP, but does not prove it.

May/June 2021 · 9708/41 · Q1(b) 4 marks The article describes how a change in the wording of a letter sped up tax payments, how personalised text messages raised job fair attendance, and how automatic enrolment into pensions helped people to save. Is there evidence in the article that a knowledge of behavioural economics can help public policy?
What the mark scheme wantsEvidence from the text, tied to a policy aim: lower unemployment (job fair attendance), higher tax payments that could help to reduce the budget deficit, and more saving which could reduce demand-pull inflation pressure. Four marks means about two developed points.
Command word: Is there evidenceYes or no, then use the article. Spend your time on evidence, not on defining behavioural economics.
KANE

Yes. The article says jobseekers were nearly twice as likely to turn up to a job fair when the text used their names, and nearly three times as likely if "good luck" was added. More people attending job fairs could lead to more people finding work, which would lower unemployment and raise output.

More behaviourally sensitive wording in a letter sped up tax payments, which may improve the government's cash flow and help reduce a budget deficit without raising tax rates. The evidence comes from small trials, so it may not apply to the whole economy, but it does suggest behavioural insights can help policy at low cost.

B · Practice questions with model answers

Practice (not a past paper question) 8 marks A government wants more workers to save for retirement. Explain how a default could help, and consider one limit of this approach.
PlanDefine the nudge. Present bias and inertia: why people do not save. Opt-out raises participation. One limit: minimum contributions, opt-outs, or poor effect on the self-employed.
KANE

A default is the option that applies if a person does nothing, so under automatic enrolment workers are placed in a pension scheme unless they choose to leave.

Many people suffer from present bias, so they value spending now more than saving for later, and inertia means they rarely fill in forms. Under an opt-in system, many may not join even if they would have wanted to. An opt-out system uses the same inertia in the other direction, so most workers are likely to stay in the scheme, and participation could rise sharply without a tax or a ban. In the UK, automatic enrolment from 2012 was followed by a large rise in participation among eligible employees.

However, the default contribution is often low, and many people might stay at it, so total saving may still be too low. Workers in the informal sector or the self-employed are also not reached by an employer default, so the policy is likely to be less effective there.

Practice (not a past paper question) 20 marks Evaluate the view that nudge theory is more effective than taxation in reducing the consumption of demerit goods.
What a Level 4 answer includes
  • Analysis (14): why demerit goods are over-consumed (information failure, present bias, external costs); how a nudge works (defaults, placement, labels, social norms) and how a tax works (diagram: unit tax shifts supply left, with price and quantity change, tax revenue); comparison with a diagram of an inelastic demand that makes a tax raise revenue more than it cuts consumption.
  • Evaluation (6): size of the effect, elasticity, time, cost, regressive effect of the tax on poorer households, ethical concern, evidence, and a judgement on when each tool works best (often together).
KANE

A demerit good, such as sugary drinks, is a good that is over-consumed because consumers under-estimate its harm and because it creates external costs. A nudge changes how the choice is presented without limiting it or changing the price, while a tax raises the price.

A nudge such as moving sugary drinks to a lower shelf, adding clear labels or making a smaller size the default could reduce demand, shifting the demand curve to the left. Because people remain free to choose, the nudge uses the behaviour that causes the problem, and it may be cheap to introduce. A tax shifts supply to the left, raising the price and reducing quantity along the demand curve. The fall in consumption depends on the price elasticity of demand: if demand is inelastic, the percentage fall in quantity is smaller than the percentage rise in price, so the tax may do little to reduce consumption but raise considerable revenue.

In England, the Soft Drinks Industry Levy led manufacturers to cut the sugar in many drinks, which shows that a tax can change behaviour on the supply side as well as the demand side. A nudge on its own has no such effect on producers, and firms may also use their own nudges, such as prominent displays, to push sales the other way.

Overall, the answer is likely to depend on the good and on the evidence. Where harm is serious or habit-forming, as with cigarettes, nudges are likely to be too weak and a tax or a ban is probably needed. Where people are mainly distracted or undecided, as with food choice in a canteen, a nudge may be more cost-effective and avoid the regressive impact of a tax on lower-income households. A tax raises revenue that can be used to fund health care, whereas a nudge does not. The strongest case is most likely to be a combination, since nudges often work better when a small price signal supports them, and the effects of nudges also tend to be measured over short periods, which makes their long-run impact uncertain. Therefore the view is probably only true for goods where consumers are open to persuasion and demand is inelastic to price.

Top tips

  • Define it in one line. Influencing behaviour by persuasion or example, or by changing how a choice is presented, without forcing it or changing the price. That wins the 2 marks.
  • A tax is not a nudge. Neither is a ban, a subsidy, a fine or a licence. The Paper 3 questions test this again and again.
  • Information and persuasion usually count. Telling people what others do, or the harm of tax evasion, is the standard "nudge" answer in tax questions (2025 papers).
  • Name the behaviour. Inertia, present bias, social norms, framing or salience. This turns a description into analysis.
  • Link to the failure. Demerit good, merit good, information failure or an externality. Then say which curve moves.
  • For data response, quote the extract. "Nearly three times as likely..." earns more than general theory.
  • Evaluate with three points: size and duration of effect, cost, and whether it works alone. Finish with a hedged judgement.
