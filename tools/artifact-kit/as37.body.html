Policies to Redistribute Income and Wealth

3.3.4: progressive, proportional and regressive taxes, marginal and average rates, transfer payments, the minimum wage and state provision. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Governments redistribute income and wealth to reduce inequality and poverty, using four main policies: a minimum wage, transfer payments (benefits and pensions), progressive taxes (income tax, inheritance and capital taxes) and state provision of essential goods and services such as education and health care. Progressive taxes take a larger proportion of higher incomes; the marginal rate of tax is higher than the average rate, whereas a regressive tax such as a sales tax takes a larger share of low incomes and a proportional tax takes the same share. Each policy has costs: higher taxes may weaken incentives, benefits cost money and may create dependency, a minimum wage above equilibrium may raise unemployment, and state provision has an opportunity cost. The best mix depends on how large the gap is and how the economy would respond.

1 · Taxes and rates

TypeShare of income taken as income risesExample
ProgressiveRises: higher earners pay a larger shareIncome tax with bands: 0%, 20%, 40%
ProportionalStays the sameA flat 15% tax on income
RegressiveFalls: low earners pay a larger share of incomeSales tax or a duty on fuel or tobacco
  • Marginal rate of tax (MRT) = the tax on the extra income earned = change in tax ÷ change in income. Average rate (ART) = total tax ÷ total income.
  • Under a progressive tax the MRT is above the ART; under a regressive tax the MRT is below the ART.
  • Direct taxes (income, profit, inheritance, capital gains) are paid directly to the government by the person or firm; indirect taxes are on spending (VAT, duties).
  • Inheritance and capital taxes tax transfers of wealth and gains on assets, so they target wealth inequality; they can be avoided by transferring assets early.

2 · The minimum wage

  • A minimum wage is a price floor in the labour market. If set above the equilibrium wage, workers who keep jobs earn more, but quantity of labour demanded falls and quantity supplied rises, so unemployment may rise.
  • It matters how elastic labour demand is: if demand is inelastic few jobs are lost and low-paid workers gain; if elastic, more jobs are lost.
  • It does not help people without a job or those with wealth differences, so it is a narrow policy.

3 · Transfer payments and state provision

  • Transfer payments (unemployment benefit, pensions, child benefit) move money from taxpayers to recipients without any output being produced. They raise the incomes of the poorest directly but cost taxpayers, may reduce the incentive to work and need administration.
  • State provision of essentials (education, health care, housing, free school meals) is redistribution in kind: poorer households gain most because it is a larger share of their income. It is costly and subject to waiting lists.

4 · Worked examples

A progressive income tax. Suppose the first $10,000 of income is tax-free, the next $20,000 is taxed at 20% and income above $30,000 is taxed at 40%.

  • The tax is progressive because the average rate rises with income (0%, 13.3%, 24%, 32%), and the marginal rate (40%) is above the average rate.
  • Marginal rate of tax = change in tax ÷ change in income. From $30,000 to $50,000: ($12,000 − $4,000) ÷ $20,000 = 40%.

A regressive sales tax. A 10% sales tax is paid on spending. A household with income of $10,000 spends $9,000 and pays $900, which is 9% of its income. A household with income of $100,000 spends $60,000 and pays $6,000, which is 6% of its income. The tax takes a smaller share of the higher income, so it is regressive even though the rate on spending is the same.

5 · Effects of a minimum wage: a worked example

Suppose the equilibrium wage is $8 an hour and 1,000 workers are employed. A minimum wage of $10 is introduced, and labour demand falls to 900 workers, while 1,100 want to work.

  • Winners: the 900 workers who keep jobs gain $2 an hour (and those on low pay in other jobs may also get a rise).
  • Losers: 100 workers lose their jobs, and there are 200 unemployed in total (1,100 − 900), so the policy helps some low-paid workers at the cost of others.
  • Employers: costs rise, so some may cut hours, raise prices or replace labour with machines.
  • If demand for labour is inelastic, few jobs are lost; if it is elastic, many jobs are lost.

6 · Evaluating redistribution policies

  • Incentives: high marginal rates of tax may reduce the reward for working and enterprise; generous benefits may discourage job search.
  • Avoidance and evasion: high taxes encourage people to move income or assets out of reach.
  • Cost and funding: transfers and state provision need tax revenue, so there is an opportunity cost.
  • Targeting: universal benefits help the poor but also go to the better off; means-tested ones are cheaper but may create poverty traps.
  • Time lags: improving education changes the distribution of income only over many years.
  • Inequality of wealth: income taxes and benefits do not directly address it; inheritance and capital taxes do, but are hard to collect.
  • Judgement: a mix of progressive taxes, transfers and provision of education and health is likely to be more effective than any one policy.
Exam tipName the policy, explain how it narrows the gap, then give a limit. For essays, compare at least two and conclude which is most effective and why. Do not confuse progressive (a feature of a tax) with direct (who pays it).

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Income tax bands

A worker earning $20,000 pays tax only on income above the allowance, so the average rate is low. A manager earning $90,000 pays 40% on the top slice, so the average rate is much higher. Both pay the same rate on a given slice, but the average rate rises with income.

Exam use: progressive taxation and the marginal versus average rate.

Sales tax on food

A flat 10% sales tax on food takes the same percentage of each purchase, but low-income households spend most of their income on food, so the tax takes a bigger share of their income. It is regressive.

Exam use: why indirect taxes can be regressive.

Child benefit and pensions

These are transfer payments: money is moved from taxpayers to families and pensioners with no output in return. They lift the incomes of low-income groups, but they cost money and may affect work choices.

Exam use: transfer payments.

A national minimum wage

Many countries set a legal wage floor. Low-paid workers who keep jobs gain, but some firms may hire fewer workers or replace them with machines, so the overall effect depends on how elastic demand for labour is.

Exam use: the minimum wage and the unemployment risk.

Progressive income tax bands

A government taxes the first slice of income at 0%, the next at 20% and income above a higher threshold at 40%. Higher earners pay a larger share of their income, which narrows the gap after tax.

Exam use: a progressive tax and the marginal rate.

Free school meals

Providing free meals in school gives children from poorer households a meal worth a larger share of their family's income than for richer households. It is redistribution in kind, paid for by taxpayers.

Exam use: state provision as redistribution.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/23 · Q3(a) 8 marks Explain the difference between a progressive direct tax and a regressive indirect tax, with an example of each.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): a direct tax is a tax on income and wealth (1) and an indirect tax is a tax on the sale of or spending on goods and services (1).
  • Application (up to 3): a valid direct tax, such as income tax (1); why it may be progressive, with reference to the burden of tax as income rises (1) and the marginal rate of taxation being higher than the average rate (1).
  • Application (up to 3): a valid indirect tax, such as VAT or a duty (1); why it is regressive, with reference to the relative burden on higher and lower income groups (1) and the marginal rate being lower than the average rate (1).
PlanDirect: income tax, rate rises with income, MRT above ART. Indirect: sales tax, takes more of low incomes, MRT below ART.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A direct tax is paid directly to the government by the person or firm on their income or wealth, while an indirect tax is placed on spending on goods and services and collected by retailers.

Income tax is a progressive direct tax: the rate rises with income, so higher earners pay a larger share. The marginal rate, the tax on the next dollar, is above the average rate. VAT or a fuel duty is an indirect tax that is regressive: everyone pays the same amount on the same purchase, but it takes a larger share of a low income because the poor spend a higher proportion of income. The marginal rate is lower than the average rate as income rises.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/24 · Q3(b) 12 marks Assess whether a minimum wage policy is the best way to redistribute income in an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): how a minimum wage works, as a price floor in the labour market, raising the pay of low-paid workers; the possible costs, such as unemployment if set above the equilibrium wage and its limited effect on those out of work; alternative policies, such as progressive taxes, transfer payments or state provision, analysed for how they would redistribute income. Indicative content may include these points.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether the minimum wage is the best method, which may depend on its level, labour demand elasticity and the aims of the policy, with a conclusion.
PlanMin wage: raises pay, unemployment risk, ignores unemployed. Alternatives: progressive tax, transfers, state provision. Judge: a mix.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A minimum wage is a floor below which pay cannot fall. If set above the equilibrium wage it raises the incomes of low-paid workers who keep their jobs, at no cost to the government. But employers may hire fewer workers, which could raise unemployment, especially if demand for labour is elastic, and it does nothing for the unemployed. Progressive income taxes narrow the gap after tax and fund transfer payments to the poorest, but may weaken incentives. State provision of education and health care helps poorer households in kind, but it has an opportunity cost.

A minimum wage is unlikely to be the best method on its own, because it affects only those in work. It is probably most effective as part of a mix with progressive taxes and transfers, and its success depends on the level at which it is set.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/22 · Q2(b) 12 marks Assess the extent to which government policies to redistribute income and wealth are likely to be successful.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): an explanation of at least two policies, such as progressive taxes, inheritance and capital taxes, transfer payments, a minimum wage and state provision of essential goods, and how each could reduce inequality; the limits on each, such as avoidance, disincentive effects, dependency, cost and unemployment.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on the likely success, based on factors such as the size of the policies, how the economy responds and time lags, with a conclusion.
PlanTwo or more policies, each with how it narrows the gap and a limit. Judge: partly successful, depends on size and design.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A progressive income tax takes a larger share from higher earners, and the revenue can fund transfer payments such as unemployment benefit, which raise the incomes of the poorest. State provision of education and health care can help poorer households gain skills and good health. But high marginal tax rates could reduce the incentive to work and encourage avoidance, benefits cost money and may create dependency, and wealth is difficult to tax because assets can be hidden or moved.

Such policies are likely to reduce inequality to some extent, but are rarely fully successful. How successful depends on how well they are targeted, how much the economy responds, and how long they take to work. A combination is probably more effective than any single policy.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/21 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain the reasons why governments impose taxes and consider whether an increase in income tax is fairer than an increase in sales taxes.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): a clear understanding of the various reasons why governments impose taxes.
  • Analysis (up to 3): the distinction between a direct tax, such as income tax, and an indirect tax, such as a sales tax, with appropriate reference to fairness.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a valid judgement on whether an increase in income tax is fairer than an increase in sales taxes, and a conclusion.
PlanReasons for tax: revenue, redistribution, discourage demerit goods. Income tax progressive; sales tax regressive. Judge on fairness.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Governments tax to raise revenue to pay for public goods and services, to redistribute income, and to discourage consumption of demerit goods.

An increase in income tax is direct and usually progressive: those with higher incomes pay a larger share, so it is generally seen as fairer. An increase in sales taxes is indirect and tends to be regressive, because people on low incomes spend a larger share of their income and so lose a larger share to the tax.

Income tax is therefore likely to be fairer, although high rates may reduce the incentive to work. A sales tax is easier to collect, and exemptions for essentials such as basic foods could make it less regressive.

Top tips

  • Know the three tax types. Progressive, proportional, regressive: describe what happens to the share of income.
  • Marginal versus average. Progressive: MRT above ART. Regressive: MRT below ART.
  • Direct is not the same as progressive. Progressive describes the rate pattern; direct describes who pays.
  • Transfer payments have no output. Pensions and benefits, not hospital spending.
  • Minimum wage: say above equilibrium. Then unemployment may rise.
  • Always give a limit. Incentives, avoidance, cost, dependency.
  • Judge with a mix. No single policy is usually best.
