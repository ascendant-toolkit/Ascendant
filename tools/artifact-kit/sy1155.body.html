External Debt, the IMF and the World Bank

11.5.5 to 11.5.7: why countries build up external debt, the consequences of a high debt burden, and the roles of the IMF and the World Bank. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

External debt is the money a country (its government, firms and households) owes to foreign lenders; it arises from persistent current account deficits, borrowing for investment or consumption, shocks such as falling export prices or rising interest rates, and a falling currency when the debt is in foreign currency. The burden is shown by the debt service ratio (interest and repayments as a share of export earnings), and heavy debt can divert spending from health and education, force austerity and reduce investment. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) promotes exchange rate stability and lends short-term to countries with balance of payments problems, usually with conditions, whereas the World Bank provides long-term loans and grants for development projects and poverty reduction.

1 · Causes of external debt (11.5.5)

  • Persistent current account deficits: importing more than is earned from exports means borrowing from abroad to pay for the difference.
  • Borrowing for investment or consumption: developing countries borrow because domestic saving is low (the savings gap), to build infrastructure or industry, or to fund government deficits. Borrowing is sound if the investment earns a return above the interest cost.
  • Shocks: a fall in the price of the main export (commodity prices), a rise in oil import prices, a rise in world interest rates (variable-rate debt), a recession in key trading partners, natural disasters or war.
  • Exchange rate falls: when debt is owed in dollars and the local currency depreciates, the debt costs more in local currency.
  • Poor management and corruption: wasteful projects, capital flight and unsound lending.

2 · Consequences of external debt

  • Debt service ratio = (interest + repayments on external debt in a year) ÷ export earnings × 100. A high ratio (often above about 20%) leaves little foreign exchange for imports of capital goods and essentials. The debt-to-GDP ratio is another common measure.
  • Opportunity cost: money for debt service is not available for health, education and infrastructure, lowering human capital and growth.
  • Austerity: to find the funds, governments may cut spending and raise taxes, which reduces aggregate demand and growth.
  • Investment and confidence: a high debt burden raises the risk premium and the interest rate on new borrowing, discourages foreign investors and may cause the currency to fall, which raises the cost of foreign-currency debt (a debt trap).
  • Loss of independence: countries in difficulty must turn to the IMF or other lenders, accepting conditions.
  • Benefits if used well: borrowed funds invested in productive assets raise future output and exports, so debt can be repaid from the extra income.
  • Solutions: debt relief or cancellation (for example the HIPC initiative), rescheduling and restructuring, shifting to grants and concessional loans, raising exports and growth, and improving governance. Relief may cause moral hazard (encouraging irresponsible borrowing in future).

3 · The International Monetary Fund (11.5.6)

  • Set up in 1944 with over 190 member countries. Its main roles: promote international monetary cooperation and exchange rate stability; surveillance of members' economies and advice; lending to countries with balance of payments problems (as a short- to medium-term lender when private lenders will not); and technical assistance and training in central banking and public finance.
  • Conditionality: loans usually come with conditions, often called structural adjustment: cut the budget deficit (expenditure reducing), control inflation, free trade and reduce import restrictions, privatise state firms, deregulate markets, and sometimes devalue the currency or expand exports.
  • Arguments for: restores confidence and stabilises the economy; gives access to funds when no one else will lend; reforms may raise efficiency and growth.
  • Arguments against: austerity can deepen recessions and raise unemployment and poverty; devaluation raises import prices (cost-push inflation); opening to imports can hurt infant industries and widen the current account deficit; advice may be one-size-fits-all and ignore social effects and environmental costs; it limits national sovereignty.

4 · The World Bank (11.5.7)

  • Consists of the IBRD (loans to middle-income countries) and the IDA (grants and interest-free or very low-interest loans to the poorest countries). It raises funds on world capital markets and from donor governments.
  • Roles: long-term loans and grants for development projects such as roads, power, water, schools and health; reducing poverty; technical advice and research; and helping with debt and policy reform. It also provides guarantees that reduce risk for private investors.
  • Criticisms: conditions may be similar to the IMF's, projects may harm the environment or displace people, loans add to debt, and richer countries have more voting power.

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

The Latin American debt crisis, 1980s

Many countries borrowed heavily in dollars in the 1970s. When world interest rates rose sharply and commodity prices fell, they could not repay. The IMF lent on condition of austerity and reforms, and growth and living standards fell in what is often called a lost decade.

Exam use: causes of debt, the debt service burden and IMF conditionality.

Asian financial crisis, 1997 to 1998

Several East Asian economies had borrowed short-term in dollars. When their currencies collapsed, the cost of the debt in local money soared. IMF loans came with conditions such as high interest rates and spending cuts, which many argued made the recession deeper.

Exam use: the effect of depreciation on foreign-currency debt and criticism of IMF conditions.

Debt relief for the poorest

Under the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries initiative and later programmes, creditors cancelled part of the debts of dozens of the poorest countries on condition that the money saved went to health and education. Some countries saw spending on social services rise.

Exam use: debt relief as a solution, with moral hazard as a limit.

World Bank infrastructure loans

The World Bank has lent for projects such as roads, hydropower, water systems and schools in low- and middle-income countries. Where well designed they raise productivity and incomes, but some large projects have been criticised for displacing communities or damaging the environment.

Exam use: the World Bank's role in long-term development lending and the costs that can come with it.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/43 · Q7 25 marks The IMF lends money to developing countries but requires that these countries reduce restrictions on imports, focus on the export of primary goods and accept a devaluation of their currencies. Based on this, assess whether developing countries should continue to borrow from the IMF.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Examine the likely effect on a developing country of each of the three conditions, and assess whether the negative effects might outweigh the benefits of the loan.
  • Removing import restrictions may cause balance of payments problems; focusing on primary exports may lead to unstable incomes and low productivity; devaluation may cause cost-push inflation.
  • Level 4 (18 to 25) analyses each requirement, weighs their combined effect against the benefits of the loan and reaches a conclusion.
PlanCondition 1: free trade. Condition 2: primary exports. Condition 3: devaluation. Benefits of the loan. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The IMF lends to countries with balance of payments difficulties, usually on condition of policy changes. Removing import restrictions could expose infant industries to competition and raise imports, which may worsen the current account in the short run. Focusing on primary exports may leave the economy dependent on volatile prices with low productivity, so incomes could be unstable. A devaluation makes imports dearer and may cause cost-push inflation, especially if the country relies on imported inputs, although it could also make exports more competitive if the Marshall-Lerner condition holds.

The loan may still be worth taking. It restores confidence, supplies foreign exchange to pay for essential imports and avoids a default, which could be worse. The answer may depend on how severe the crisis is, whether other sources of finance exist and how flexible the conditions are. Overall, borrowing from the IMF may be reasonable as a last resort, but the conditions suggest that developing countries should try to avoid needing the loan.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/43 · Q1(d) 8 marks The IMF encouraged Indonesia to expand its production of palm oil, a major export, to help to solve its external debt problems. To what extent do you agree with this advice?
What the mark scheme wants
  • In support: the country had external debt problems and palm oil was a big earner of foreign exchange, which could repay debt and raise incomes at home (up to 4 marks).
  • Against: expansion destroys rainforest; fires add to greenhouse gases and destroy habitats; external costs exceed private costs; dependence on one crop (monoculture) leaves prices influenced by foreign buyers.
PlanFor: foreign exchange and incomes. Against: environmental costs and monoculture. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The IMF advice could help the debt problem. Palm oil is a large earner of foreign currency, so higher exports may reduce the current account deficit and provide funds to repay external debt, and may raise rural incomes. However, clearing rainforest creates external costs such as lost habitats and greenhouse gas emissions that may exceed the private benefits. Dependence on one crop also leaves earnings exposed to price falls. So the advice may help in the short run but be less sound for sustainable growth.

External debt may arise because a developing country has a persistent current account deficit or low saving, so it borrows abroad to invest or to fund the government. Shocks such as a fall in commodity prices, a rise in world interest rates or a fall in the currency (when debt is in dollars) could make the debt harder to repay. The debt service ratio is interest and repayments as a share of export earnings. A high ratio means a large part of foreign exchange earnings goes on the debt, leaving less for imports of capital goods. The government may also cut spending on health and education or raise taxes, which could reduce growth and human capital.

The burden could cause the currency to fall and investors to leave, making the debt even costlier. However, if the borrowing was invested in productive assets that raise exports and output, the debt may be manageable. So the consequences may depend on how the funds were used and on how large the ratio is.

Top tips

  • Separate causes and consequences. Deficits, shocks and a weaker currency cause debt. Austerity, lower investment and dependence follow.
  • Define the ratio. Debt service ratio = (interest + repayments) ÷ export earnings.
  • Mention foreign-currency debt. A fall in the exchange rate raises the cost of debt owed in dollars.
  • Split IMF and World Bank. IMF: short-term, balance of payments, monetary stability. World Bank: long-term development.
  • Name the conditions. Cut the deficit, free trade, privatisation, sometimes devaluation.
  • Weigh benefits against costs. The loan avoids default but conditions may bring austerity and inflation.
  • Use the three conditions in 25 marks. Analyse each, then compare to the benefits.
