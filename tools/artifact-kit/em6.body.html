Extension: Changing Patterns and Levels of Employment

How who works, where and on what terms changes, and why employment and unemployment can move together. Coursebook Chapter 30; not in the 2027–29 syllabus.

Extension: not in the 2027–29 syllabusCambridge removed changing patterns and levels of employment from the 0455 syllabus for 2027, 2028 and 2029. Your coursebook still covers it (Chapter 30). Read it for background and stronger examples. It is not a set topic in those exams.

The big idea

The pattern of employment (who works, where, for whom and on what terms) tends to change as an economy develops. The level of employment is how many people have jobs. Employment and unemployment do not always move in opposite directions, because the labour force can change too.

How patterns of employment change

ChangeWhat tends to happenPossible reason or effect
Industrial structureWorkers move from primary to secondary, then to tertiaryRising incomes and productivity shift demand and jobs. Some industries expand while others contract, so workers may need to be occupationally and geographically mobile
Women in workThe number and proportion of women employed has risen in most countriesChanging social attitudes and less discrimination may open up jobs and raise women's pay
Public and private sectorIn many countries the private sector's share has grownA move towards a market economy, including privatisation
Full-time and part-timeMost work full-time; some work part-timePart-time may be a choice (family care, other interests) or forced because full-time jobs cannot be found
Employees and self-employedSelf-employment is rising in some countries and already high in othersIn some lower-income countries many self-employed people work in the informal economy
FlexibilityFirms look for workers who can adjust quicklyNumbers, hours, location, tasks or pay may change (see 4.6.1)

Formal and informal economies

Formal

  • More likely to have social security, employment protection and legal rights.
  • Unions may bargain for better conditions.
  • A growing formal economy tends to raise job quality and productivity.

Informal

  • Includes some self-employed, migrant and casual workers.
  • Minimum wage laws and pensions may not reach them.
  • Training, productivity and pay tend to be lower.

High and low quality employment

High quality

Skilled and interesting work with training, chances to progress, good conditions and relatively secure jobs. Its share tends to rise as an economy develops.

Low quality

Unskilled work with little or no training and poor conditions.

Changes in the level of employment

What happensHow it is possible
Employment up, unemployment downUnemployed people fill at least some of the new jobs, and the labour force does not grow by more
Both upThe labour force grows faster than the number of jobs
Unemployment down, no rise in employmentUnemployed people retire, enter full-time education, emigrate or stop looking

Why the labour force changes size

The labour force may grow if the working-age population rises, the participation rate rises, or both. The working-age population may rise because of a higher birth rate (after a long delay), a lower death rate or net immigration.

Participation rate = labour force ÷ working-age population × 100. Those in the labour force are economically active. Those of working age outside it, such as early retirees, students, homemakers and the long-term sick, are economically inactive.

Influence on participationLikely effect
WagesHigher wages may draw more people into work and keep some working past the usual retirement age
Attitudes to women workingGreater acceptance may raise participation
Care for children and the elderlyMore nursery and care places may free carers to work
Attitudes and provision for disabled workersAcceptance and access, such as wheelchair ramps, may widen the labour force
School leavers entering higher educationParticipation may fall, but the quality of the labour force may rise

Vietnam and the Netherlands

The coursebook reports that in 2017 about 48% of Vietnam's workers were in the primary sector, while about 82% of workers in the Netherlands were in services.

Exam use: Contrasting employment structures at different stages of development.

Australia's shift to private employers

The coursebook reports that the public sector's share of Australian workers fell from 21% in 2000 to 15% in 2016.

Exam use: A change in the public and private pattern of employment.

Participation compared

The coursebook gives 2016 participation rates of 71% in New Zealand, 62.6% in the USA (down from 67.3% in 2000) and 52% in Pakistan.

Exam use: Differences may reflect attitudes, care provision and years in education.

Exam-style questions

These are our own practice questions on extension content.

Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Q1 Define 2 marks What is meant by the labour force participation rate?
ReminderTwo ideas: the labour force, and the working-age population.
It is the proportion of the working-age population that is in the labour force, either employed or unemployed.
Common mistakeDividing by the total population.
Q2 Calculate 2 marks A country has a working-age population of 60 million, with 36 million employed and 3 million unemployed. Calculate the participation rate.
ReminderFind the labour force first and show the working.
Labour force = 36 + 3 = 39 million. Participation rate = 39 ÷ 60 × 100 = 65%.
Common mistakeLeaving out the unemployed.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain two reasons why the proportion of women in employment may rise.
ReminderTwo reasons, each with a developed link.
Point 1 · 2 marksSocial attitudes to women working may change. If paid work is more widely accepted, more women are likely to look for jobs and be hired.
Point 2 · 2 marksMore childcare places may become available. Parents who looked after children at home could then take paid work.
Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain how unemployment could fall even though no new jobs are created.
ReminderThink about the ways people leave the labour force.
Unemployment counts only people in the labour force who are without work. If some unemployed people retire or start full-time education, they leave the labour force and are no longer counted. Others may emigrate or give up looking. For example, if 2 million jobseekers enrol on full-time courses, unemployment could fall by 2 million with employment unchanged.
Common mistakeAssuming every fall in unemployment means more jobs.
Q5 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how economic development may change the pattern of employment.
ReminderBuild chains: sectors, job quality, formal work.
Development tends to move workers from the primary sector to the secondary and then the tertiary sector. Machinery may raise productivity in farming, so fewer workers are needed to grow food. Higher incomes are likely to raise demand for manufactured goods and then for services, so firms in these sectors hire more. Better education may help workers move into skilled jobs, raising the share of high quality employment. More firms may also operate in the formal economy, giving more workers legal protection. A farm worker's child might train and work in banking or tourism.
Common mistakeListing changes without explaining why they happen.
Q6 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not a rise in self-employment benefits an economy.
ReminderBoth sides, then a judgement that depends on something.
Self-employed people work for themselves. A rise could benefit the economy if people start successful businesses, adding output and competition and perhaps later employing others. Their rewards depend on their own effort, which may raise productivity. On the other hand, many may work in the informal economy with low pay, little training and no protection. Tax revenue could be lower if earnings go unrecorded, and some may be self-employed only because they cannot find a job. Overall, the benefit is likely to depend on why people become self-employed. A rise in skilled, registered businesses may help growth, whereas a rise in casual survival work may signal a shortage of jobs.
Common mistakeOne-sided answers, or ending without a judgement.

Top tips

  • Know it is extension. Not a set topic for 2027–29, but the examples can support answers on development, unemployment and supply-side policy.
  • Employment is not the mirror image of unemployment. Check what happened to the labour force.
  • Numbers or rates? Say whether data show people or percentages.
  • Divide by the right group. Participation: working-age population. Unemployment rate: labour force.
  • Say why the pattern changed. Attitudes, incomes, technology, competition or government policy.
