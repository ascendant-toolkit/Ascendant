Policies to Achieve Balance of Payments Stability

Expenditure-reducing, expenditure-switching and supply-side policies to reduce a deficit or surplus, and how effective each is. Syllabus 6.4.4.

The big idea

The aim is balance of payments stability: avoiding a large persistent deficit (or surplus). Governments can use three groups of policies: expenditure-reducing (cutting demand to cut imports), expenditure-switching (making imports dearer or exports cheaper so spending switches to home goods) and supply-side (raising competitiveness). Each has costs: some cut growth and jobs, some invite retaliation, and some take years. Match the policy to the cause and weigh its effectiveness.

Policies to reduce a surplus

  • Expansionary demand policy (cut taxes, raise spending, cut interest rates) to raise spending on imports.
  • A higher exchange rate (revaluation) to raise the price of exports and cut the price of imports.
  • Reducing trade restrictions and encouraging imports and overseas investment.

How effective are they?

  • Elasticity: a lower exchange rate or tariffs only cut a deficit if demand for imports and exports is elastic enough.
  • Time lags: supply-side policy takes years; fiscal policy takes months.
  • Conflicts of aims: cutting demand lowers growth and raises unemployment; a lower exchange rate raises inflation.
  • Retaliation: trade restrictions may lead others to retaliate, cutting exports.
  • Cause matters: a deficit caused by a fast-growing economy importing capital goods may need no policy; one caused by weak competitiveness needs supply-side policy.
  • A mix of policies is often best.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Austerity to close deficits

After 2010 countries such as Greece and Spain cut spending and raised taxes. Imports fell and deficits narrowed, but output fell and unemployment rose sharply.

Exam use: Expenditure-reducing policy and its cost.

Devaluations and depreciations

After the pound fell in 2016 and in earlier episodes, exports rose slowly while import prices rose quickly, showing that effects take time.

Exam use: Expenditure-switching policy.

Germany's supply-side strategy

Germany invested in skills, apprenticeships and high-quality engineering, helping it build a persistent surplus.

Exam use: Supply-side policy raising competitiveness.

Trade wars

When the US raised tariffs in 2018 to cut its deficit, other countries retaliated and the overall deficit did not fall.

Exam use: Why tariffs may fail.

Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain two policy measures a government could use to reduce a current account deficit.
ReminderDifferent types, each with a chain: cut demand, lower exchange rate, tariffs, supply-side.
A government could raise taxes or cut its spending (contractionary fiscal policy). Households have less income, so spending on imports falls and the deficit narrows. It could allow the exchange rate to fall. Exports become cheaper abroad and imports dearer, so export demand could rise and import demand fall.
Q2 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a country's current account deficit might be reduced if its firms become more competitive. (Oct/Nov 2020, P21)
ReminderSee 6.4.2. Competitiveness: price, quality, productivity; exports rise, imports fall.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
If firms become more competitive their goods are cheaper or better. Foreign demand for exports rises, increasing export revenue. Domestic buyers switch from imports to home-produced goods, so import spending falls. The goods deficit narrows, and so does the current account deficit. It takes time to build competitiveness.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a government could use supply-side policy to improve the current account.
ReminderEducation, investment, technology, infrastructure; productivity, costs, quality; exports up, imports down.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Supply-side policy aims to raise productivity and lower costs. Spending on education and training makes workers more skilled, and investment in infrastructure and technology cuts firms' costs. Goods become cheaper and better, so export demand and revenue rise, and domestic firms replace some imports. The current account improves without cutting growth. But it takes years and is costly.
Q4 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not a fall in a country's foreign exchange rate will improve its current account position.
ReminderFor: exports cheaper, imports dearer. Against: elasticity, imported inflation, retaliation, time lag, higher import costs for inputs.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A fall in the exchange rate makes exports cheaper in foreign currency and imports dearer. Export demand could rise and import demand fall, increasing export revenue and reducing import spending, so the current account could improve. This is more likely if demand is elastic and if the country has spare capacity. However, if demand is inelastic, the quantity of imports hardly falls but each import costs more, so the deficit could widen at first. Dearer imported raw materials raise costs and cause inflation, which reduces the competitive advantage. Overall, a fall is likely to help in the longer run if demand is elastic, but not for sure: it depends on elasticity, import dependence and time.
Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not an increase in foreign MNCs will reduce a deficit on the current account. (Oct/Nov 2021, P23)
ReminderFor: exports, import substitution, technology, jobs. Against: profits sent home (primary income), imported inputs, may import more.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
MNCs are firms that operate in several countries. They could reduce a deficit by producing goods in the country for export, increasing export revenue, and by producing goods that replace imports. They may bring technology and investment that raise productivity and competitiveness. This is more likely if they export most of their output. However, MNCs send profits home, which is a primary income outflow, and may import raw materials and machinery, increasing imports. They may produce mainly for the local market. Overall, MNCs may reduce the goods deficit but their profit outflows could worsen the current account, so the net effect is uncertain.

Top tips

  • Three groups of policies: expenditure-reducing, expenditure-switching, supply-side.
  • Match the policy to the cause. Competitiveness problem: supply-side. Overspending: demand-side.
  • Always evaluate. Growth and jobs, inflation, retaliation, elasticity, time.
  • For a surplus, do the opposite. Expansionary policy, higher exchange rate, free trade.
  • A mix of policies is often best. Mention both short-run and long-run measures.
