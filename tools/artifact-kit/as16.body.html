The PPC: Shape and Opportunity Cost

1.5.1 to 1.5.2: what a production possibility curve shows, how to read opportunity cost from it, and why it is straight (constant cost) or concave (increasing cost). Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

A production possibility curve (PPC) shows the maximum combinations of two goods that an economy (or firm) can produce with its given resources and technology, when all resources are fully and efficiently used. Moving along the curve means producing more of one good and less of the other, and the slope shows the opportunity cost. A straight-line PPC has constant opportunity cost because resources are equally suited to both goods, whereas the usual concave (bowed-out) PPC has increasing opportunity cost because resources are specialised and the ones moved later are less suited to the new use.

1 · What a PPC shows (1.5.1)

  • Draw it with two named goods on the axes (for example consumer goods and capital goods, or wheat and cotton), and the curve should touch both axes. Axes labelled only "X and Y" lose marks in some mark schemes.
  • Every point on the curve is the maximum output of the two goods together, when resources are fully and efficiently used. A point outside is unattainable with current resources, and a point inside means resources are unused or used inefficiently (see syllabus 1.5.4).
  • It illustrates scarcity (the frontier is a limit), choice (picking a point) and opportunity cost (what is given up when moving along the curve).
  • The opportunity cost of moving from one point to another is the units of one good given up per unit of the other gained (the slope of the curve).

2 · The shape of the PPC (1.5.2)

Straight-line PPCConcave (bowed-out) PPC
Opportunity costConstant: each extra unit of X always costs the same number of units of YIncreasing: each extra unit of X costs more and more of Y
WhyResources are equally suited to making either good and can be switched without lossResources are specialised: the first ones moved are best suited to the new good, later ones are less suited, so output of the other good falls faster
RealismRare, a simplificationThe usual case, because land, labour and capital differ in what they are good at
SlopeThe same everywhereGets steeper as more X is produced
  • Increasing opportunity cost arises because resources are not perfectly mobile or equally productive in all uses. Farmland that is best for wheat is poor for cotton: switching the best wheat land first loses little wheat, but switching the last plots loses a lot.
  • A convex PPC (bowed in) would mean decreasing opportunity cost, which would be unusual and is not expected at AS Level.
  • In a mixed economy the government might choose a different mix of goods (such as more consumer goods and fewer capital goods) by moving along the same curve.
Exam tipFor "why is a PPC usually concave?", say that resources cannot easily be switched between products, so the cost in terms of the other good rises as more is produced.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A farm choosing crops

A farmer has land that suits both wheat and potatoes. At first she plants potatoes on the plots that are best for them, and she loses little wheat. As she puts potatoes on plots better suited to wheat, each extra tonne of potatoes costs more wheat. This is increasing opportunity cost.

Exam use: why a PPC is usually concave: resources are better suited to some uses than others.

A country choosing between consumer and capital goods

A country can make fewer consumer goods now and more factory machinery, or the reverse. If it moves resources into machinery, it gives up shops and clothes. The PPC shows the trade-off, and the choice affects future growth.

Exam use: choice along the PPC and the consumer goods and capital goods axes.

Straight-line trade-offs

A firm that can switch identical workers and machines between two similar products, such as two kinds of box, loses a fixed number of boxes of one type for each extra box of the other. This is close to a straight-line PPC with constant opportunity cost.

Exam use: constant opportunity cost when resources are equally suited to both goods.

A desert island economy

People on an island can catch fish or gather coconuts. If all the islanders are equally good at both, the trade-off is constant, so the PPC is a straight line. If some are good climbers and others good fishers, the PPC is bowed out.

Exam use: how the nature of the resources determines the shape of the PPC.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/22 · Q2(a) 8 marks Use a diagram to explain how a production possibility curve can be used to show opportunity cost and why such a curve is usually drawn with increasing opportunity costs.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): a PPC diagram with correctly labelled axes.
  • Application (up to 3): what opportunity cost is, in terms of the products on the diagram (1); a movement along the curve and its effect on the output of the two goods (1); how this shows opportunity cost (1).
  • Application (up to 3): why increasing opportunity cost is expected, so the curve must be concave (1); increasing opportunity cost clearly shown on the diagram (1); explained, for example because resources cannot easily be switched between products (1).
  • If the only diagram shows a straight line, the maximum is 5 marks.
PlanConcave PPC with named goods. A movement along it shows the loss of one good. Why the cost rises: resources are not equally suited.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A production possibility curve shows the maximum combinations of two goods that can be produced with the available resources. If the axes are labelled consumer goods and capital goods, the curve slopes downwards, touching both axes. A movement along the curve from point A to point B means more capital goods and fewer consumer goods, and the consumer goods given up are the opportunity cost.

The curve is usually drawn concave to show increasing opportunity cost. This is because resources are not equally suited to making both goods, so they cannot easily be switched. The first resources moved to capital goods are best suited to them and little consumer output is lost, but later resources are less suited, so each extra unit of capital goods costs more consumer goods. The curve gets steeper as more capital goods are produced.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/22 · Q3(a) 8 marks With the help of a PPC diagram, explain the difference between constant and increasing opportunity costs and consider how choices in deciding which type of goods to produce in the short run may influence future economic growth.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (3): an accurate PPC with named goods on the axes: curved to show increasing opportunity cost and a straight line to show constant (the curves must touch both axes); an explanation of the difference: constant means no change in the rate of trade-off, increasing means the trade-off rises.
  • Analysis (3): future growth depends on the split between consumer goods and capital goods; choosing consumer goods may bring short-run growth that may not be sustainable; choosing capital goods may have a short-run negative impact but create long-term sustainable growth as output expands.
  • Evaluation (2): valid evaluation based on the analysis, leading to a conclusion.
PlanTwo PPCs or two lines. Straight: constant. Curved: increasing. Consumer or capital goods now: effect on growth later. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A straight-line PPC shows constant opportunity cost: each extra unit of good X always means giving up the same number of units of Y. A concave PPC shows increasing opportunity cost: each extra unit of X costs more and more of Y, because resources are not equally suited to the two goods.

The choice of goods now influences future growth. If the economy chooses more consumer goods, living standards rise in the short run, but with fewer capital goods the productive capacity may grow slowly. If it chooses more capital goods, consumption is lower now, but the stock of capital expands, which shifts the PPC outwards in the future.

So a choice for capital goods is likely to give more sustainable long-run growth, but at a cost in the short run. The best choice may depend on how urgent current consumption is and how well the capital is used.

Top tips

  • Name the goods on the axes. Consumer goods and capital goods, not just X and Y.
  • Touch both axes. The curve must meet each axis.
  • Slope equals opportunity cost. Say how many units of one good are given up for the other.
  • Straight means constant. Resources equally suited to both goods.
  • Concave means increasing. Resources are specialised and not easily switched.
  • Use a movement along the curve. A movement along shows choice and opportunity cost; a shift is covered in the next topic.
  • Link to scarcity. A point outside the curve is unattainable.
