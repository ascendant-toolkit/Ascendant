Specialisation by Country

What specialisation is, why countries specialise, its advantages and disadvantages, and why specialisation changes. Syllabus 6.1.1.

The big idea

Specialisation by country is when a country concentrates its resources on producing a limited range of goods and services, usually the ones it can make best or at lowest cost, and trades them for the other things it needs. The basis for specialisation is best resource allocation (using land, labour, capital and skills where they are most productive) and low-cost production. Specialisation can raise output and income, but it makes a country dependent on a few products and on trade.

Low-cost production and opportunity cost

A country should specialise in the goods whose opportunity cost (what it gives up to make them) is lowest. Suppose country A can produce either 100 tonnes of wheat or 50 cars, and country B either 60 tonnes of wheat or 60 cars. A gives up 1 car for 2 tonnes of wheat; B gives up 1 car for 1 tonne. A is relatively better at wheat and B at cars. If each splits resources evenly they make 80 tonnes of wheat and 55 cars in total. If A makes only wheat and B only cars, they make 100 tonnes of wheat and 60 cars: more of both, which they can then trade.

Advantages of specialisation

  • Lower costs and prices: workers gain skills (practice makes perfect), better use of machinery, economies of scale.
  • Higher output and quality, and less waste.
  • Efficient use of resources, so more total output from the same inputs.
  • Exports earn income and create jobs; firms gain a good reputation and higher demand; easier to mechanise and improve technology.
  • Consumers can buy a wider choice of goods through trade, often cheaper.

Disadvantages of specialisation

  • Over-dependence on a few products: if demand or the price falls (or a harvest fails), incomes, jobs and export earnings fall sharply.
  • Vulnerable to other countries and to world prices and to shocks such as wars, disasters and trade restrictions.
  • Dependence on imports of everything not produced: supplies can be cut off.
  • Structural unemployment if the main industry declines.
  • Resources used up or environmental damage from one industry; loss of traditional skills.

The risk is lower if a country specialises in several goods or in goods with strong, stable demand, and can switch if conditions change.

Why a country may change its specialisation

  • Resources run out or new ones are found (oil, minerals).
  • Technology and education change what the country can make well (a farming economy moves into manufacturing or services).
  • Costs change: rising wages make labour-intensive goods less competitive.
  • Demand and world prices change, or competitors with lower costs appear.
  • Government policy, investment in infrastructure, or changes in exchange rates and trade agreements.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Saudi Arabia and oil

Saudi Arabia specialises in oil, which makes up most of its exports. It earns huge revenue when prices are high but suffered when prices fell sharply in 2014 and 2020, which is why it is trying to diversify.

Exam use: Resource-based specialisation, with price risk.

Bangladesh and clothing

Bangladesh specialises in garments, using abundant low-cost labour. Clothing is the large majority of its exports, creating jobs, but leaving it exposed to changes in global demand.

Exam use: Low-cost labour as the basis, and over-dependence.

Switzerland's watches and finance

Switzerland has few natural resources but specialises in high-value watches, pharmaceuticals and banking, using skilled labour and a strong reputation.

Exam use: Specialisation based on skills.

Venezuela and oil dependence

Oil makes up the great majority of Venezuela's exports. When oil prices fell after 2014 its export earnings and economy shrank sharply.

Exam use: A clear example of the risk of over-dependence.

Changing specialisation in Mauritius

Mauritius moved from sugar to textiles, then tourism and financial services as costs and opportunities changed.

Exam use: Why countries change specialisation.

Q5 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how specialisation can benefit firms. (May/June 2022, P21)
ReminderSkills, lower costs, reputation and demand, economies of scale, technology.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Specialisation means a firm focuses on one product, and its workers become skilled at making it. Productivity rises and the cost of production falls, so the firm can offer lower prices. It may gain a reputation for high quality, which could raise demand, output, revenue and profit. It may also benefit from economies of scale, for example buying inputs in bulk, becoming more internationally competitive. It is easier to use machines and new technology when making one product, speeding up production.
Q6 Discuss 6 marks Discuss whether or not specialisation at a national level is harmful to an economy. (May/June 2022, P23)
ReminderHarm: over-dependence, price shocks, unemployment, imports. Not harm: lower costs, higher output, exports, growth. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
National specialisation is when a country concentrates on producing a few goods or services. It could be harmful because the country becomes dependent on those industries, so a fall in world prices or demand, a poor harvest or new competitors could cut export earnings and cause unemployment. It may also depend on imports for other goods, which could be cut off. The risk is greatest where the specialisation is in one primary product. However, specialisation can raise productivity, lower costs and increase output, and exports bring income, jobs and growth. Overall, specialisation is likely to benefit an economy that can diversify across several products or switch industries, but could be harmful to one dependent on a single product with unstable prices.
Q7 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not countries specialising benefits consumers. (May/June 2021, P21)
ReminderFor: lower prices, more choice via trade, higher quality. Against: fewer domestic goods, dependence on imports, price rises if supply cut, job losses reduce incomes.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Specialisation is when countries produce a limited range of goods and trade for the rest. It could benefit consumers because lower costs and economies of scale mean lower prices, and trade allows consumers to buy a wider choice of goods and services, often of higher quality. Increased competition between producers can also keep prices down. This is more likely if trade is free and there are many suppliers. However, consumers may become dependent on imports, and if supplies are disrupted or a producer gains market power, prices could rise. If specialisation causes job losses in the industries a country gives up, consumers affected may have lower incomes. Overall, specialisation is likely to benefit consumers through lower prices and choice, but the benefits depend on free trade, competition and how well displaced workers find new jobs.

Top tips

  • Define it precisely. A limited range of goods and services, produced best or at lowest cost, and traded.
  • Basis: resources and low cost. Climate, minerals, labour skills, capital, technology, economies of scale.
  • Link each advantage. 'Skills rise, so productivity rises and costs fall.'
  • Disadvantages: dependence. Price shocks, unemployment, imports, sustainability.
  • Judge: depends on how many goods and how stable demand is. Diversification reduces the risk.
