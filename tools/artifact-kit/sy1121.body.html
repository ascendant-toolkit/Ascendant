Measuring Exchange Rates: Nominal, Real and Trade-Weighted

11.2.1: the nominal exchange rate, the real exchange rate that adjusts for relative prices, and the trade-weighted index of a currency against a basket. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

The nominal exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another (for example $1.25 per £1). The real exchange rate adjusts the nominal rate for differences in price levels, so it shows whether a country's goods have become dearer or cheaper relative to its trading partners', which is what matters for competitiveness. A trade-weighted exchange rate (effective exchange rate) is an index of the currency against a basket of partner currencies, each weighted by the share of trade with that partner, so it shows the overall movement rather than the rate against one currency. The real effective exchange rate combines both ideas.

1 · The nominal exchange rate

  • The nominal exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another, quoted as, say, £1 = $1.25. It is set by supply and demand in a floating system, or fixed by the central bank in a fixed system.
  • If £1 buys more dollars (for example $1.25 rising to $1.40) the pound has appreciated (under a floating rate) or been revalued (under a fixed rate). If it buys fewer, it has depreciated or been devalued. The difference between these terms is covered in 11.2.3.
  • The weakness of the nominal rate on its own is that it ignores inflation. If a currency's nominal rate is constant but domestic prices rise faster than abroad, exports are becoming less competitive even though the exchange rate looks unchanged.

2 · The real exchange rate

  • The real exchange rate adjusts the nominal rate for relative price levels: real rate index = nominal rate index × (domestic price index ÷ foreign price index), with the nominal rate measured as foreign currency per unit of domestic currency, so a rise means appreciation.
  • A rise in the real exchange rate (real appreciation) means domestic goods have become relatively dearer: exports are less competitive and imports are cheaper in real terms. A fall (real depreciation) makes domestic goods relatively cheaper.
  • Worked idea: the nominal rate is unchanged, domestic inflation is 6% and foreign inflation is 2%. The real exchange rate rises by about 4% (6% − 2%), so the country has lost competitiveness even though the nominal rate has not moved.
  • A nominal depreciation can be cancelled out if domestic inflation rises by more than foreign inflation, so the real rate (and competitiveness) hardly changes. This is why devaluations in high-inflation countries often fail to last.
  • The real rate links to purchasing power parity (PPP): if the real rate stays constant, the nominal rate is moving in line with relative inflation. A currency is said to be overvalued if it is above its PPP value and undervalued if below.

3 · The trade-weighted exchange rate

  • A country trades with many partners and its currency can rise against some and fall against others. The trade-weighted exchange rate index (TWI, or effective exchange rate) measures the currency against a basket of other currencies.
  • Each partner's currency is weighted by that partner's share in the country's trade (exports plus imports). A currency movement against a major partner counts for more than a movement against a minor partner.
  • Calculation: TWI = (weight A × index against A) + (weight B × index against B) + ... The weights add to 1 (or 100%) and the index starts at 100 in a base year.
  • The real effective exchange rate (REER) is the trade-weighted index adjusted for each partner's relative price level. Many central banks and the BIS publish it as the best single guide to price competitiveness.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Switzerland and the strong franc

The Swiss franc has tended to appreciate during global uncertainty, because investors treat it as a safe haven. Even with low Swiss inflation, the real value of the franc has risen over time, which squeezes exporters and tourism and has led the central bank to intervene in currency markets at times.

Exam use: a real appreciation and its effect on competitiveness. Link to exports, employment and imported inflation.

High inflation, falling currency

In countries with very high inflation, such as Argentina or Turkey at times, the nominal currency can fall sharply while the real exchange rate changes much less, because domestic prices rise quickly too. Exporters may gain less than the headline fall in the currency suggests.

Exam use: why the real rate is a better guide to competitiveness than the nominal rate, and why a depreciation may be eroded by inflation.

Sterling's fall after 2016

After the 2016 Brexit vote the pound fell against the US dollar and the euro, and the trade-weighted index fell too. The fall made UK exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer, but the effect on the trade balance was slower than many expected, and imported inflation rose.

Exam use: a trade-weighted index, the J curve (see 11.2.5) and imported inflation.

The Big Mac index

The Economist compares the price of a burger in dollars across countries. If a burger costs less in dollars in a country, its currency may be undervalued against the dollar on a simple purchasing power basis, though differences in wages, taxes and rents limit the accuracy.

Exam use: purchasing power parity as a benchmark for over- and under-valued currencies, with its limitations.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/42 · Q6(a) 12 marks Explain how changes in the internal value of a country's currency can affect the external value of that currency.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Define the internal value (purchasing power at home, the inverse of the price level, so linked to inflation) and the external value (the exchange rate against other currencies).
  • Explain the link: if inflation is higher than in partner countries, exports become dearer and imports cheaper, so demand for the currency falls and supply rises, lowering the exchange rate (the idea behind purchasing power parity).
  • Explain the reverse link: a fall in the exchange rate raises import prices and can cause inflation, lowering the internal value.
  • Comment on the exchange rate system: under a floating rate the market adjusts the rate; under a fixed rate the central bank must defend it, so the link works through reserves, interest rates or a devaluation.
PlanDefine internal and external value. Higher inflation leads to a weaker currency. A weaker currency leads to higher inflation. Fixed against floating.

The internal value of a currency is what it can buy at home, so it falls when the price level rises. The external value is the exchange rate, or what it can buy of other currencies. If domestic inflation is higher than in partner countries, home goods may become dearer relative to foreign goods. Exports could fall and imports could rise, so demand for the currency may fall and supply may rise, putting downward pressure on the exchange rate. This is the logic of purchasing power parity, although the link may be weak in the short run because capital flows and interest rates can matter more.

The link may also run the other way. A depreciation raises import prices, and if imports are a large share of spending or are used as inputs, it could raise inflation and lower the internal value. Under a floating rate the market adjusts, while under a fixed rate the central bank would need to use reserves or interest rates to defend the rate, or devalue.

An over-valued currency makes imports cheaper and exports dearer in foreign currency. Spending may switch towards imports and away from exports, so the current account could worsen and, because exports are part of aggregate demand, growth and employment may fall in export industries.

Practice question · modelled on the 12 mark style (no past paper) 12 marks Explain the difference between a nominal and a real exchange rate and why a trade-weighted index is useful.
What the mark scheme wants
  • The nominal rate is the price of one currency in another; the real rate adjusts for relative price levels.
  • Why the real rate matters: it shows competitiveness, so a nominal depreciation can be offset by higher domestic inflation.
  • Trade-weighted index: a basket of partner currencies weighted by trade shares, giving the overall movement.
  • Evaluation: weights change, price indices differ, and non-price competitiveness matters.

Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The nominal exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another, such as $1.25 per pound. The real exchange rate adjusts this for the price levels in the two countries. If the nominal rate is unchanged but domestic inflation is higher than abroad, the real rate rises. Home goods become relatively dearer, so competitiveness could fall even though the nominal rate looks stable. In the same way a nominal depreciation may be offset if domestic prices rise faster.

A trade-weighted index measures the currency against a basket of partner currencies, with each weighted by its share of trade. This is useful because a currency can rise against one partner and fall against another. A movement against a major trading partner will count for more, so the index gives a better picture of the overall effect on exports and imports.

However, the weights change over time, price indices may not be comparable and quality and other non-price factors also affect competitiveness, so the index may only be a guide.

Top tips

  • Nominal first, then real. Give the nominal definition, then say the real rate adjusts for relative inflation. That is the extra mark.
  • Always say which way it moves. Appreciation is a rise in the currency's value. A real appreciation makes the country's goods relatively dearer.
  • Use a simple number. Nominal unchanged, home inflation 6%, foreign 2%: real rate up about 4%.
  • Explain the weights. In a trade-weighted index each currency is weighted by its share of trade, so major partners count for more.
  • Link to competitiveness. Real appreciation hurts export competitiveness, real depreciation helps it, subject to elasticities.
  • Evaluate with limits. Price indices, changing weights and non-price competitiveness.
  • Appreciation or revaluation? Floating rate: appreciation or depreciation. Fixed rate: revaluation or devaluation.
