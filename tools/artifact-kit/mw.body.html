The Minimum Wage

Why governments set a legal floor on pay, what it does in a competitive labour market and in a monopsony, who gains and loses, and how to evaluate it. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions, with mark scheme points.

This part in four sentences

A minimum wage is a legal floor on the pay an employer may offer. In a competitive labour market a binding minimum (set above the equilibrium wage) raises the quantity of labour supplied and cuts the quantity demanded, so it creates unemployment. In a monopsony a minimum between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage can raise both the wage and employment. The overall effect depends on the level of the minimum, the elasticity of demand for labour, the market structure, and who gains and loses.

This is part 5 of syllabus 8.3. Part 1 covers the demand for labour, part 2 supply and wage differentials, part 3 economic rent, and part 4 monopsony and trade unions.

1 · Why have a minimum wage?

  • To protect basic living standards and reduce poverty for low-paid workers.
  • To make the distribution of income more equitable (syllabus 8.2).
  • To encourage people to take jobs rather than rely on benefits, by making work pay.
  • To raise productivity and morale: better-paid workers may work harder and stay longer.
  • To limit the power of employers who are monopsonists.

2 · A competitive labour market

The wage is where demand (MRP) meets supply. The minimum wage only matters if it is set above that level (a binding minimum).

  • The higher wage means each worker costs more than they add to revenue at the old level of employment, so the quantity of labour demanded falls.
  • The higher wage attracts more people into the labour market, so the quantity supplied rises.
  • The gap between the two is unemployment. Those who keep their jobs gain; those who lose them, or cannot find work, lose.

3 · A monopsony

A monopsony pays less than the competitive wage and employs fewer workers (part 4). A minimum wage between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage raises the wage and employment: the firm cannot pay less than the minimum, so extra workers cost only the minimum wage until supply runs out, and it hires up to where MRP meets that wage. Above the competitive wage, employment falls as in the competitive case.

Always state the rangeThe monopsony result only holds for a minimum between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage. Say where on the diagram you have drawn it.

4 · Evaluating a minimum wage

Arguments for

  • Higher pay for low-paid workers who keep their jobs
  • May reduce poverty and inequality
  • May raise productivity, lower turnover and cut benefit spending
  • Higher incomes raise consumer spending
  • Corrects monopsony power

Arguments against

  • Job losses and unemployment if set above equilibrium in a competitive market
  • Higher costs: firms may raise prices, cut hours, replace workers with machines, or close
  • May not target the poor: many low-paid workers are in households that are not poor, while the poorest may have no job
  • Could cost the government if more people become unemployed
  • May push some work into the informal economy
  • The size of the minimum: a low minimum may not bind; a very high one is more likely to cost jobs.
  • Elasticity of demand for labour: the less elastic (for example if labour is a small share of costs, or machines are not a good substitute), the smaller the job losses.
  • Market structure: real labour markets are rarely perfectly competitive, so job losses may be smaller than the simple model suggests.
  • Substitutability of capital: the easier it is to replace labour with machines, the greater the job loss.
  • Time period: effects may differ in the short and long run, because firms can change methods of production over time.
Change in the wage bill ≈ (1 + % wage rise) × (1 + % change in employment) − 1

If demand for labour is inelastic the total pay of low-paid workers rises even though some jobs are lost; if it is elastic, total pay falls.

5 · Other ways to help low-paid workers

  • Tax credits or in-work benefits top up low incomes without raising employers' costs, but cost the government.
  • Training and education subsidies raise skills and MRP over time (supply-side policy).
  • Strengthening unions or anti-discrimination laws (part 4 and syllabus 8.2).

The UK National Minimum Wage and the Low Pay Commission

The UK introduced a national minimum wage in 1999 and a higher rate for workers aged 25 and over, the National Living Wage, in 2016. An independent body, the Low Pay Commission, advises on the rates. Many studies found small effects on total employment when the wage was introduced, though the effects of larger rises are debated.

Exam use: evidence for evaluation. The job losses predicted by the simple competitive model were small, which suggests many low-wage markets have some monopsony power or that demand for labour is inelastic.

Fast food in New Jersey and Pennsylvania

In the 1990s the economists David Card and Alan Krueger compared fast-food employment in New Jersey, which raised its minimum wage, with neighbouring Pennsylvania, which did not. They did not find the fall in employment the competitive model would predict.

Exam use: a well-known study that supports the monopsony view. Evaluate by noting that other researchers disputed the methods and results.

A very high minimum wage in a small business

A small café with thin profits may respond to a large rise by cutting hours, raising prices, using self-service machines or closing. The effect depends on how easily it can pass costs on and on whether machines can replace staff.

Exam use: evaluation: the substitutability of capital for labour and the effect on small businesses, as in the Feb/March 2025 20-mark essay.

Linking the minimum wage to inflation

Some governments raise the minimum wage each year in line with inflation, so that its real value does not fall. That keeps the protection for low-paid workers but raises labour costs every year.

Exam use: the Oct/Nov 2021 data response asked whether a minimum wage always gives a different employment outcome from a free market: it does not if it is not binding or if the market is a monopsony.

A country introducing its first minimum wage

When a country introduces a national minimum wage, the effect can differ across regions and sectors. In low-wage sectors where many workers earn below the minimum, the wage bill rises most, while higher-paid sectors are unaffected.

Exam use: the Oct/Nov 2020 data response on Uruguay asked for a diagram and the likely effects on labour markets: a shortage of demand against supply, but a possible gain in productivity.

Nurses, care workers and the state as employer

Where the government is the main employer, it sets pay and can act as a monopsony. A higher pay floor for care workers could increase the number of people willing to work in the sector without causing unemployment.

Exam use: a monopsony case where a minimum wage could raise employment. Link to "state the range".

Feb/March 2025 · 9708/42 · Q3 20 marks With the help of a diagram, evaluate the consequences of imposing an effective minimum wage on the employment level and the wage level in a monopsony labour market.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (14): a monopsony diagram with MRP, ACL (supply) and MCL; the wage and employment before the minimum wage; the effect of an effective minimum wage on both.
  • Evaluation (6): the effect depends on the relative elasticities of demand and supply in a monopsony; the size of the rise decides the impact; a higher wage might raise productivity (MRP shifts right) and so raise wages further; a minimum wage may close small, struggling firms and cut employment; higher costs may make firms less competitive globally; the effect on employment depends on how easily capital can substitute for labour; a conclusion on the likely net effect.
Diagram to drawMRP, ACL and MCL. Mark the monopsony employment where MCL = MRP and the wage on ACL. Draw the minimum wage line between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage: employment rises to where MRP (or supply) meets it. Add a second line above the competitive wage to show the fall.
KANE

A monopsony is a market with a single buyer of labour, so the employer faces the whole upward sloping supply curve of labour (the average cost of labour) and must raise the wage to all workers to hire one more. A minimum wage is a legal floor on pay, and it is effective if it is set above the wage the market would otherwise pay.

Without a minimum wage the monopsonist hires where marginal cost of labour equals marginal revenue product, at employment Q1, and pays the wage W1 read from the supply curve, which is below MRP. An effective minimum wage W2 above W1 means the firm cannot pay less than W2 to any worker, so the marginal cost of an extra worker is the minimum wage until supply runs out. The firm now hires up to the point where MRP equals W2, so employment rises to Q2 and the wage rises from W1 to W2, provided W2 is below the competitive wage.

If the minimum wage is set above the competitive wage, the result changes. MRP is below the minimum wage for the marginal workers, so employment falls below the competitive level and, if it is also below Q1, below the monopsony level. The quantity supplied is greater than the quantity demanded, so there is unemployment. The impact therefore depends on the size of the rise and on the elasticities of demand for and supply of labour in the market.

Other effects may change the outcome. A higher wage might increase productivity through better motivation and lower turnover, shifting MRP to the right and supporting even higher wages. However, higher costs could force small firms with low profit to close, or make firms less competitive against foreign producers, and employment may fall if firms can easily replace workers with machines. Overall, an effective minimum wage in a monopsony is likely to raise both the wage and employment if it is set between the monopsony and competitive wages, but the benefit is less certain if it is set too high or if firms can substitute capital for labour. The government needs good information on the monopsony wage, which is hard to estimate.

May/June 2022 · 9708/41 and 43 · Q4(b) 13 marks The introduction of a national minimum wage by a government will always benefit employees at the expense of employers. Discuss the extent to which you agree with this statement.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Why a government might introduce a minimum wage: to protect basic living standards, make the distribution of income more equitable, encourage people to take work, and raise productivity.
  • The impact of a minimum wage imposed above the existing market equilibrium, and why the effect of a wage rise differs by type of market and by the elasticities of supply of and demand for labour.
  • A conclusion on "always".
KANE

A national minimum wage is a legal floor on hourly pay. Governments introduce it to protect the living standards of low-paid workers, to make the distribution of income fairer, and to encourage people to take jobs rather than rely on benefits.

A minimum wage is likely to benefit employees who keep their jobs, at the expense of employers whose costs rise. If the minimum wage is set above the equilibrium wage in a competitive labour market, the higher wage raises the pay of workers who stay in work. Employers pay a higher wage bill for the same workers, so their profits are likely to fall unless they raise prices. However, not all employees benefit, because the quantity of labour demanded falls and some workers lose their jobs or hours. How many depends on the elasticity of demand for labour: if demand is elastic, the job losses are large and total pay for low-paid workers could fall.

The statement may not hold in a monopsony. An employer with monopsony power pays workers less than their MRP and restricts employment. A minimum wage between the monopsony and competitive wages raises the wage and employment, and employers may lose some of their excess profit but can still employ more workers. Employers may also gain if higher pay raises productivity or reduces staff turnover, or if higher incomes raise spending in the shop.

Overall, the statement is too strong. A minimum wage is likely to benefit those who keep their jobs but may harm others, and employers do not always lose, so the result depends on the type of market, the level of the minimum and the elasticities of demand and supply.

Top tips

  • A minimum wage must be above the equilibrium wage to bite in a competitive market. Draw it above, and show the surplus of workers.
  • Label the quantities: quantity demanded, quantity supplied, and the gap as unemployment.
  • Monopsony is the exception. A minimum between the monopsony and competitive wages can raise employment. State the range.
  • Say who gains and who loses. Workers who keep jobs gain, those who lose jobs or cannot find them lose, employers' costs rise, consumers may pay more.
  • Use elasticity. The less elastic the demand for labour, the smaller the job loss and the more likely total pay rises.
  • Evaluate with: the size of the minimum, market structure, substitutability of capital, productivity effects, and the time period.
  • Use evidence: small employment effects after the UK minimum wage were introduced support the monopsony or inelastic-demand view.
  • Check strong words: "always", "will", "only". The minimum wage does not always benefit employees at the expense of employers.
