Monetary Indicators of Living Standards: GDP, GNI, NNI and PPP

11.3.3 to 11.3.4: real income per head, GDP, GNI and NNI, purchasing power parity, the problems of comparing living standards and comparing growth rates. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

The standard of living is the material and non-material well-being of people, and the most common monetary indicator is real income per head, measured as GDP (output within the country), GNI (GDP plus net income from abroad) or NNI (GNI less depreciation), each divided by the population and adjusted for price changes. Because prices differ between countries, purchasing power parity (PPP) converts incomes using the cost of the same basket of goods rather than the market exchange rate. These indicators are easy to calculate but give an imperfect comparison of living standards over time and between countries because of the informal economy, income distribution, quality, externalities, leisure and data errors. Non-monetary indicators such as life expectancy and literacy fill some of the gaps.

  • Real GNI (or GDP) per head is the usual starting point for the standard of living. Use real figures (to remove inflation) and per capita figures (to remove the effect of population size).
  • GDP versus GNI: a country with large foreign investments or with many foreign firms taking profits home will have a GNI different from its GDP. NNI is a better guide to income that can be consumed without running down the capital stock.

2 · Purchasing power parity

  • PPP compares the cost of the same bundle of goods and services in two countries. If the bundle costs 500 rupees at home and $2 in the US, the PPP rate is 250 rupees = $1.
  • Converting incomes at market exchange rates understates living standards in low-price countries, since a dollar of income buys much more locally. PPP-adjusted GDP per capita shows what incomes can actually buy and gives a better comparison.
  • Limits: baskets differ across countries, quality differs, and non-traded goods (haircuts, rents) make comparisons rough. PPP estimates are revised when the surveys are updated.

3 · Problems of comparing living standards using monetary indicators

ProblemWhy it matters
Informal and subsistence economyUnrecorded and unpaid output (home farming, unpaid housework) is left out, so poorer countries are understated
Income distributionAn average hides inequality; a rise may go to a few
Exchange rates and pricesMarket exchange rates are volatile and ignore price level differences (use PPP)
Quality and the composition of outputMore output may be military, capital or poor quality; total value does not show what is produced
Externalities and sustainabilityPollution and resource depletion are not deducted; clean-up costs even add to GDP
Leisure, health, freedom, culture and climateNon-material well-being is left out
Accuracy and cost of dataLow-income countries often have weaker statistics and larger errors
Population and price index choicePopulation changes alter per capita figures; different baskets give different inflation

Non-monetary indicators

Life expectancy, infant mortality, literacy and years of schooling, doctors per 1,000 people, access to clean water and electricity, and the share of people in poverty. These measure outcomes more directly but are harder to combine; composite measures such as the HDI (next page) try to do so.

4 · Comparing growth rates and living standards (11.3.4)

  • Over time: use real GDP per capita in constant prices. Growth rate = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Compare average annual growth over several years and use the rule of 70 (years to double = 70 ÷ growth rate).
  • Between countries: compare real GDP per capita at PPP. Compare growth rates too, but beware: a poor country growing at 6% from a low base adds less in dollars per head than a rich country growing at 2%, so a fast growth rate does not mean it has caught up.
  • Growth is not the same as better living standards. Check distribution, employment, health, education and the environment. Real GDP per head can rise while living standards for most people fall.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

India and China at market rates and at PPP

India's economy looks much smaller per head when measured at market exchange rates than at PPP, because prices of food, housing and services are lower than in the US. PPP estimates narrow the gap, though India is still well below the US per head on either measure.

Exam use: why PPP gives a better comparison of living standards, with limits.

The informal economy in low-income countries

In many low-income countries a large share of work is in subsistence farming or small informal businesses that do not appear in the official statistics. GDP therefore tends to understate output and living standards, and the growth rate may be inaccurate too.

Exam use: the problem of unrecorded output in a comparison between low- and high-income countries.

Oil spills and GDP

Spending on cleaning up a major oil spill raises measured output, even though the spill reduces welfare. National income statistics normally add the clean-up spending and do not deduct the environmental damage.

Exam use: externalities and why alternatives such as MEW adjust GDP.

Growth and inequality

Some fast-growing economies have seen the top income groups take most of the gains while the incomes of the poorest changed little. Average GDP per head rose strongly, yet many people saw little improvement.

Exam use: why a rise in GDP per head may not raise living standards for most people.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/41 · Q5 20 marks National income statistics are often used as a measure of the standard of living. Consider to what extent they can be used to compare the standard of living between low-income and high-income countries.
What the mark scheme wants

  • Difficulties of comparison: culture, climate, the subsistence and informal economy, difficulty of collecting data, income distribution, exchange rates and purchasing power.
  • Comment on which measure of national income should be used (real, per capita, PPP).

PlanDefine. Real per capita PPP. Four difficulties. Alternatives. Judge: useful but incomplete.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The standard of living is the material and non-material well-being of a population. National income per head, adjusted for inflation and at purchasing power parity, is often used as a proxy. It may be a rough guide because high-income countries usually have better health, education and housing. However, comparisons between low- and high-income countries have problems. In low-income countries a large share of output is subsistence or informal and is not recorded, so income may be understated. Data collection is also harder and less accurate.

Averages hide distribution, so a high GDP per head could coexist with many people in poverty. Market exchange rates also ignore differences in price levels, so PPP should be used. Leisure, health, education, freedom and the environment are not included, so two countries with the same income could have different living standards. National income statistics may therefore be useful as a first indicator, especially at PPP, but they are best supported by non-monetary indicators such as the HDI.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/42 · Q6(b) 13 marks Discuss whether GDP is the best measure of the standard of living.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Define GDP and its use as real GDP per head as a measure of the standard of living.
  • Discuss the merits of HDI, MEW or other alternatives, setting them against GDP.
  • Level 4 (9 to 13) compares GDP with at least one alternative and evaluates the relative merits.
PlanGDP and why it is used. Its limits. One alternative. Judge which is better and when.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Real GDP per head measures the total value of output per person, adjusted for inflation, so it is easy to calculate and compare, and is often linked to higher living standards. However, it ignores distribution, unrecorded work, leisure and the environment, and spending on clean-up or defence adds to GDP. The HDI adds life expectancy and education, so it shows health and knowledge, while the MEW adjusts GDP for leisure, unpaid work and environmental damage.

GDP may be the best measure for the speed with which data can be collected and compared, but as a guide to well-being the HDI or MEW are probably better, although they involve judgements about weights.

Top tips

  • Use 'real' and 'per capita'. Real removes inflation. Per capita removes population size. Say both.
  • GDP versus GNI. GNI = GDP + net property income from abroad. NNI = GNI minus depreciation.
  • PPP in one line. The same basket costs the same in both countries. It corrects for price level differences.
  • Give three or four problems. Informal economy, distribution, externalities, accuracy, non-material factors.
  • Quote percentage changes. In data questions, calculate the growth rate and say what it means.
  • Mind the low base. A poor country growing quickly may still add less per head than a rich one.
  • Judge. Useful as a rough guide, especially at PPP, but best used with non-monetary indicators.
