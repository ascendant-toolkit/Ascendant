Monopsony and Trade Unions

What happens when employers have wage-setting power, and when workers organise to bargain. The monopsony diagram (MCL, ACL, MRP), what a trade union can and cannot achieve in a competitive market and against a monopsony, and when unions are powerful. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

A monopsony is a market with a single buyer of labour (in practice, an employer with wage-setting power). Because it must raise the wage for all its workers to hire one more, its marginal cost of labour (MCL) lies above the supply curve, and it hires where MCL = MRP but pays the lower wage on the supply curve, so employment and the wage are below the competitive level. A trade union bargains collectively: in a competitive market a higher wage tends to cost jobs, but against a monopsony it can raise the wage and employment together. How much power a union has depends on the elasticity of demand for the labour and other factors.

1 · Monopsony

A monopsony might be the main hospital in a region, a large factory in a small town, or a supermarket chain buying from farmers. It faces the whole upward-sloping supply curve of labour.

Why MCL is above the supply curve

The supply curve is the average cost of labour (ACL): the wage each worker receives. To hire one more worker the firm must raise the wage, and if it cannot pay different workers different wages it must pay the higher wage to everyone it already employs. The extra cost of the last worker (MCL) is therefore the new wage plus the rise for all the others, so MCL is above ACL and steeper.

The outcome

The firm hires where MCL = MRP. It then pays the wage on the supply (ACL) curve for that quantity. Compared with a competitive market, employment is lower and the wage is lower, and the wage is below the worker's MRP.

Read the diagram in this orderMRP meets MCL → go down to the quantity → go up to the supply (ACL) curve → across to the wage. Reading the wage off MCL or MRP is the commonest error.
  • Sources of monopsony power: few employers in the area, immobile workers (housing, family), specialised skills that only one employer needs, and imperfect information.
  • Consequences: workers are paid below their MRP (the gap is sometimes called exploitation), and fewer workers are employed than in a competitive market.
  • It is not always a pure single buyer: any employer able to set the wage has some monopsony power. The gig economy and large retailers are modern examples.

2 · Trade unions

A trade union is an organisation of workers. It bargains collectively over pay, hours, conditions and job security, and may use the threat of industrial action.

In a competitive labour market

A union that achieves a wage above the equilibrium wage acts like a minimum wage: the quantity of labour demanded falls and the quantity supplied rises, so some members lose their jobs (insiders gain, outsiders lose). The loss of jobs is smaller if demand for labour is inelastic.

Against a monopsony

A single buyer facing a single seller is a bilateral monopoly. A union wage between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage can raise both the wage and employment, because the firm can no longer cut the wage to hire fewer people. Above the competitive wage, employment falls.

Unions without job losses

A union may raise wages without cutting jobs if it also raises productivity (through training, better morale and lower turnover), which shifts the MRP curve to the right, or if it increases the demand for the product. The employer's spokesperson in a 2020 question made this point: the deal "would have to be met through improved productivity or by reductions in other costs".

4 · Evaluating monopsony and union power

  • Few labour markets are perfectly competitive or pure monopsonies. Real outcomes lie in between, so job losses from a union wage may be smaller than the simple model predicts.
  • Insiders and outsiders: higher pay for members may come at the cost of non-members and the unemployed.
  • Costs for firms and consumers: higher wages may raise prices or reduce profit and investment; strikes disrupt output.
  • Benefits: better conditions, training and job security, and higher productivity and lower turnover.
  • The effect depends on the size of the wage rise, the elasticity of demand for labour, and the time period.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Nurses and the health service

In many areas one health service employs most nurses, and pay is largely set centrally. Nurses have few other employers who need their skills. This is close to a monopsony, and pay rises that bring more people into nursing are consistent with the model.

Exam use: an example of a monopsony buyer of specialised labour, and of a union bargaining against it (bilateral monopoly).

A factory in a small town

When one large employer provides most of the jobs in a town and workers cannot easily move, the employer may be able to set pay below what it would pay in a competitive market.

Exam use: geographical immobility as a source of monopsony power.

Train drivers

Rail unions in several countries bargain for pay of train drivers. A strike can stop a whole network and there are few substitutes, so the demand for their labour is inelastic and the union has bargaining power.

Exam use: the factors that raise union power: inelastic demand for the labour, high membership, few substitutes.

Gig economy platforms

Ride-hailing and delivery platforms set the rate paid to many drivers and couriers who may have few alternatives locally. Some economists treat this as monopsony power. Drivers can often work for more than one platform, which weakens the case.

Exam use: a modern application. Evaluate with multi-apping and the ease of switching.

A strike at a car factory

A strike can raise a firm's costs by stopping production, so firms may agree a pay rise. The union's power falls when firms can move production to places where unions are weaker.

Exam use: the 2020 Paper 4 data response mentioned production moving to US states where unions were not as strong. This links to elastic demand and mobile capital.

Productivity deals

Some unions agree higher pay in return for training, new working practices or lower absence. If output per worker rises, the MRP of workers rises, which pays for the higher wage without job losses.

Exam use: a way for a union to raise wages without cutting employment: MRP shifts right.

Feb/March 2024 · 9708/42 · Q3 20 marks The introduction of a trade union into a perfectly competitive labour market will always lead to higher wage levels and a higher level of unemployment. With the help of a diagram, evaluate this statement.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (14): the characteristics of a perfectly competitive labour market; the role and main aims of a union, with the importance of wage bargaining; a diagram showing how the wage and employment are set in a perfectly competitive labour market and what happens in the firm; alternative ways a union might affect wages and employment, with reference to factors affecting demand and supply; the extent to which a union can influence wage and employment levels. Level 2 maximum without an accurate diagram.
  • Evaluation (6): "always" is the word to challenge: a union can raise wages without job losses if it raises productivity, or if demand is inelastic, or if the union wage is not above equilibrium.
KANE

In a perfectly competitive labour market there are many employers and many identical workers, labour is mobile, information is perfect and firms are wage takers, so the wage is set where the demand for labour (MRP) meets the supply of labour. A trade union is an organisation of workers that bargains collectively over pay and conditions, and it aims to raise wages, protect jobs and improve conditions.

A union may raise wages above the equilibrium wage, and if it does, unemployment is likely to follow. On a diagram with equilibrium wage W1 and employment Q1, a union wage W2 above W1 reduces the quantity of labour demanded to Q2, because each worker now costs more than they add to revenue at the old level of employment. The higher wage also attracts more people to offer their labour, so the quantity supplied rises to Q3. The gap Q2 to Q3 is unemployment. Union members who keep their jobs gain, while those who lose them, and those who cannot get a job, lose. How many jobs are lost depends on how elastic the demand for labour is: if firms can easily replace labour with machines, the loss will be larger, while if the demand is inelastic the loss could be small.

But a union will not always lead to both higher wages and higher unemployment. If the union negotiates better training, motivation and conditions, productivity could rise, which increases MPP and so MRP and shifts the demand for labour to the right. At the higher wage the firm may then employ as many workers as before, or more. Higher demand for the product would have the same effect. A union may also fail to raise wages if it is weak or if there are many non-union workers, so the wage stays at equilibrium.

In practice few labour markets are perfectly competitive, and in a monopsony a union could raise both wages and employment. Higher wages could also lead firms to raise prices or move production, which could reduce employment further in the long run. Overall, a union in a perfectly competitive market is likely to raise wages for those who keep their jobs and is likely to cause some unemployment unless it also raises productivity, so the claim that it will always lead to both is too strong.

Feb/March 2021 · 9708/42 · Q4(b) 13 marks 'Trade unions can gain an increase in wage levels only at the expense of higher levels of unemployment.' To what extent do you agree with this statement?
What the mark scheme wants
  • The impact of unions is judged by their ability to raise wages and the extent to which this has a negative effect on employment, using the traditional demand and supply model.
  • It is possible to argue that unions can raise wages without loss of jobs by increasing training and ultimately MRP, or in a bilateral bargaining situation where they negotiate with a monopsony buyer.
  • Good responses consider when unions raise wages only at the expense of unemployment, then give a conclusion on the statement.
KANE

A trade union bargains collectively for higher wages and better conditions for its members. In a competitive labour market the wage is set where demand equals supply, so a union wage above this level reduces the quantity of labour demanded and raises the quantity supplied.

This supports the statement in a competitive market. If the union secures a wage above equilibrium, employers hire fewer workers because the wage exceeds MRP for the marginal workers, and more people want the jobs, so unemployment rises. Union members who keep their jobs gain while others lose, and the more elastic the demand for labour, the larger the job loss.

However, a union may not need to cause unemployment. If a union negotiates training and better working practices that raise productivity, the MRP curve shifts to the right, so the higher wage can be paid without cutting jobs. Against a monopsony employer, a union wage between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage raises both the wage and employment, because the employer can no longer restrict hiring to keep the wage down.

Overall, the statement is partly correct. In a competitive market with no change in productivity, higher wages are likely to come at the cost of jobs, but where a union raises productivity or faces a monopsony the cost may be small or absent, so the claim that it happens only at the expense of unemployment is too strong.

Top tips

  • Explain why MCL is above ACL in one sentence: a higher wage to hire one more worker must be paid to all.
  • Find the quantity first, then the wage. MCL = MRP gives the quantity. Go up to the supply (ACL) curve for the wage.
  • Compare with competition: employment is lower, the wage is lower, and the wage is below MRP.
  • Draw the union wage line above the starting wage and show what happens to employment in the market you are given.
  • A union is not always bad for jobs. Productivity gains shift MRP right, and in a monopsony a union wage up to the competitive level raises employment.
  • Say "insiders and outsiders" when discussing who gains and loses from a union wage.
  • Union power depends on elasticity of demand for the labour, the share of the workforce in the union, profitability and substitutes.
  • Check strong words: "always", "only", "necessarily". Both statements in the questions on this page are too strong.
