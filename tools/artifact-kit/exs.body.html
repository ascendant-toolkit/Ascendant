Externality Diagrams, Step by Step

Six animated walk-throughs, each in three stages: what causes the externality, why it is a problem, and which government intervention puts output back at the socially optimum quantity. Step through each diagram, build the fix yourself, then practise.

The three-stage method

Every externality diagram tells the same story. Cause: decision makers count only their private costs and benefits, so a cost or benefit lands on a third party who is not part of the deal (an external cost or benefit). Problem: the market settles at the private equilibrium (Q1), but the socially optimum quantity (Q*) is where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost, so output is too high or too low and there is a welfare loss. Solution: the government internalises the externality so that the decision maker faces the full social cost or benefit, which moves output to Q*. A correct diagram always ends at Q*.

2 · How to read the labels

Private and social

MPC and MPB are what the producer or consumer counts. MSC = MPC + MEC when production harms others, and MSB = MPB + MEB when consumption helps others. A curve shifts only if there is an externality on that side.

Q1, Q* and the welfare loss

Q1 is where the market clears (private MB = private MC). Q* is where MSB = MSC. The welfare loss is the triangle between MSB and MSC for the units between Q1 and Q*: each is either costing society more than it is worth, or is worth more than it costs but is not made.

Size the fix correctlyThe tax or subsidy should equal the marginal externality at Q*. Too small and some welfare loss is left; too large and the market overshoots Q*, creating a new welfare loss on the other side. The Build the fix tab lets you test this.

The numbers used throughout (D, S, an external cost or benefit of $20 per unit) are illustrative, chosen so that every diagram is easy to read and Q* comes out exactly.

5 · Multiple choice

Cases are described in general terms. Check current details before quoting a specific rate, date or figure in an exam answer.

London congestion charge: a charge on a negative externality

Introduced in central London in 2003, drivers pay a daily charge to enter the zone. Each driver adds to delay, noise and pollution for others, which the driver does not pay for. The charge is a tax that makes drivers face more of the social cost. Traffic entering the zone was reported to fall after the charge began, although the effect could weaken over time as the road network and traffic patterns change.

In an exam: use it for an indirect tax on a negative externality of consumption, and evaluate by noting the charge is a single price that may not match the true external cost, and that it could hit lower-income drivers hardest.

EU Emissions Trading System: a cap and tradable permits

Started in 2005, the system sets a cap on carbon emissions from power stations and heavy industry and issues permits that firms can trade. The cap fixes the quantity, much like the vertical supply in the fifth walk-through, and the market finds the permit price. Early on, too many permits were handed out and the price fell very low, which shows how much depends on setting the cap correctly.

In an exam: use it to explain why permits are a quantity-based alternative to a tax, and to evaluate the problem of knowing where to set the cap.

England's carrier bag charge

A 5p charge on single-use plastic bags began in large shops in 2015 and was later raised and extended. Giving away bags free meant that the litter and marine pollution they cause were not in the price. Use of bags in the main supermarkets was reported to fall sharply, which suggests a small charge could change behaviour a good deal when substitutes (reusable bags) are easy to find.

In an exam: use it to show that the size of the response depends on the elasticity of demand.

The UK soft drinks levy

From 2018 the Soft Drinks Industry Levy charged producers according to the sugar content of their drinks. The aim was to reduce the health costs of sugar, a cost partly borne by the NHS and so by other taxpayers. Many producers reformulated their drinks to cut sugar, which shows how a tax on producers can shift supply as well as demand.

In an exam: use it for a tax aimed at a negative externality of consumption, and for the argument that the burden may be shared between firms and consumers.

Vaccination programmes

The NHS offers some vaccines free to eligible groups. A person who is vaccinated is less likely to pass on the disease, so others gain without paying, a positive externality of consumption that leads to under-consumption if people pay the full price. Free provision is the extreme form of a subsidy: the price to the user falls to zero.

In an exam: use it for a subsidy or provision on a positive externality, and mention the opportunity cost to the taxpayer.

Research and development tax relief

The UK and many other governments give firms tax relief on qualifying R&D spending. Innovation tends to spill over to rivals and to the wider economy, so a firm's private return is lower than the social return. The relief lowers the firm's cost of research, a subsidy that moves the supply of R&D towards the social optimum.

In an exam: use it for a positive externality of production, and evaluate by pointing out that spillovers are hard to measure and that some firms may claim relief for work they would have done anyway.

Solar panel feed-in tariffs

From 2010 the UK paid households and businesses for the electricity they generated from solar panels. Cleaner generation benefits people who never pay for it, so the market on its own could be expected to install too few panels. The payments acted as a subsidy, and the cost to bill-payers and the fall in the scheme's rates as panel costs dropped show the trade-offs.

In an exam: use it to link a subsidy to the external benefit and to discuss government failure through cost and poorly targeted payments.

Section A style: data response (20 marks)

Extract: a cement works in Marlow (invented for practice)

A large cement works in the town of Marlow produces 400,000 tonnes a year and sells to builders across the region. Its fuel and labour costs are the main items in the price of $60 a tonne. Local health authorities report that admissions for breathing problems among residents living near the works are 30% above the national average. A study estimates that dust and fumes impose a cost on the local community of about $12 for every tonne produced. The works employs 600 people, and the owners say that a higher cost of production would force them to move production abroad. The government is considering either a tax of $12 a tonne on cement output or a cap on the works' total output with tradable permits.

(a) Explain 4 marks Explain what is meant by a marginal external cost, using the cement works as an example.
ReminderFour marks: define it, say who bears it and why the market ignores it, and use the figure from the extract.

A marginal external cost is the extra cost that falls on third parties, who are not part of the transaction, when one more unit is produced or consumed. In Marlow, dust and fumes impose a cost of about $12 on local residents for each tonne of cement, and this is not paid by the works or its buyers. Because the works counts only its private costs, the price of $60 does not reflect the full cost to society, which could be about $72 a tonne.

Common mistakeDefining an externality in general terms and not using the $12 from the extract.
(b) Explain 6 marks Using a diagram, explain why the market output of cement could be higher than the socially optimum output.
ReminderDraw D (= MPB = MSB), S (= MPC) and MSC above it by $12. Mark Q1 and Q*, shade the welfare loss, and refer to the diagram in your words.

A negative externality of production means the marginal social cost (MSC) is above the marginal private cost (MPC) by the marginal external cost, here about $12 a tonne. The works decides output where its marginal private benefit equals MPC, at Q1. The socially optimum output is lower, at Q*, where MSB equals MSC.

Between Q* and Q1, each tonne costs society more than it is worth to buyers, so the market is likely to over-produce cement and there is a welfare loss, shown by the shaded triangle. The health costs of the residents near Marlow are not in the price, which is why the market could produce too much.

Common mistakeDrawing a diagram but not referring to Q1, Q* and the welfare loss in the text.
(c) Evaluate 10 marks Evaluate whether a tax of $12 a tonne or a cap with tradable permits would be the better policy for the government of Marlow.
ReminderExplain how each policy moves output to Q*, compare them using the extract (the $12 estimate, the 600 jobs, the threat to move abroad), then reach a judgement that depends on a condition.

A tax of $12 a tonne equal to the marginal external cost shifts supply up onto MSC, so output falls to Q* and the externality is internalised. The works faces a cost of $72 a tonne, and the tax raises revenue that could help pay for local health care. Tradable permits cap output at Q*. Because the permits can be traded, the producers that can cut pollution most cheaply may sell permits to those that cannot, so a given reduction could be achieved at the lowest cost.

The tax gives certainty about the cost but not about the quantity: if demand is more elastic than expected, output could fall by more than Q*, and if the $12 estimate is wrong the tax could be too high or too low. A cap gives certainty about the quantity but the price of permits could vary, and the cap must still be set at the right level. The owners say a higher cost could move production abroad, which could cost 600 jobs. A tax could be set low enough to reduce this risk, but it may then leave some welfare loss, and permits issued free to the works at first might cost less but would reward the polluter.

On balance, the tax is likely to be better in Marlow because there is only one main polluter, so there are few gains from trading permits and a tax is simpler to administer. If more works joined the market and their abatement costs differed widely, permits could become the better option. The answer also depends on how accurate the $12 estimate is.

Common mistakeDescribing both policies without comparing them, or ignoring the details in the extract. The judgement should say when each policy would be better.

Section B style: essays (20 marks)

Essay 1 Discuss 20 marks With the help of a diagram, discuss whether an indirect tax is the most effective way to correct a negative externality of production.
What Level 3 needsA correct labelled diagram that is explained in the text (without one the 14 marks stop at Level 2), a developed and applied chain for the tax, a second developed point or alternative, and evaluation that reaches a justified conclusion (E2).

A negative externality of production arises when producing a good imposes a cost on third parties, such as pollution, so that the marginal social cost (MSC) is above the marginal private cost (MPC). An indirect tax is a tax on a good that raises the cost of supplying it. Producers decide output where MPB = MPC, at Q1, so the market over-produces compared with the socially optimum Q*, where MSB = MSC. Between Q* and Q1 each unit costs society more than it is worth to consumers, which is the welfare loss shown by the shaded triangle.

A tax equal to the marginal external cost at Q* raises costs by that amount at every output, so supply shifts upwards and to the left, from S to S plus tax, which lies on MSC. The market equilibrium moves to Q*, the welfare loss disappears and the externality is internalised. The tax also raises revenue that the government could use to repair the damage. For example, a carbon tax on electricity generation makes coal-fired power more costly relative to renewable energy, so producers have an incentive to switch.

An alternative is tradable permits, where the government caps output at Q* and firms trade the right to pollute. Supply becomes vertical at Q*, so the quantity is certain, and firms that can reduce pollution cheaply could sell permits to those that cannot. Regulation, such as banning a harmful process, could also be used and gives certainty about the outcome, but it does not make firms pay for the pollution that remains and it gives no incentive to cut it further.

A tax has limits. The marginal external cost is hard to measure, so the tax could be set too high or too low; if the tax is too low a welfare loss remains, and if it is too high output falls below Q*. If demand is inelastic, consumers could bear most of the tax and output could fall by little, and a tax could reduce competitiveness if rivals abroad do not face the same charge. Permits give a firmer limit on quantity but depend on setting the cap correctly, and the initial allocation could favour existing firms.

Overall, an indirect tax is likely to be effective where the external cost can be estimated reasonably well, where there are many polluters with different abatement costs and where demand is elastic enough to respond. Where the damage is severe or the cost of error is high, a cap or regulation may be preferable because it fixes the quantity. A tax is therefore one of the more efficient tools, but not always the most effective.

Common mistakeWriting the whole essay about how the tax works and leaving the evaluation to one short last paragraph. About 6 of the 20 marks are for evaluation, so weigh the tax against alternatives and say what the answer depends on.
Essay 2 Discuss 20 marks With the help of a diagram, discuss whether a government information campaign is a better way than a subsidy to increase the consumption of a good with a positive externality of consumption.
What Level 3 needsTwo developed, applied chains (one for the campaign shifting demand, one for the subsidy shifting supply), a diagram showing Q1 and Q*, and a judgement that compares them and says when each is better.

A positive externality of consumption arises when consuming a good benefits third parties, so the marginal social benefit (MSB) is above the marginal private benefit (MPB) by the marginal external benefit. A merit good is one that consumers tend to under-value, for example because of information failure. Consumers decide using MPB, so the market clears at Q1 where MPB = MPC. The socially optimum quantity is Q*, where MSB = MSC, and between Q1 and Q* each unit is worth more to society than it costs, so there is a welfare loss and the good is under-consumed. Vaccination is an example: a vaccinated person is less likely to infect others.

An information campaign tries to correct the under-valuation. If people understand the full benefits, their willingness to pay rises and demand (MPB) shifts to the right. If the campaign shifts demand as far as MSB, the market reaches Q* and the welfare loss is removed without any change in the price.

A subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit lowers firms' costs, so supply shifts down and to the right. The price falls and quantity rises to Q*. It is paid to firms first, and it helps consumers indirectly, because it is the lower price that persuades them to buy more. A subsidy may help in poorer areas where price is the main barrier to vaccination.

Both have limits. The effect of a campaign is uncertain: it could change only a part of the demand, its effect could fade and it may not reach those who are most reluctant. A subsidy costs the taxpayer, has an opportunity cost, and some of it could go to people who would have bought the good anyway. Both depend on measuring the external benefit. A campaign is likely to be cheaper per head and respects choice, but it may take longer. A subsidy gives a more predictable, faster response if demand is elastic.

Overall, where the main problem is that people lack information or underestimate the benefit, a campaign is likely to be the better first step because it tackles the cause. Where cost is the barrier, a subsidy is more likely to work. The best policy could be a combination of both, and it depends on why consumption is below Q*.

Common mistakeShifting supply for the campaign. It changes preferences, so the demand curve moves. Also avoid concluding that one policy is always better: say what the answer depends on.

Top tips

  • Tell the whole story. Cause, problem, solution. Many answers stop after the welfare loss and forget to show the intervention.
  • End at Q*. After the tax or subsidy, mark the new equilibrium at the socially optimum quantity and say that the welfare loss has gone.
  • Know which curve moves. Costs change with a tax or subsidy, so supply shifts. The demand curve moves only if preferences change (for example through education).
  • Match the direction. Negative externality: too much, tax pushes supply up and left. Positive externality: too little, subsidy pushes supply down and right.
  • Size it at Q*. The tax or subsidy equals the marginal externality at the socially optimum quantity.
  • Label everything. MPB, MPC, MSB or MSC, Q1, Q*, P1, P*, and the welfare loss. Refer to them in your text.
  • Evaluate the policy. Measuring the externality, elasticity, who bears the tax, the cost of a subsidy, and the risk of government failure.
