Effects of Supply-Side Policy on the Aims

How supply-side policy measures could help a government meet its macroeconomic aims, and why they may not work as planned. Syllabus 4.4.3.

The big idea

Supply-side policy can help a government reach several macroeconomic aims at once: higher growth, lower unemployment, lower inflationary pressure and a better balance of payments. Unlike fiscal and monetary policy it does this by raising productive capacity, so there is less conflict between growth and inflation. But it is slow, costly and its effects are uncertain, and some measures may harm equity or the environment.

Aim by aim

AimHow supply-side policy could helpPossible problem
Economic growthAS shifts right: potential output and long-run growth riseTakes years; measures may not raise productivity
Full employmentTraining and mobility reduce structural unemployment; incentives help the unemployed find workDoes not cure cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment; some measures cost jobs at first
Stable pricesLower costs and higher output hold prices down (cost-push pressure falls)Little effect on inflation caused by excess demand in the short run
Balance of paymentsLower costs make exports more competitive; domestic goods may replace importsHigher incomes also raise imports
Redistribution of incomeTraining can raise incomes of the low-skilled; some jobs createdLower direct taxes and benefit cuts may widen inequality
Environmental sustainabilityGreen infrastructure and training in new technologiesDeregulation and faster growth may increase pollution

Supply-side policy and the budget

  • Could reduce the deficit: if growth and employment rise, tax revenue rises and benefit spending falls; privatisation raises sale revenue and ends subsidies to loss-making firms.
  • Could widen the deficit at first: education and infrastructure spending cost money, and tax cuts reduce revenue.

Why supply-side policy may not work as planned

  • Time lags. It can take years for training or new roads to raise output.
  • Cost. Many measures need government spending, which has an opportunity cost.
  • Uncertainty. Workers may not respond to tax cuts; skills may not match jobs.
  • Wrong problem. If unemployment is caused by low demand (cyclical), supply-side policy alone may not help and cuts to benefits could reduce demand.
  • Side effects. Inequality, loss of public services, private monopolies, pollution.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Fewer people out of work after reform

Countries that combined retraining, job-search help and flexible labour rules, such as Germany in the 2000s, saw unemployment fall while others had high unemployment for longer. Some critics note a rise in low-paid jobs.

Exam use: Supply-side measures reducing unemployment, evaluated with inequality.

Selling state firms to raise revenue

Governments have raised large sums by privatising firms. This helped to reduce deficits in the short run but removed future profits and sometimes led to higher prices.

Exam use: Privatisation and the budget: short-run revenue against long-run loss.

Long delays on big projects

Major infrastructure projects often take a decade or more and cost more than first planned.

Exam use: A clear example of time lags and costs when evaluating supply-side policy.

Uganda and unemployment

A past paper describes a government using supply-side measures to cope with persistently high unemployment.

Exam use: Linking measures to the cause of unemployment: structural against cyclical.

Q1 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how supply-side policy measures could reduce unemployment. (Oct/Nov 2023, P22)
ReminderCover several measures, each with a mechanism: education, lower taxes or benefits, lower corporation tax, privatisation or deregulation. Reward the same point once.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Supply-side policy measures aim to increase aggregate supply. Education and training raise skills and mobility, so workers can fill vacancies and structural unemployment could fall. Lower income tax or lower unemployment benefit could increase the incentive to find work quickly, reducing frictional and voluntary unemployment. Lower corporation tax and tax incentives could encourage firms to invest and expand output, increasing demand for workers. Privatisation and deregulation could lower costs and raise efficiency, so firms may be able to afford more workers. However, these measures take time to work.
Q2 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not the use of supply-side policy measures will reduce unemployment. (May/June 2022, P21)
ReminderTwo sides. For: training, incentives, investment and competition. Against: time lag, does not cure cyclical unemployment, benefit cuts reduce demand. Judge with 'it depends on the type of unemployment'.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Supply-side policy measures are designed to increase aggregate supply. Education and training can improve workers' skills and mobility, which could reduce structural unemployment, and lower income tax or lower unemployment benefit could raise the incentive to work. Privatisation, deregulation and lower corporation tax could encourage firms to expand and employ more workers. However, these measures can take years to work. They are unlikely to reduce cyclical unemployment caused by low demand in a recession, because firms will not hire if they cannot sell more, and a cut in unemployment benefit could reduce spending and make the problem worse. Overall, supply-side policy is more likely to reduce unemployment that is structural or frictional. For cyclical unemployment demand-side policy may work better, so the answer depends on the cause.
Q3 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether supply-side policy measures will reduce a government's budget deficit. (Feb/Mar 2025, P22)
ReminderBoth sides. For: growth, tax revenue, privatisation revenue, less benefit spending. Against: spending on education and infrastructure, tax cuts reduce revenue, time lag. Do not confuse a budget deficit with a trade deficit.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A budget deficit is when government spending is higher than revenue. Supply-side policy could reduce it if education and training raise productivity, growth and employment, since higher incomes raise direct and indirect tax revenue and fewer people claim benefits. Privatisation could raise revenue from the sale and end subsidies to loss-making state firms, and profitable private firms may pay more tax. However, these effects can take years to appear. In the short run, spending on education and infrastructure raises government spending, and tax cuts reduce revenue, so the deficit could widen. Overall, supply-side policy is more likely to reduce the deficit in the long run if it succeeds in raising growth, but in the short run it may increase it. The outcome depends on the size of the measures and how quickly output responds.
Q4 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not supply-side policy measures always promote economic growth. (May/June 2022, P23)
ReminderThe word 'always' invites 'not always'. Show how they could promote growth, then why they might not.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Economic growth is a rise in real GDP, and supply-side measures aim to raise it by increasing productive capacity. Training could make workers more productive, infrastructure could cut costs and tax incentives could raise investment, all shifting AS to the right. However, they do not always work. Training may not match the skills firms need, firms and workers may not respond to tax cuts, and large projects can be late or wasteful. Cuts in benefits could reduce demand, and if the economy is in recession, low demand rather than supply may be holding growth back. Overall, supply-side policy is likely to promote growth in the long run when measures are well targeted, but it is not guaranteed. The result depends on the measure chosen and on the state of demand.
Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not supply-side policy measures can reduce inflation. (Oct/Nov 2021, P22)
ReminderFor: lower costs, higher output, competition. Against: little effect on excess demand, slow, some measures raise AD.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Supply-side policy raises aggregate supply, which can lower the price level or slow price rises. Training and infrastructure could raise productivity and lower costs per unit, so firms could hold or cut prices; deregulation and privatisation could increase competition. With a higher supply of goods, demand-pull pressure may ease. However, the effects can take years. Supply-side policy does little to reduce inflation caused by excess demand in the short run, and tax cuts could even raise spending and prices. Overall, supply-side policy could reduce inflation over the long term, particularly cost-push inflation, but demand-side policy may be needed for faster results.

Top tips

  • Match the cause of unemployment to the policy. Structural and frictional: supply-side. Cyclical: demand-side.
  • Give a long-run gain and a short-run cost. Training raises productivity later but costs money now.
  • Budget deficit is not a trade deficit. A past paper punished this: keep to government spending and revenue.
  • Always evaluate. Time lag, cost, uncertainty, inequality, side effects.
  • 'Always' means 'not always'. Question the word in the exam and show when it fails.
