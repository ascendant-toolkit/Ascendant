Measurement of Economic Growth

Real GDP, nominal GDP, GDP per head, how to calculate the growth rate, and the limits of GDP. Syllabus 4.5.2.

The big idea

Economic growth is measured by the change in real GDP. Gross domestic product (GDP) is the total value of all final goods and services produced in a country in a year. Nominal GDP is measured at current prices, so it rises when prices rise even if output does not. Real GDP removes the effect of inflation, so a rise in real GDP means more goods and services were actually produced.

Nominal and real GDP

Nominal GDP

Output valued at the prices of the year. If prices rise 4% and output stays the same, nominal GDP still rises by 4%, which would mislead.

Real GDP

Output valued at constant (base-year) prices, adjusted for inflation. It shows the change in the amount produced.

Real GDP ≈ nominal GDP adjusted for inflation  ·  Growth rate = (GDP this year − GDP last year) ÷ GDP last year × 100

Example: Nominal GDP rises from $100bn to $106bn (+6%) but prices rise 4%. Real GDP growth is about 6% − 4% = 2%.

GDP per head (per capita)

Real GDP per head = real GDP ÷ population. It is a better guide to the average person's income than total GDP, because a large economy with a huge population can have low output per person. If population grows faster than real GDP, real GDP per head falls.

What GDP does and does not tell us

What it is useful for

  • Comparing growth over time and between countries
  • Checking whether the economy is in a boom or recession
  • Seeing the size of the economy

Limitations

  • The informal economy and unpaid work are missed
  • It says nothing about how income is shared
  • It ignores pollution and use of resources
  • It does not capture quality of life, leisure or health
  • Data may be inaccurate

Inflation and nominal GDP

In countries with high inflation, such as Argentina in recent years, nominal GDP figures can rise sharply even when real output falls, which is why economists use real GDP.

The informal economy

In many low-income countries a large share of work is informal (street trading, small farms), so official GDP may understate total output.

Q1 Calculate 1 marks Calculate India's economic growth rate in 2017 using the table: real GDP $8,750.0bn (2016) and $9,362.5bn (2017).
ReminderChange ÷ original × 100.
(9,362.5 − 8,750.0) ÷ 8,750.0 × 100 = 7%.
Q2 Define 2 marks What is meant by real GDP?
ReminderTwo ideas: the value of output, and adjusted for inflation.
Real GDP is the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year adjusted for changes in prices (inflation).
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain why real GDP is a better measure of economic growth than nominal GDP.
ReminderPrices can rise without output rising. Give a number if you can.
Nominal GDP is measured at current prices, so it rises if prices rise even when output is unchanged. Real GDP removes the effect of inflation, so it shows the change in the volume of goods and services produced. For example, if nominal GDP rises by 6% and prices rise by 4%, real output has risen only about 2%.
Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain why real GDP per head could fall even when real GDP rises.
ReminderPopulation must rise faster than output.
Real GDP per head is real GDP divided by population. If population grows faster than real GDP, output is spread across more people, for example real GDP growing 2% while population grows 3%, so the average person has less output and real GDP per head falls.
Q5 Explain 4 marks Explain two limitations of using GDP to compare living standards.
ReminderTwo separate limitations: inequality, informal economy, pollution, health or leisure.
GDP does not show how income is shared. A country could have a high GDP but with most income going to a small group, so many people may have low living standards. It also leaves out the informal economy and unpaid work. Output in the informal economy is not recorded, so GDP could underestimate how much is produced, especially in poorer countries.

Top tips

  • Real, not nominal. Always use real GDP for growth; nominal includes price rises.
  • Growth rate = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Divide by the old figure, not the new.
  • Show your working. Cambridge awards marks for method as well as answer.
  • GDP per head divides by population. It can fall even when GDP rises.
  • Know the limits of GDP. Inequality, informal economy, pollution, quality of life.
