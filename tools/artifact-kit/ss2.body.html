Supply-Side Policy Measures

Education and training, infrastructure, labour market reforms, lower direct taxes, deregulation, incentives and privatisation: how each raises AS and the catch. Syllabus 4.4.2.

The big idea

The syllabus lists seven supply-side measures: education and training, infrastructure spending, labour market reforms, lower direct taxes, deregulation, improving incentives to work and invest, and privatisation. Each works by raising the quality or quantity of resources, or by raising efficiency and incentives. For each one learn how it shifts AS, who gains, and the catch.

The seven measures

MeasureWhat it meansHow it raises AS
Education and trainingSchools, colleges, apprenticeships, retrainingMore skilled and productive workers; workers can move to jobs that are available
Infrastructure spendingRoads, rail, ports, power, broadbandLower costs and faster trade; better capital for all firms
Labour market reformsEasier hiring and firing, weaker union power, reduced unemployment benefit, help with job search and mobilityMore people willing to take work; lower and more flexible labour costs
Lower direct taxesCuts in income tax and corporation taxMore incentive to work, save and invest; firms keep more profit to invest
DeregulationRemoving rules and barriers that restrict competitionMore firms enter, competition lowers costs and raises efficiency
Improving incentives to work and investTax breaks for investment, grants and subsidies for new firms, lower benefits for the able to workMore employment, more investment, more start-ups
PrivatisationSelling state-owned firms to private ownersProfit motive and competition cut costs; sale raises revenue

Raising the quality or quantity of resources

Quality of labour and capital

Education and training raise labour quality. Infrastructure and investment raise the quality of capital. Both are slow and costly.

Quantity of labour and capital

Lower taxes and reduced benefits may increase the number of people willing to work. Investment incentives raise the capital stock.

Incentives

If people keep more of what they earn, and benefits are not much higher than wages, more people may choose to work, work longer or start a firm.

Efficiency and competition

Deregulation and privatisation expose firms to competition and the profit motive, so they may cut waste. Without competition, privatisation may just replace a public monopoly with a private one.

Evaluating the measures

  • Time lags: training and infrastructure take years to pay off.
  • Cost and opportunity cost: education and infrastructure need spending, which may mean borrowing or giving up other uses.
  • Uncertainty: training may not match the skills employers want; lower taxes may not change behaviour much.
  • Winners and losers: labour reforms and cuts to benefits may widen inequality; privatisation may cost jobs.
  • Demand effects: cutting benefits or wages can reduce spending and AD.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Reforming labour markets

Germany's early-2000s reforms tightened the rules on unemployment benefit and encouraged flexible jobs. Unemployment fell, but some of the new jobs were low paid.

Exam use: Labour market reforms and an evaluation point about inequality.

Privatising state firms

In the 1980s the UK sold state-owned firms such as the telephone and gas companies. Many became more efficient, but in some cases prices rose and critics said competition was weak.

Exam use: Privatisation: efficiency gain and the risk of private monopoly.

Infrastructure for growth

Many governments fund roads, rail, ports and broadband. Large projects such as high-speed rail often cost more and take longer than planned.

Exam use: Infrastructure lowering costs, and a good source of evaluation: cost and time lag.

Cutting corporation tax

Several countries, including Ireland, have used low corporation tax rates to attract foreign firms and investment.

Exam use: Lower direct taxes and incentives to invest, with the cost of lost revenue.

Deregulation in China

As China loosened controls on prices and entry from the late 1970s, many private firms were created. A recent paper used China to show how deregulation can move an economy towards a market system.

Exam use: Deregulation, competition and the speed of change.

Q1 Identify 2 marks Identify two supply-side policy measures.
ReminderTwo named measures from the list. Short phrases.
Education and training (or infrastructure spending). Privatisation (or deregulation, lower direct taxes, labour market reforms).
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain two supply-side policy measures that a government could use. (May/June 2020, P23 style)
ReminderTwo different measures, each with a mechanism. Say how each raises productivity or output.
One measure is education and training. This could raise the skills and productivity of workers and help them move into jobs that are available, so aggregate supply could rise. A second measure is privatisation. Private owners seek profit and may face competition, so they could cut costs and raise efficiency, which may raise output and lower prices.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain how lower income tax rates could increase the supply of labour.
ReminderLink tax to take-home pay and then to the decision to work.
Lower income tax rates raise workers' take-home pay. Working becomes more rewarding compared with leisure or unemployment benefit, so more people may enter the labour force or work longer hours. Aggregate supply could rise as a result. However, some workers may not change their hours, particularly if they have fixed commitments.
Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain how privatisation could improve efficiency.
ReminderProfit motive, competition, cutting costs. Add a limit.
Privatisation is the sale of state-owned firms to private owners. Private owners are motivated by profit, so they may cut waste and costs, and if the firm faces competition it may also improve quality and prices. However, if it becomes a private monopoly it may raise prices.
Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not education and training is the best supply-side policy measure.
ReminderWeigh education and training against another measure. For: raises skills, mobility, productivity, lowers structural unemployment. Against: slow, costly, mismatch; other measures may be quicker.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Education and training is a supply-side measure that raises the skills of the labour force. It could raise productivity and help workers move into jobs that are available, reducing structural unemployment. Better skills could attract investment and raise earnings, increasing tax revenue in the long run. However, it takes years to work and has an opportunity cost. By comparison, privatisation or lower taxes could affect incentives more quickly, and infrastructure may lower costs for all firms. Training is also wasted if the skills taught do not match what employers want. Overall, education and training is likely to be a strong long-term measure, but the best policy depends on the problem: if unemployment is structural it may be the best, whereas if firms need cheaper transport, infrastructure may be more effective.

Top tips

  • Seven measures: education and training, infrastructure, labour market reforms, lower direct taxes, deregulation, incentives, privatisation.
  • For each measure give a mechanism. 'Training raises productivity' is a developed point; 'training is good' is not.
  • Say it raises AS. Link every measure to a shift of AS, higher output and often lower prices.
  • Evaluate with time lag, cost and uncertainty. And with who loses: lower benefits, job losses, monopoly.
  • Use hedged words. Could, may, is likely to.
