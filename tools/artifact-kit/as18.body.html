Free, Private and Public Goods

1.6.1 to 1.6.2: free goods, private (economic) goods and public goods, rivalry and excludability, and why the free rider problem means a market will not supply public goods. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

A private good (an economic good) is rivalrous (one person's use stops another using it) and excludable (non-payers can be kept out), so it is scarce, has a price and an opportunity cost. A free good is not scarce: it has zero opportunity cost and uses no factors of production, such as fresh air. A public good is non-rivalrous and non-excludable, so people can free ride and enjoy it without paying; private firms cannot make a profit supplying it, so a free market will not provide it and the government usually does, funded by taxation. Do not confuse a good that is free of charge (paid for by taxes) with a free good.

  • A good that costs nothing at the point of use is not necessarily a free good. A government vaccination programme or school lunches are free of charge to the user but use scarce resources and are paid for by taxpayers, so they have an opportunity cost.
  • A good provided by the government is not automatically a public good: free school meals, health care and education are private goods (rival and excludable) that are provided free or subsidised by the state.
  • Private goods are not only supplied by the private sector. The state can supply private goods too.
  • A product may be both a private good and a demerit good (for example a plastic bottle): rival and excludable, but over-consumed because of information failure (see syllabus 1.6.3 and 1.6.4).

2 · Public goods (1.6.2)

CharacteristicMeaningExample
Non-rivalrousOne person's use does not reduce the amount available to othersMany ships can use the light from a lighthouse
Non-excludableIt is impossible or impractical to stop people who have not paid from using itEveryone in a country is protected by national defence
  • The free rider problem: because no one can be excluded, each person has an incentive to let others pay and enjoy the good free. If everyone does so, no one pays, so revenue is zero and no firm will supply it.
  • Therefore a free market will not provide public goods (market failure): there is no price mechanism. The government provides them and pays for them from tax revenue, which compels everyone to contribute.
  • Examples: national defence, street lighting, flood-control barriers, lighthouses, clean air regulations, public parks (to a degree).
  • Pure public goods are rare. Many goods are quasi-public (partly non-rival or non-excludable), such as a beach, which may be crowded (rival) or have a fee or gate (excludable). Roads, education and health care are not public goods in the strict sense, because they are rival and excludable.
  • A public good can become a private good if technology makes it excludable (for example, a toll gate or electronic charging on a motorway, or scrambled satellite signals).
  • Opportunity cost: provision of public goods uses scarce resources, so there is an opportunity cost even though users pay nothing directly.
Exam tipGive both characteristics of a public good and explain the free rider problem to say why the market will not supply it. Use pure examples such as national defence and street lighting, not roads or schools.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A lighthouse

A lighthouse beam helps every ship, and one ship's use does not reduce the light for another. The owner cannot stop passing ships from using it, so cannot charge them. Lighthouses are the classic example of a public good, which is why governments and harbour authorities build them.

Exam use: non-rivalry, non-excludability and the free rider problem.

Free school lunches

A government pays for lunches for all children in school. The lunches are free of charge to the child, but each meal can be eaten by only one child (rival) and a child who is not registered can be kept out (excludable). They are private goods provided by the state, and cost taxpayers resources.

Exam use: the difference between a good that is free of charge and a free good, and between a private good and a public good.

A beach

A public beach has no entry fee and few people use it in winter, so it behaves like a public good. In summer it becomes crowded, so one person's use reduces another's enjoyment (rival), and some beaches charge for entry or parking (excludable). It is a quasi-public good.

Exam use: why real goods fall between the pure types.

Motorway tolls

A free motorway is close to a public good because it is hard to charge individual drivers. Electronic tolling makes it possible to exclude those who do not pay, so the road becomes a private good that can be run for profit.

Exam use: how technology that makes a good excludable can turn it from a public good into a private good.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/22 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain, using examples, the difference between private goods and public goods and why public goods will not be supplied by private enterprise.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): the distinction based on excludability (1) and rivalry in consumption (1).
  • Application (up to 6): why public goods are not supplied in a free market economy, because of non-excludability and non-rivalry (4 marks), with reference to the free rider issue (2 marks).
PlanPrivate: rival and excludable. Public: non-rival and non-excludable. Free riders: no revenue, so no private supply.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A private good, such as a loaf of bread, is rival, because when one person eats it another cannot, and excludable, because the seller can refuse to sell to someone who does not pay. A public good, such as street lighting, is non-rival, because one person's use does not reduce the amount available, and non-excludable, because it is impractical to stop people from using it.

Private enterprise will not supply public goods because, if people cannot be excluded, they can enjoy the good without paying. This is the free rider problem: each person waits for others to pay. As a result the firm cannot collect revenue or make a profit, so the market fails to provide the good and the government must provide it and pay for it through taxes.

Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/21 · Q2(a) 8 marks Explain whether private goods, free goods and public goods will all be sold in a free market economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): of private goods, free goods and public goods (just two types earns 1).
  • Application (up to 6, 2 for each type): private goods are sold, as they are rival and excludable; free goods are not sold as they are not scarce, have no price attached, supply equals demand at zero price and need no factors of production; public goods are not sold because of non-rivalry and non-excludability.
PlanPrivate: yes. Free: no, as they have no price. Public: no, because of the free rider problem.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Private goods are economic goods consumed by an individual for their own benefit, are rival and excludable, so they are sold in a free market. Free goods, such as fresh air, are not scarce and have a zero opportunity cost. Public goods are non-rival and non-excludable.

In a free market private goods will be sold because firms can charge a price and earn a profit. Free goods will not be sold because they have no price, supply equals demand at zero price and no factors of production are used. Public goods will not be sold, because people cannot be excluded and so will not pay, so firms cannot make a profit.

Based on Feb/March 2025 · 9708/22 · Q3(a) 8 marks Explain what is meant by a public good and a private good (economic good) and consider the extent to which a beach could be described as a public good.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (3): explanation of rivalrous and non-rivalrous (1); excludable and non-excludable (1); correctly applied to a public good and a private good to explain the difference (1).
  • Analysis (3): the extent to which a beach might be rivalrous (1); the extent to which it might be excludable (1); alternatives are also considered (1).
  • Evaluation (2): a valid explanation that the extent a beach is public may vary (a quasi-public good) and a justified conclusion.
PlanDefine both goods using rivalry and excludability. Test the beach on each. Quasi-public: depends on crowding and charges.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A public good is non-rival, so one person's use does not reduce its availability for others, and non-excludable, so non-payers cannot be kept out. A private good is rival and excludable: when one person uses it, others cannot, and it can be denied to those who do not pay.

A beach may be non-rival when it is uncrowded, since more visitors do not reduce anyone else's use, and it may be non-excludable if it is open to anyone with no charge. But in summer a beach can become overcrowded, which makes it rival, and some beaches charge for entry or parking, which makes them excludable.

Therefore a beach is probably a quasi-public good: it is close to a public good when open and uncrowded, but not when it is crowded or has an entry charge. Whether it is public depends on the beach and the time of year.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/22 · Q2(a) 8 marks With the help of examples, explain the difference between public goods and free goods and consider whether a market economy can ever produce public goods.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (3): free goods are not scarce, have zero opportunity cost and need no factors of production (1); public goods are non-excludable and non-rival, each explained (1); appropriate examples of both, such as fresh air as a free good and national defence as a public good (1). Roads, education or health are not accepted as examples, nor is 'provided by the government'.
  • Analysis (3): the characteristics of public goods make it easy not to pay, so firms cannot charge a price (the free rider problem) (1), so people enjoy benefits without paying and firms cannot make a profit (1), so a market economy is unlikely to provide them (1).
  • Evaluation (2): unless some method of making them excludable exists, or the government subsidises production, or firms act in the public interest, it is unlikely that a market economy could produce them.
PlanFree good: not scarce. Public good: non-rival and non-excludable. Free rider problem. Evaluate: excludability by technology, subsidies.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A free good, such as fresh air, is not scarce and has no opportunity cost, since no factors of production are used. A public good, such as national defence, is non-rival and non-excludable, but it is scarce, because it uses resources.

The two characteristics of public goods let people enjoy the good without paying, which is the free rider problem. Firms cannot charge a price or earn a profit, so a market economy is unlikely to produce them.

However, a market might produce some public goods if technology makes them excludable, such as scrambled signals, or if the government pays a subsidy to private firms. So a pure market probably cannot produce true public goods, but a mixed arrangement can.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/23 · Q2(a) 8 marks With the aid of examples, explain the characteristics of public goods and free goods and consider whether free-of-charge vaccinations offered by a government should be classified as a free good.
What the mark scheme wants

PlanCharacteristics of both. Vaccine: free of charge but uses resources, so not a free good. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A free good, such as fresh air, is not scarce and has zero opportunity cost. A public good, such as street lighting, is non-rival and non-excludable.

A vaccination offered free of charge costs the user nothing, but it uses scarce resources such as doctors, nurses and medicines, which are paid for by taxpayers. The government therefore has an opportunity cost, because the money could have been used for other things.

So free-of-charge vaccinations should not be classified as a free good. Free of charge is not the same as free: the good is scarce and has a cost to society, although not to the individual.

Top tips

  • Define with rivalry and excludability. Private: rival and excludable. Public: non-rival and non-excludable.
  • Free is not 'free of charge'. A free good is not scarce. A government service paid for by taxes still has an opportunity cost.
  • Explain the free rider problem. No one can be excluded, so people will not pay, and the market does not supply.
  • Use pure examples. National defence, street lighting, lighthouses, flood barriers. Not roads, schools or health.
  • Government provision does not make a good public. School meals are private goods.
  • Mention quasi-public goods. A beach is partly rival and partly excludable.
  • Say technology can change the type. Tolls make a motorway excludable.
