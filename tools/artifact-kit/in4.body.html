Consequences of Inflation

How inflation affects savers, lenders and borrowers, consumers, workers, firms and the economy, and the effects of deflation. Syllabus 4.7.4.

The big idea

Inflation makes some people better off and some worse off. The main idea is the real interest rate: the nominal interest rate minus the inflation rate. If inflation is higher than the interest rate, savers and lenders lose (their money buys less) and borrowers gain (the real value of their debt falls). Inflation also affects consumers, workers, firms and the whole economy, and deflation has its own costs.

Savers, lenders and borrowers

Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate
GroupWhen inflation is higher than the interest rate
SaversThe real value of savings falls: $1,000 earning 3% grows to $1,030, but with 5% inflation it buys less than before (real rate ≈ −2%)
LendersAre repaid in money that is worth less. They lose unless the interest rate rises with inflation
BorrowersRepay in money that is worth less, so the real value of the debt falls. They gain, particularly with fixed-rate loans

Consequences for other groups

GroupPossible consequences of inflation
ConsumersPurchasing power falls; people on fixed incomes (pensioners) lose most; may buy now before prices rise further
WorkersReal wages fall if pay does not keep up; pressure for higher pay, possible strikes; uncertainty
Producers and firmsHigher costs and menu costs (changing prices); uncertainty reduces investment; exports less competitive if inflation is higher than rivals; but may gain if prices rise faster than costs
The economyFalling international competitiveness and a worse current account; lower growth if uncertainty cuts investment; redistribution from savers to borrowers; very high inflation can destroy confidence in money

Advantages of low inflation

  • Purchasing power is not eroded much and wages may keep pace.
  • Greater certainty encourages investment and so growth and jobs.
  • Exports stay price competitive, helping the current account.
  • Savings keep their value.

Consequences of deflation

  • Spending may be delayed as people wait for lower prices, so demand and output fall.
  • Firms' profits fall and they may cut jobs: unemployment rises, and a recession may follow.
  • The real value of debt rises, hurting borrowers.
  • But: if it is caused by lower costs or better technology, output can rise, and exports may become more competitive.

Savers in the 1970s

Inflation was above interest rates in several countries in the 1970s, so savers lost purchasing power while borrowers, including home buyers, gained.

Exam use: Real interest rates and redistribution.

Wage demands in 2022-23

As prices rose quickly, workers in many sectors went on strike for higher pay to protect their real wages.

Exam use: Consequences for workers and the wage-price spiral.

Japan's lost decades

Prolonged deflation in Japan made consumers delay purchases and raised the real burden of debt, contributing to weak growth.

Exam use: Consequences of deflation.

Venezuela's hyperinflation

Inflation of many thousand per cent destroyed savings and made it hard for firms to plan, and many people left the country.

Exam use: Extreme consequences for savers, firms and the economy.

Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain how inflation could affect savers and borrowers.
ReminderReal interest rate: if inflation above interest rate, savers lose and borrowers gain.
If inflation is higher than the interest rate, the real interest rate is negative. Savers lose because the money in their accounts buys less than before. Borrowers gain because they repay loans in money that is worth less, so the real value of their debt falls. For example, a saver with 3% interest and 5% inflation loses about 2% of purchasing power.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain two consequences of deflation. (May/June 2020, P21)
ReminderReduced demand, unemployment, recession, real debt; or lower costs and exports.
Output may fall. If prices fall because of lower demand, firms cut production as sales and profits fall. Unemployment may rise. With less output, firms need fewer workers, so jobs could be lost and a recession may follow.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse the advantages of a low rate of inflation. (May/June 2021, P21)
ReminderPurchasing power, certainty and investment, competitiveness, savings. Credit employment once.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Low inflation means prices are rising only slowly. Purchasing power is not eroded much, so wages may keep pace with prices and savings keep their value. There is more certainty, so firms are more willing to invest, which could raise output and employment. The country's goods could be more price competitive than those of countries with higher inflation, so exports could rise and imports fall, improving the current account.
Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain two reasons why a low inflation rate may increase a country's economic growth rate. (Oct/Nov 2023, P22)
ReminderCertainty and investment; competitiveness and exports; spending power.
A low inflation rate creates certainty. Firms can plan and are more likely to invest, which increases total demand and productive capacity, raising growth. It also helps price competitiveness. If prices rise more slowly than abroad, exports are cheaper and may rise, raising AD and growth.
Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not inflation will harm a country's firms. (Feb/Mar 2025, P22)
ReminderBoth sides. Harm: higher costs, uncertainty, menu costs, exports less competitive, wage demands. Not harm: prices may rise faster than costs, debts eased, low inflation helps. End with 'depends on the rate'.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. It could harm firms by raising their costs of production, such as wages and raw materials, which may reduce profits if they cannot pass them on. Uncertainty may reduce investment, firms face menu costs of changing prices, and if inflation is higher than competitors' abroad their exports could become less competitive. These effects are larger the higher and more unpredictable inflation is. However, firms may gain if their prices rise faster than their costs, and borrowing firms see the real value of their debts fall. Low, stable inflation can give firms the confidence to invest and may raise demand. Overall, high inflation is likely to harm most firms, particularly exporters, but low and stable inflation is unlikely to do so. It depends on the rate, whether it is expected, and whether firms can raise their prices.
Q6 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not inflation is always a disadvantage to an economy. (May/June 2022, P23)
Reminder'Always' invites 'not always'. Disadvantages and when it might be helpful (borrowers, growth, low and stable).
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. It can be a disadvantage because it reduces the purchasing power of savers and people on fixed incomes, raises firms' costs, creates uncertainty and can make exports less competitive. High inflation could redistribute income unfairly and reduce growth. However, it is not always a disadvantage. Low, stable inflation can accompany growth and falling unemployment, borrowers gain as the real value of their debt falls, and firms may see profits rise if prices rise faster than costs. Overall, high or unpredictable inflation is mostly harmful, but low inflation (around 2%) is not always a problem and may be a sign of a healthy economy.
Q7 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not deflation will benefit an economy. (Oct/Nov 2020, P23)
ReminderFor: cheaper goods, lower costs, exports competitive. Against: delayed spending, falling profits, unemployment, real debt, recession.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Deflation is a sustained fall in the general price level. It could benefit consumers by raising the purchasing power of their money, and if it is caused by lower costs or better technology, firms could produce more cheaply and exports could become more competitive. However, deflation caused by weak demand is likely to harm the economy, as consumers delay spending, firms' profits fall, and jobs are cut. The real value of debts rises, which hurts borrowers and could cause defaults. Overall, deflation is more likely to harm an economy, especially if it is caused by falling demand, although a mild fall caused by productivity gains could be beneficial.

Top tips

  • Real interest rate = nominal − inflation. Savers lose when it is negative; borrowers gain.
  • Always say who gains and who loses. Savers, lenders, borrowers, workers, firms, exporters.
  • Link inflation to competitiveness. Higher than rivals' inflation hurts exports.
  • Say whether inflation is high, low, expected. Effects depend on the level and predictability.
  • Deflation can be harmful. Delayed spending, real debt, falling profits.
