Macroeconomic Objectives, the Budget and the National Debt

5.1 and 5.2.1 to 5.2.3: macroeconomic policy objectives, the government budget, budget deficits and surpluses, and the national debt. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Governments use macroeconomic policy to pursue objectives such as price stability (a low, stable rate of inflation), low unemployment and economic growth, and AS Level does not require knowledge of policy conflicts and trade-offs. The government budget is the annual statement of the government's revenue (mainly taxes) and expenditure; if spending exceeds revenue there is a budget deficit, if revenue exceeds spending a budget surplus, and if they are equal a balanced budget. The national debt is the total stock of money the government owes, built up from past deficits, while the deficit is a flow within a single year. A large debt has interest costs and an opportunity cost, but it matters most relative to the size of the economy (the debt to GDP ratio) and who owns it.

1 · Macroeconomic objectives (5.1.1)

ObjectiveWhat it meansTypical measure
Price stabilityAvoiding high inflation and deflation, often a low target such as 2%Consumer price index
Low unemploymentKeeping as many people as possible in workUnemployment rate
Economic growthRaising real output over timeAnnual change in real GDP
  • The government can use fiscal policy (taxes and spending), monetary policy (interest rates, money supply, credit rules) and supply-side policy to pursue them.
  • Policies may be expansionary (to raise AD and output) or contractionary (to reduce AD and inflation).

2 · The government budget (5.2.1 to 5.2.2)

  • Revenue: income tax, corporation tax, sales taxes (VAT), duties, charges and sales of assets. Expenditure: current spending (wages of public workers, benefits) and capital spending (roads, hospitals).
  • Budget deficit = spending − revenue when spending is greater; the government borrows the difference.
  • Budget surplus = revenue − spending when revenue is greater; it can be used to repay debt.
  • Balanced budget: revenue equals spending.
  • A deficit tends to increase AD (expansionary); a surplus tends to reduce it (contractionary). Deficits rise in recessions because tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises.

3 · The national debt (5.2.3)

FlowStock
Budget deficit: borrowing in one yearNational debt: all outstanding government borrowing
Deficits add to the debtSurpluses reduce the debt
  • Significance of a large debt: interest payments take money from other spending (opportunity cost); a burden on future taxpayers; possible crowding out of private investment if the government borrows heavily; lenders may lose confidence and demand higher interest rates.
  • Why it may be less serious: if the debt is owed to domestic residents it is a transfer within the country; the debt may have financed productive investment; what matters is the debt relative to GDP and whether growth is faster than the interest rate.
  • The debt can be reduced by running surpluses, raising taxes, cutting spending, or growing the economy.

5 · Financing a deficit and the burden of debt

  • How governments borrow: by selling bonds to domestic savers, banks and pension funds, or to foreign investors. If the central bank creates money to buy the bonds, it risks inflation.
  • Crowding out: heavy government borrowing can push up interest rates, making it dearer for firms to borrow, so private investment may fall.
  • Owed to residents or to foreigners: debt held by residents is partly a transfer within the country; debt held by foreigners means that interest payments leave the country.
  • Financing productive investment: borrowing to build assets that raise future output may pay for itself; borrowing to pay for current spending leaves a burden on future taxpayers.
  • Confidence: if lenders doubt that the debt can be repaid they demand higher interest rates, which makes the problem worse.

PositionStrengthsWeaknesses

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A recession and the deficit

When the economy shrinks, fewer people pay income tax and VAT, and more claim benefits. The budget moves into deficit even if policy has not changed, which helps support demand.

Exam use: why deficits rise in recessions.

Paying interest on the debt

A government with a large debt spends a big share of revenue on interest. That money cannot be used for schools or hospitals, so there is an opportunity cost.

Exam use: the opportunity cost of the national debt.

Debt and GDP

A debt of $2,000bn sounds large, but in an economy with GDP of $4,000bn it is 50% of GDP. A country with the same debt and GDP of $1,000bn has a ratio of 200%, which is much harder to manage.

Exam use: why the ratio matters more than the size.

Borrowing to invest

A government borrows to build railways and broadband. The debt rises, but if the investment raises productivity and growth, future tax revenue may rise enough to pay for it.

Exam use: debt that finances productive spending.

A recession widens the deficit

When firms cut jobs, income tax and VAT receipts fall while spending on unemployment benefits rises. The budget deficit grows even though the government has not changed its policies.

Exam use: automatic stabilisers and a cyclical deficit.

Interest on the national debt

A government with large debt spends a growing share of its revenue on interest payments. This reduces what is available for schools and hospitals, which is the opportunity cost of the debt.

Exam use: the significance of the national debt.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q5(a) 8 marks Explain the difference between a budget surplus and a budget deficit and consider the extent to which a budget surplus is better than a budget deficit.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): understanding of a government budget (1), a budget surplus (1) and a budget deficit (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): the benefits and limitations of a budget surplus (up to 2) and of a budget deficit (up to 2). If only a surplus or only a deficit is considered the maximum is 2, with no evaluation marks. The question is not about the current account of the balance of payments.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a valid judgement on the extent to which a surplus is better than a deficit (1) and a conclusion (1).
PlanBudget: revenue and spending. Surplus: revenue greater. Deficit: spending greater. Weigh benefits and limits of each. Judge: depends on the economy.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The government budget shows planned revenue and spending. A budget surplus is when revenue exceeds spending, and a budget deficit is when spending exceeds revenue, which has to be financed by borrowing.

A surplus allows the government to repay debt and reduces interest payments, but it takes demand out of the economy and may mean cuts to services or higher taxes. A deficit can boost AD and support output and jobs, especially in a recession, and can finance investment, but it adds to the national debt and interest costs, and may crowd out private investment.

A surplus is not always better. In a boom a surplus can help control inflation and reduce debt, but in a recession a deficit is probably more appropriate to support demand. The right position depends on the stage of the economic cycle.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/21 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess whether a government should always aim to balance its budget rather than have a budget surplus or a budget deficit.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the arguments for balancing the budget, such as avoiding debt and interest costs, fairness to future taxpayers and keeping confidence of lenders (up to 4); the arguments for a deficit or surplus, such as supporting AD and growth in a recession with a deficit, investing in infrastructure, or repaying debt and reducing inflationary pressure with a surplus (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether a balanced budget should always be the aim, taking account of the stage of the economic cycle, the level of debt and the purpose of spending, with a conclusion.
PlanFor balance: debt, interest, future taxpayers. Against: deficit in recession, borrowing to invest; surplus in boom. Judge: depends on the cycle and debt level.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A balanced budget avoids adding to the national debt, so there are no growing interest payments, future taxpayers are not burdened and lenders stay confident. But always balancing the budget would mean cutting spending or raising taxes in a recession, when revenue falls, which would reduce AD and deepen the downturn. A deficit used to invest in infrastructure can raise future growth, while a surplus in a boom can control inflation and repay debt.

A government should not always aim for balance. A deficit is justified in a recession or to fund productive investment, provided the debt is sustainable relative to GDP, while a surplus is more suitable in a boom. Over the cycle the budget may roughly balance.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/22 · Q5(b) 12 marks Assess whether the achievement of a balanced budget should always be a main government macroeconomic objective.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the question asks whether a balanced budget should always be a main objective rather than the main objective, so analysis of other objectives is not essential; a direct comparison of the strengths, weaknesses and uses of a balanced budget with a budget in surplus and in deficit may reach Level 3; a balanced budget may bring stability and encourage domestic and inward investment but may not be flexible enough to deal with unemployment and inflation; a deficit may enable growth or reduce unemployment but may be unsustainable in the long run; a surplus may be needed to reduce inflation but has an opportunity cost in lost government spending.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether a balanced budget should always be a main objective, with a conclusion.
PlanBalanced: stability, confidence, but inflexible. Deficit: growth, jobs, but debt. Surplus: lower inflation, but lost spending. Judge: balance over the cycle.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A balanced budget gives stability and may encourage domestic and foreign investors, since it does not add to debt. But it is inflexible: in a recession the government may need a deficit to raise AD and reduce unemployment, while in a boom a surplus may be needed to control inflation. A deficit can support growth but may be unsustainable, with rising interest costs and crowding out, and a surplus involves the opportunity cost of lower spending.

A balanced budget should not always be a main objective. It is better seen as a long-run aim, with deficits in recessions and surpluses in booms, than a goal in every year, since the right budget position depends on the state of the economy and the level of existing debt.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/22 · Q1(c)(i) 2 marks Distinguish between a budget deficit and a current account deficit.
What the mark scheme wants
  • A budget deficit means that government expenditure exceeds government revenue (1).
  • A current account deficit means that exports, or inflows, are less than imports, or outflows, of current account items such as goods and services (1).
PlanBudget deficit: government spending above revenue. Current account deficit: payments abroad above receipts.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A budget deficit is where the government spends more than it receives in revenue in a year. A current account deficit is where a country's payments to the rest of the world for goods, services and income are greater than the receipts it gets from abroad.

Top tips

  • Know the three objectives. Price stability, low unemployment, growth.
  • Deficit is a flow; debt is a stock. Deficits add to the debt.
  • Define the budget. Planned revenue and spending.
  • Surplus is not always better. It depends on the economic cycle.
  • Mention the debt ratio. Debt relative to GDP.
  • Say what deficits do to AD. A deficit is expansionary; a surplus is contractionary.
  • Do not mix with the current account. Budget deficit ≠ trade deficit.
