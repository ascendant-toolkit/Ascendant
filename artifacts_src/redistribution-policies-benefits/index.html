Redistribution Policies, Benefits and Trade-offs

Fiscal and supply-side policies to reduce inequality, transfer payments and the poverty trap, and the trade-offs between equity and efficiency. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Governments reduce inequality with fiscal policy (progressive taxes and transfer payments such as means-tested benefits, pensions and free public services) and with supply-side and labour market policies (education, training, a minimum wage) that raise low earners' incomes. These policies involve trade-offs: very high taxes may weaken incentives, benefits that are withdrawn as earnings rise can create a poverty trap, redistribution costs money and can be avoided, and some policies take years to work. Evaluation weighs equity against efficiency and asks how large each effect is.

1 · Benefits and policies to redistribute

Fiscal policy

  • Progressive income tax, higher taxes on wealth and inheritance
  • Transfer payments: unemployment, disability and child benefits, pensions (money paid with no good or service produced in return)
  • Means-tested benefits (targeted at low incomes) or universal benefits (paid to everyone)
  • Spending on health, education and housing

Supply-side and labour market policies

  • Education, training and apprenticeships, to raise skills and earnings
  • Improving infrastructure in poorer regions, to create jobs
  • A national minimum wage (see syllabus 8.3)
  • Equal opportunity and anti-discrimination laws, a role for trade unions

2 · Trade-offs and evaluation

  • Incentives: very high taxes could reduce the incentive to work, save and take risks, which could lower growth and, in time, tax revenue. High benefits could reduce the incentive to look for work.
  • The poverty trap: when means-tested benefits are withdrawn as earnings rise, and tax is paid too, a worker may gain little or nothing from earning more (a very high effective marginal tax rate).
  • Equity versus efficiency: redistribution is most appropriate when equality is valued more highly than efficiency. Others argue that rich people have a lower marginal utility of income, which justifies redistribution, though comparing utility between people is hard.
  • Cost and fiscal limits: benefits and services must be paid for, and tax avoidance and capital flight limit what a tax can raise.
  • Time lags: education and training take many years to change the distribution.
  • Growth may reduce poverty without reducing inequality. The Kuznets curve suggests inequality may first rise then fall as a country develops, though evidence is mixed.

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Means-tested and universal benefits

A means-tested benefit goes only to those below an income level, which targets help but can create a poverty trap. A universal benefit, such as a state pension or child benefit paid to all, is simpler and avoids stigma but costs more.

Exam use: compare equity, cost and incentives. A 2020 Paper 3 question asked which combination redistributes most.

A free school meal or a scholarship

Education and nutrition raise the future earning power of children from low-income families, which is a supply-side route to equality of opportunity. The effect takes years to appear and depends on the quality of schooling.

Exam use: the supply-side example asked for in the May/June 2024 8-mark question. Link to long-term earnings and to the time lag.

An inheritance tax

An inheritance tax charges the wealth passed on at death, often with an exempt amount. It aims to limit the build-up of wealth across generations, but wealthy families may find ways to avoid it, and it may weaken the incentive to save.

Exam use: a tax on wealth. Evaluate with avoidance and incentives.

May/June 2024 · 9708/41 and 43 · Q1(d) 8 marks Use one example of a supply-side policy and one example of a fiscal policy to assess how a government might be able to achieve greater equality in the distribution of income.
What the mark scheme wants
  • For each policy: a correct example (1), development (2), and a link to equality (1).
  • Fiscal: for example progressive income tax or transfer payments. Supply-side: for example education and training, or infrastructure in poorer regions.
KANE

A fiscal policy example is a more progressive income tax. Higher tax rates on high incomes, with a tax-free allowance for low incomes, mean that richer households pay a larger share of their income, and the extra revenue can be paid to poorer households as transfer payments or spent on public services. This would reduce the gap between the highest and lowest disposable incomes and move the Lorenz curve closer to the line of equality. However, very high rates may reduce the incentive to work and encourage tax avoidance, so the effect could be smaller than expected.

A supply-side policy example is increased spending on education and training, particularly for girls and for poorer regions. Better skills raise the productivity and earnings of those who would otherwise be low paid, which narrows the distribution of earnings and raises growth. The article suggests that unequal access to education between females and males is an obstacle to a more equal distribution of income. The effect is likely to take many years to appear and depends on the quality of the schools and whether jobs are available.

Overall, a government might use both: fiscal policy can reduce inequality quickly, while the supply-side policy may be more lasting but slow.

Top tips

  • Use the data carefully. Quote figures from the table, then say what cannot be concluded (correlation is not causation, small sample).
  • For policies, name a fiscal example and a supply-side example, then link each to the distribution of income, with development.
  • Evaluate with incentives, the poverty trap, avoidance, cost and time lags. Say which of equity or efficiency the government values more.
