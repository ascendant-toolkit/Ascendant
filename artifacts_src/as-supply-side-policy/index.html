Supply-Side Policy: Meaning, Objectives and Tools

5.4.1 to 5.4.3: what supply-side policy is, its objectives, and the interventionist and market-based tools. Animated chains, labs, drills and past paper questions.

This part in five sentences

Supply-side policy consists of government measures designed to increase aggregate supply by raising the quantity and quality of the factors of production and the efficiency with which they are used, so that potential output (the LRAS curve) shifts to the right. Its objectives are to raise productivity and productive capacity, which supports economic growth, lowers structural unemployment and inflationary pressure, and improves international competitiveness. The tools fall into two broad groups: interventionist measures, in which the government spends to improve the supply side (education, training, health, infrastructure, support for research), and market-based measures, which aim to increase incentives and competition (lower taxes, deregulation, privatisation, labour market reform, free trade zones). Supply-side policy differs from demand-side policy (fiscal and monetary), which changes spending rather than capacity. Its effects are usually long term, costly and uncertain, and the effects on AD and AS are covered on the next page.

1 · Meaning and objectives

  • Meaning: policies that shift the LRAS curve to the right by raising the economy's productive capacity and productivity. They are also called aggregate supply policies.
  • Productivity: output per unit of input, such as output per worker.
  • Productive capacity (potential output): the maximum the economy can produce using all its resources efficiently.
  • Objectives: higher long-run economic growth; lower structural and long-term unemployment; lower inflation (costs fall, capacity rises); more competitive firms and a stronger current account; more enterprise and innovation.

5 · Which factor of production is affected?

  • Labour: quantity (tax and benefit incentives, immigration policy, retirement age) and quality (education, training, health).
  • Capital: infrastructure, investment allowances, research support, technology.
  • Enterprise: lower business taxes, start-up grants, deregulation, training in management skills.
  • Land and natural resources: planning and property rules, environmental policy, resource management.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Apprenticeships and training

A government funds training programmes so workers gain skills that employers need. Productivity rises and structural unemployment falls, shifting LRAS right, but the benefits take years to appear.

Exam use: human capital as a supply-side tool.

Broadband and transport links

New rail and fibre networks lower firms' costs and open new markets. Capital spending adds to AD at once and to productive capacity over the long run.

Exam use: infrastructure raising AS.

Cutting red tape

Simplifying the rules for starting a business makes it quicker and cheaper to set up firms, encouraging enterprise and competition. But less regulation might reduce consumer protection or environmental standards.

Exam use: deregulation and its drawbacks.

Privatisation

Selling a state-owned industry to private owners gives managers a profit motive and exposes the industry to competition, which may cut costs. It can also create a private monopoly that raises prices.

Exam use: market-based supply-side policy and its risks.

Free trade zones

A region offers firms tax incentives and fewer controls to attract investment. Costs fall and bureaucracy is reduced, but some activity may just move from elsewhere in the country.

Exam use: free trade zones as a supply-side policy.

Tax cuts to encourage work

A government cuts the income tax rate on earnings to make work more rewarding. More people may enter the labour force, but if they simply spend the extra income the effect is on demand rather than supply.

Exam use: the incentive effect and its limits.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/22 · Q1(c) 3 marks Identify one policy that could be considered a supply-side measure and explain how this policy could make a country's businesses more competitive.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Identification of a valid supply-side measure that could make businesses more competitive (1 mark), for example cutting business taxes, cutting income taxes or improving infrastructure; it must be explained as a supply-side tool, not as a demand-side one.
  • An explanation of how this makes businesses more competitive (up to 2 marks), the third mark referring specifically to being more competitive: for example, reducing business taxes (1) gives more money to invest in new technology (1), which improves quality or allows lower prices, making the business more competitive (1).
PlanPolicy, then how: lower business tax, more investment, better quality or lower prices, more competitive.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

One supply-side policy is a cut in business taxes. Firms keep more of their profits, so they have more money to invest in new technology. This raises the quality of their goods and can allow them to cut prices, which makes them more competitive against foreign rivals.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/21 · Q1(d)(i) 2 marks Explain why 'free trade zones' can be considered a supply-side policy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Up to 2 marks for an explanation of free trade zones as a supply-side policy: for example, generous tax incentives leave firms with more funds to finance development and expansion; they help to lower costs, remove bureaucracy and make the economy more flexible and efficient; and they promote the role of the private sector in raising productivity.
PlanTax incentives, lower costs, less red tape: raises productivity and capacity.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Free trade zones give firms tax incentives and fewer controls, which lowers their costs and removes bureaucracy. This encourages investment and raises productivity and productive capacity, so they are a supply-side policy.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/21 · Q3(b) 12 marks Discuss whether supply-side policies will be successful in increasing the quantity and quality of the factor enterprise in a modern economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the potential advantages of supply-side policies in increasing the quantity and quality of enterprise (up to 4), such as lower taxes encouraging entrepreneurs, expenditure on education and training improving quality, deregulation making it easier to set up, and financial support so that firms survive; and the potential limitations (up to 4), such as long delays, dependence on government support and no guarantee of success.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether the policies will be successful, with a conclusion (1 mark reserved).
PlanTax cuts, training, deregulation, start-up support raise enterprise. Limits: slow, dependence, no guarantee. Judge: partly successful.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Lower taxes on profits raise the reward for taking risks, so more people may become entrepreneurs, and training in management and innovation could improve their quality. Deregulation makes it easier and cheaper to set up a business, and financial support helps new firms survive. But the effects take a long time, some entrepreneurs may become dependent on government support and inefficient, and policies cannot guarantee that firms will succeed.

Supply-side policies are likely to be partly successful, since they improve the environment for enterprise, but the outcome depends on culture, access to finance and the state of demand. They are more likely to increase the quantity than guarantee the quality.

Based on Feb/March 2021 · 9708/22 · Q2(b) 12 marks Discuss the view that supply-side policies to increase the supply of labour to an economy are likely to be more successful than those designed to increase the supply of entrepreneurship.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): at least two supply-side measures designed to increase the supply of labour (up to 4; a maximum of 3 if only one), and at least two designed to increase the supply of entrepreneurship, such as training in management skills, lower business taxes, subsidies and start-up grants (up to 4; a maximum of 3 if only one).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): evaluative comment on which policies are likely to be successful (up to 3), leading to a reasoned conclusion (1).
PlanLabour: tax and benefit incentives, training. Enterprise: start-up grants, lower business tax, training. Judge which is more likely to succeed and why.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Policies to increase the supply of labour include cutting income tax and benefits to raise the reward for work, and training to raise skills, which can have a fairly direct effect on the labour force. Policies for entrepreneurship include start-up grants, lower business taxes and training in management skills, but success depends on attitudes to risk, access to finance and market conditions, which policy cannot easily change.

Labour supply policies are probably more reliably successful because incentives and training affect a large number of people directly, while entrepreneurship is harder to influence. But enterprise policies could have a bigger effect on growth if they succeed, so the answer depends on the economy.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/22 · Q3(b) 12 marks Discuss the difficulties that might be encountered when adopting supply-side policies to expand the productive capacity of an economy and consider whether these difficulties can be overcome.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): how supply-side policies may expand productive capacity and the difficulties of adopting them (up to 4; a maximum of 3 if only one policy and one difficulty are discussed), such as cost and the long time before they take effect; possible methods of overcoming these difficulties (up to 4; a maximum of 3 if only one method is discussed), for example inward investment where funds are short in developing economies.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): the likely success of adopting supply-side policies, taking into account the difficulties (up to 3), with a reasoned conclusion (1).
PlanDifficulties: cost, time lags, uncertainty, resistance. Overcoming: inward investment, private sector, phasing, targeting. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Supply-side policies such as spending on education and infrastructure expand capacity, but they are costly, which is a major problem for countries with limited tax revenue, and they take many years to work. Workers may not respond to training, and some policies, such as privatisation, can face political opposition. Costs may be overcome by inward investment from abroad, partnerships with private firms or phasing spending over time, and delays by targeting the policies where they have the biggest effect.

The difficulties can be partly overcome, but not fully, since long time lags are unavoidable. Success is likely to depend on the country's resources and on how well the policies are chosen and managed.

Top tips

  • Define it. Policies to increase aggregate supply, shifting LRAS right.
  • Name the tool and the factor. Which factor of production, and why productivity rises.
  • Classify. Market-based or interventionist.
  • Say the aim of a tax cut. Incentive (supply-side) or spending (demand-side).
  • Give a limitation for each. Cost, time, uncertainty, side effects.
  • Link to objectives. Growth, structural unemployment, inflation, competitiveness.
  • Be specific. Name the policy and say how it works.
