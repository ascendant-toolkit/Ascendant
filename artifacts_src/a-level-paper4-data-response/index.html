Paper 4 Section A: Data Response Technique

How to score on the 20 mark data response question: the structure, what each part asks, using the data, evaluating in short parts and three full reworded Section A questions. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and practice.

This part in four sentences

Section A of Paper 4 is one compulsory data response question worth 20 marks, split into four parts, and it is based on a short passage plus a table or figure. The parts get harder and bigger: in the 40 papers we studied the last part was worth 7 or 8 marks in 34 of them, and the first part was usually only 2 to 4. Most marks are awarded one per point, so you gain by being precise, using the source and developing each point briefly, not by writing essays on the small parts. The last part usually needs evaluation (a judgement), and the passage is there to be used.

2 · How the 20 marks are split

We counted the marks for the four parts in all 40 Paper 4 papers (2020 to 2025) in our folder. The most common patterns are 2, 4, 6, 8 (six papers), 4, 4, 4, 8 (four), 2, 5, 6, 7 (four) and 3, 3, 6, 8 (four). The largest part is always last.

Spending the marks on the last part

An 8 mark part often follows one of two shapes. Two items each worth 4 (example, development, link) as in "use one fiscal and one supply-side policy". Or four for support and four against, as in "to what extent do you agree with the advice". Read the stem to see which, then give each side its share and end with a judgement.

Data skills worth marks

SkillWhat to do
Quote accuratelyGive the number, the unit and the year: "GDP per head at PPP rose from $9,000 to $11,500 between 2015 and 2020". Quoting alone often earns only part of the data marks.
Calculate a changePercentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. A rise from 9,000 to 11,500 is 27.8%.
Percentage points are not percentagesUnemployment from 6% to 5% is a fall of 1 percentage point (and 16.7% in relative terms).
Compare, do not just list"Country X's rose faster than Y's" and then say what it means.
Do not confuse correlation and causeA pattern across a few countries is not proof of cause; say what else could explain it.
State what the data cannot showFew countries, one year, averages that hide inequality, no PPP, no information on relative poverty or on the environment.
Read figuresDescribe direction, size and turning points: "rose until 2011 then stabilised". Compare lines by gap and by trend.

Evaluation inside a short part

You do not write a mini-essay. In a 6 to 8 mark part, one or two well-aimed evaluative sentences earn the marks. Use one of these moves after you have made a point.

MoveWhat it addsSentence starter
Depends on sizeThe effect is bigger or smaller depending on elasticity, scale or share"This depends on how elastic..."
Short run versus long runThe effect changes with time"In the short run this may..., but over time..."
Other factorsSomething else could explain or offset the result"However, other factors such as... may..."
Who gains and losesDifferent groups are affected differently"This could benefit... but harm..."
Evidence limitsThe data in the passage cannot prove the claim"The data cover only five countries, so..."
JudgementA final reasoned view that answers the question"Overall, ... is probably..., because..."

Using the passage in the last part

  • Quote or paraphrase a line from the passage to support each point, for example "the article says the IMF encouraged palm oil exports to repay debt".
  • If the stem says "with reference to the article" or "using the information", answers that ignore the passage lose marks.
  • Link the passage to a theory: the quotation is application; the economics is analysis; the judgement is evaluation.

Diagrams in short parts

  • A 3 to 4 mark part that says "with the help of a diagram" usually gives about half the marks for the diagram: correct axes, curves labelled, a clear shift and the new and old equilibria.
  • Draw it small but complete, then write one or two sentences to explain it. A diagram with no explanation, or an explanation with no diagram, loses marks.
  • Common short-part diagrams: a PPC for potential versus actual growth, supply and demand for an externality or a tax, AD and AS for a shock, a Lorenz curve, the money market, a tariff diagram.
Good habitsAnswer the parts in order, but if you are stuck on (c) or (d) leave a gap and return. Never leave an identify or define part blank: they are the easiest marks.

Three Section A questions in full, reworded

These follow real Section A questions from 2023 to 2025. The data and wording are our own, so they are safe to set as practice. Read each part, decide the command word and marks, and then open the mark scheme points and the model answer.

A · Income inequality and poverty (modelled on May/June 2024 · 9708/41 · Question 1)

Passage (summary): Income inequality has fallen in some low-income countries. The Gini coefficient is the most widely used measure of inequality, but it ignores wealth and the informal economy. Governments could use fiscal and supply-side policies to make the distribution of income more equal. Table: Country A: Gini 0.52, ratio of female to male literacy 1.00, 5% of people below the poverty line. Country B: 0.41, 0.97, 8%. Country C: 0.38, 0.93, 12%. Country D: 0.34, 0.88, 22%. Country E: 0.29, 0.70, 31%.

Part (a) · Describe 3 marks Describe what is meant by the Gini coefficient and its possible range of values.
What the mark scheme wants
  • A measure of income inequality (or less often wealth) among a population (1).
  • It ranges from 0 to 1, or 0% to 100% (1).
  • 0 means perfect equality and 1 means perfect inequality (1).
PlanWhat it measures. The range. What 0 and 1 mean.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The Gini coefficient measures the inequality of income, or sometimes wealth, in a population. It ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 is perfect equality and 1 is perfect inequality, with one person having everything.

Part (b) · Distinguish 3 marks Distinguish between absolute poverty and relative poverty.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Absolute poverty: income too low to buy what is needed to sustain life, often shown as a fixed line such as the World Bank's international poverty line.
  • Relative poverty: income low compared with that of other groups in the same society.
  • Credit for the difference, not just two definitions.
PlanAbsolute against a fixed line. Relative against others.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Absolute poverty is having an income below the amount needed to meet basic needs, usually measured against a fixed line. Relative poverty is having an income that is low compared with others in the same society, so it can exist in rich countries.

Part (c) · Consider with reference to the table 6 marks Consider whether the table supports the conclusion that greater inequality is linked to lower female to male literacy and leads to greater poverty.
What the mark scheme wants
  • No apparent link to poor literacy: countries with higher Gini coefficients have better literacy ratios.
  • Higher Gini coefficients are found where fewer people are below the poverty line, so no link to absolute poverty.
  • Cause and effect cannot be determined; nothing about relative poverty.
  • Up to 2 marks for correct use of the data.
PlanQuote A and E. Say the pattern is opposite. Say what is missing.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The table does not support the conclusion. Country A has the highest Gini (0.52) but the highest literacy ratio (1.00) and the lowest share in poverty (5%), whereas country E has the lowest Gini (0.29) but a ratio of only 0.70 and 31% in poverty.

In any case, five countries cannot show cause. The table says nothing about relative poverty, and income levels or education policy could explain the pattern.

Part (d) · Assess 8 marks Use one supply-side policy and one fiscal policy to assess how a government could make the distribution of income more equal.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Fiscal: correct example (1), development (2), link to equality (1).
  • Supply-side: correct example (1), development (2), link to equality (1).
PlanFiscal: progressive tax and transfers. Supply-side: education. Develop each and link to the Gini.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A fiscal policy is a more progressive income tax with higher benefits. This takes a larger share of high incomes and raises the incomes of low earners, so it could reduce the Gini coefficient quickly, although high marginal rates may reduce incentives.

A supply-side policy is spending on education and training. This may raise the productivity and wages of low-paid workers, so incomes become more equal over time, although the effect takes years.

B · Population change (modelled on Feb/March 2025 · 9708/42 · Question 1)

Passage (summary): For decades a country's economy was built on a large supply of low-cost labour. Its birth rate has fallen sharply, the share of people above retirement age has risen, and annual population growth has slowed each decade, although the total was still rising in the latest year. Some economists warn of lower output and a pension burden; the government disagrees and has introduced policies to raise the birth rate.

Part (a) · Explain 2 marks Explain what is meant by an optimum population.
What the mark scheme wants
  • The size of population which, with existing resources, factors of production and technology, gives the highest income per head (1).
  • Where the standard of living is at a maximum (1).
PlanHighest income per head, given resources, capital and technology.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

An optimum population is the size at which, given the economy's resources, capital and technology, income per head is highest, so the standard of living is at a maximum.

Part (b) · Explain and consider 5 marks Explain how the age structure changed between 1970 and 2020, and consider whether the claim that the population is falling is supported by data showing annual population growth was positive every year to 2020 but falling.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Falling birth rate (1); ageing population (1); rising dependency ratio (1).
  • The data show the population still growing, only more slowly (1), so the claim 'it is falling' is not supported (1).
PlanThree structure changes. Then the data: still positive, so not falling.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The birth rate fell, so the share of young people fell and the share of older people rose, giving an ageing population. As fewer workers support more retirees, the dependency ratio increased.

The data show that annual population growth was positive in every year to 2020, even though it slowed, so the total population was still rising. The claim that the population is falling is therefore not supported by this evidence.

Part (c) · Identify and analyse 6 marks Identify two observations economists have made about the changes in the population and analyse how each would affect GDP.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Fewer young people mean a smaller pool of workers, innovators and entrepreneurs, lowering output and productivity.
  • More older people raise spending on pensions and care, which may raise the budget deficit and reduce investment, lowering GDP.
PlanObservation, link, GDP effect, twice.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

One observation is that there are fewer young people. This means a smaller pool of future workers and entrepreneurs, so labour force growth and productivity may slow and GDP could be lower.

A second is that the share of older people is rising. This may raise government spending on pensions and health care, which could widen the deficit or crowd out investment, reducing GDP.

Part (d) · Consider 7 marks The government says the economists' warnings are alarmist and has introduced policies to raise the birth rate. Consider the effects on the economy of these policies.
What the mark scheme wants
  • The earlier policy to cut births moved the economy towards a skills-based economy, but led to a more dependent population, lower savings and investment and lower long-term AS and AD.
  • The new policy aims to rebuild a young, skilled workforce supported by technology, which could raise competitiveness and long-run AS and AD.
PlanExplain the aim. Effects on workforce, AS and AD. Time lag and judgement.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The policy aims to raise the birth rate so that a larger young workforce grows up. In the long run, more young, skilled workers could raise potential output and aggregate supply, and support demand, making the economy more competitive.

However, the effect has a long time lag of about twenty years, and in the short run more children raise dependency and costs for families and the government. So the policy may help in the long run, but is unlikely to solve the problem quickly.

C · Palm oil, debt and the IMF (modelled on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/43 · Question 1)

Passage (summary): Palm oil production has quadrupled in twenty years and brings rural incomes and foreign exchange to producing countries. After a financial crisis, one producer faced external debt, and the IMF encouraged it to expand palm oil exports. A multinational company invested heavily in plantations. Clearing rainforest creates large external costs. Figure: European use of palm oil: biodiesel rising every year; heating and electricity rising and then levelling off after 2011; food and animal feed falling slowly.

Part (a) · Identify 3 marks Identify the trends in demand for the different uses of palm oil shown in the figure.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Biodiesel use rises throughout the period (1).
  • Heating and electricity use rises and then stabilises after 2011 (1).
  • Food and animal feed use falls over the period (1).
PlanOne trend per use: rising, rising then flat, falling.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Biodiesel use rises every year. Use for heating and electricity rises and then levels off after 2011. Use in food and animal feed declines over the period.

Part (b) · Analyse 4 marks Analyse why developing countries that produce palm oil see it as a way to reduce poverty.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Agriculture is linked to rural economies that suffer underemployment and low, seasonal incomes (1).
  • High world demand makes palm oil a source of income and foreign exchange (1) so rural incomes rise (1) and more people leave poverty (1).
  • More employment; multinationals' investment supports rural economies.
PlanDemand, income and jobs, foreign exchange, rural living standards.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

World demand for palm oil is high, so it earns income and foreign exchange. Production in rural areas creates jobs for people who were underemployed, and raises incomes and living standards, which could lift many households out of poverty. Investment by multinationals expands production and sustains these rural economies.

Part (c) · Explain 5 marks Explain how a proposed removal of a subsidy on biodiesel by a European parliament could affect palm oil producing countries.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Removing the subsidy raises biodiesel prices for consumers in Europe, which could lower demand and imports, and so the demand for palm oil (up to 2).
  • Adverse effects on the balance of payments (1), incomes (1) and employment (1) in producing countries.
  • Multinationals may cut investment if profits fall.
PlanSubsidy removal, higher price, lower demand for palm oil, then BoP, incomes, jobs.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Removing the subsidy raises the price of biodiesel in Europe. Demand for biodiesel and so for palm oil could fall, reducing export earnings for producing countries and worsening their current account. Lower export earnings may reduce incomes and employment in the sector, and multinationals might cut investment if profits fall.

Part (d) · To what extent 8 marks To what extent do you agree with the IMF's advice to expand palm oil production in order to deal with external debt?
What the mark scheme wants
  • In support (up to 4): the country had external debt problems, palm oil is a big earner of foreign exchange which could repay debt, and incomes could rise.
  • Against (up to 4): clearing rainforest destroys habitats and releases greenhouse gases, so external costs exceed private costs; dependence on one crop (monoculture) leaves earnings exposed to foreign buyers and price swings.
PlanFor: foreign exchange and incomes. Against: external costs and monoculture. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The IMF advice could help. Palm oil is a large earner of foreign currency, so expansion may reduce the current account deficit and provide funds to repay external debt, and raise rural incomes.

However, clearing rainforest creates external costs, including lost habitats and greenhouse gas emissions, which may exceed the private benefits. Dependence on one crop leaves the economy exposed to price falls. So the advice may help in the short run but is less sound for sustainable development.

Top tips

  • Read the mark and the command word first. Marks tell you how many points; the command word tells you what to do with them.
  • Match your length to the marks. Roughly one point per mark. A 2 mark part is one or two sentences, not a paragraph.
  • Use the source. Quote the figures or the passage in parts (c) and (d). The source is the application.
  • Show the calculation. Write the formula and the numbers. A wrong final answer can still earn method marks.
  • Judge in the last part. End with a reasoned conclusion that answers the question: 'to a large extent', 'only partly', 'depends on'.
  • Draw when asked. Labelled axes, curves and equilibria, then explain it in words.
  • Leave time for (d). It is the biggest part. Do not run out of time on the 2 and 4 mark parts.
