Changes in Consumer and Producer Surplus

2.5.3 to 2.5.4: causes of changes in consumer and producer surplus and how price elasticity of demand and supply affect the extent of the change. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Consumer and producer surplus change whenever the price or quantity traded changes: a shift of demand or supply, an indirect tax or subsidy, a maximum or minimum price, or a change in trade policy such as a tariff. In general, a fall in price raises consumer surplus and a rise lowers it, while a rise in price raises producer surplus and a fall lowers it. Price elasticity of demand and supply decide how big the changes are: for the same price change, flatter (elastic) curves mean a bigger change in quantity and so a bigger change in surplus; for the same shift of supply, steeper (inelastic) demand means a larger price change. Policies that move the market away from equilibrium can cause a welfare loss, where total surplus falls.

  • An indirect tax shifts supply to the left; the price consumers pay rises and CS falls. Part of the tax is borne by producers, whose PS also falls, and the government gains revenue.
  • A subsidy shifts supply to the right; consumers pay less, so CS rises, and producers receive the subsidy as well as the market price.
  • A price control that stops trade at the equilibrium creates a welfare loss (a deadweight loss): surplus on trades that no longer happen.

2 · The role of elasticity (2.5.4)

  • For the same supply shift to the left (a tax or cost rise): if demand is price inelastic (steep), price rises a lot and quantity falls a little; if elastic (flat), price rises a little and quantity falls a lot. The consumer surplus loss is usually larger for inelastic demand, as in the example shown, because consumers pay much more for most units.
  • For the same price fall: with elastic demand quantity rises a lot, so many extra consumers gain and CS rises more.
  • The more inelastic supply, the more of a tax is borne by producers and the more producer surplus falls.
  • Judgement: the extent of any change in surplus depends on the size of the price change as well as elasticity, and surplus cannot be measured exactly in real markets.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Taxing cigarettes

Smokers have inelastic demand, so a tax raises the price a lot and cuts quantity only a little. Consumer surplus falls sharply, producers lose less volume, and the government raises large revenue.

Exam use: inelastic demand, a large price rise and a big loss of consumer surplus.

A subsidy on vaccines

A subsidy shifts supply to the right, so vaccinations become cheaper and more people are vaccinated. Consumer surplus rises, and the gain is larger when demand is elastic because more extra people take the vaccine.

Exam use: a subsidy raising consumer surplus, and the role of elasticity.

Price cap on energy bills

A cap below the market price helps households that can buy at the lower price, but firms supply less. Producer surplus falls and some households cannot get all the energy they want, so total surplus falls.

Exam use: a maximum price causing a welfare loss.

A tariff on imported steel

A tariff raises the price of steel, so buyers lose consumer surplus. Domestic steel producers gain producer surplus, and the government gains revenue, but there is a welfare loss as less steel is traded.

Exam use: trade protection and the distribution of surplus.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/22 · Q3(a) 8 marks Explain what is meant by consumer surplus and use diagrams to assess the impact on consumer surplus when an indirect tax is imposed on a good with price-elastic demand compared with the impact when the demand is price inelastic.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): consumer surplus is the difference between the price a consumer is willing to pay and the price actually paid.
  • Application (up to 3): a diagram for price-elastic demand (flatter demand curve and a leftward shift of supply), the impact on price and quantity, and the fall in consumer surplus.
  • Application (up to 3): a diagram for price-inelastic demand (steeper demand curve), the impact on price and quantity, the impact on consumer surplus compared with the elastic case, and possible reasons for the difference such as substitutability or addiction.
PlanDefine CS. Two diagrams: tax shifts supply left. Elastic: smaller price rise, larger quantity fall. Inelastic: larger price rise. Compare CS losses.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Consumer surplus is the difference between the price consumers are willing to pay for a product and the market price actually paid, shown as the triangle below the demand curve and above the price.

An indirect tax shifts the supply curve to the left, raising the price and cutting quantity. If demand is price elastic (a flatter curve), consumers have many substitutes, so the price rises only a little and quantity falls a lot, and the fall in consumer surplus is smaller per unit sold. If demand is inelastic (a steeper curve), perhaps because the good is addictive or has few substitutes, the price rises by more and quantity falls little, so consumers lose a larger area of consumer surplus. Comparing the two diagrams, the inelastic case shows the bigger loss for the same tax.

Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/21 · Q3(a) 8 marks With the aid of diagrams, explain how consumer surplus is affected by a decrease in the price of a luxury product with many substitutes and of an essential product with few substitutes.
What the mark scheme wants

  • Application (up to 3): how consumer surplus is affected by a decrease in the price of a luxury product with many substitutes (elastic PED).
  • Application (up to 3): how consumer surplus is affected by a decrease in the price of an essential product with few substitutes (inelastic PED).
PlanDiagram with CS. A price fall raises CS as more consumers are willing to pay. Luxury: elastic, quantity rises a lot. Essential: inelastic.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

On a demand diagram with price and quantity axes, consumer surplus is the triangle below the demand curve and above the price.

A fall in price increases consumer surplus, because existing buyers pay less and additional buyers whose willingness to pay is above the new price enter the market. For a luxury product with many substitutes, demand is elastic, so the quantity bought rises sharply and many new consumers gain surplus. For an essential product with few substitutes, demand is inelastic, so the quantity rises only slightly, and the gain is mainly to existing buyers who pay less for the same quantity. The extent of the change therefore depends on price elasticity of demand.

Consumer surplus is the difference between what consumers are willing to pay and the price they actually pay.

A subsidy to vaccine producers lowers their costs, so supply shifts to the right. The equilibrium price falls and the quantity rises, so the consumer surplus triangle becomes larger: existing buyers pay less and new buyers join. The size of the change in price depends on the size of the subsidy and on the price elasticities of demand and supply, so these also determine how much consumer surplus rises.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/23 · Q2(b) 12 marks When the price of a product changes, it usually changes the consumer surplus in the market. Assess how variations in price elasticity of demand for a product determine the extent of changes in consumer surplus in a market.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge and analysis (up to 8): consumer surplus with an accurate labelled diagram; the effects on consumer surplus when price increases and decreases; the change for a product with price elastic demand and for one with inelastic demand; a comparison where PED varies. A one-sided answer is capped at Level 2.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): the change depends on the relative extent of the price change, and other factors alongside elasticity; a justified conclusion.
PlanDefine CS with a diagram. Price up/down. Elastic vs inelastic. Compare. Evaluate: size of price change, other factors.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Consumer surplus is the triangle between the demand curve and the price. If price rises, for example because of a tax, consumer surplus falls. With inelastic demand, quantity falls little, so most consumers continue to buy at the higher price and lose a large amount of surplus. With elastic demand, consumers switch away, so quantity falls more and the price rise is smaller, so the loss on each unit is smaller. If price falls, elastic demand brings in many new consumers, so the gain is larger than for inelastic demand for the same price fall.

Elasticity is not the only factor: the extent of the price change matters at least as much, and a large price change on an elastic product could cut surplus more than a small one on an inelastic product. Elasticity therefore determines how quantity responds, but the overall change in consumer surplus depends on both.

Top tips

  • Name the cause and the direction. Tax: supply left, price up, CS down.
  • Compare two diagrams. Elastic and inelastic demand side by side.
  • Say which loses more and why. For a tax, inelastic demand usually loses more CS: price rises more.
  • Mention extra consumers. A price fall brings new buyers: bigger gain when demand is elastic.
  • Producers matter too. Inelastic supply means producers bear more of a tax.
  • Price controls cause welfare loss. Trade stops below the equilibrium quantity.
  • Conclude with 'depends'. On elasticity and the size of the price change.
