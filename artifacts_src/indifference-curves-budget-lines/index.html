Indifference Curves and Budget Lines

Consumer choice without measuring utility. Indifference curves, budget lines, equilibrium, and the split of a price change into a substitution effect and an income effect: the tool that explains normal, inferior and Giffen goods. Animated diagrams built from real numbers, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

The chapter in four sentences

An indifference curve joins all the combinations of two goods that give a consumer the same satisfaction. A budget line shows all the combinations the consumer can afford. The consumer is in equilibrium where the budget line touches the highest indifference curve it can reach. When a price changes, the move to a new equilibrium splits into a substitution effect and an income effect, and the size and sign of the income effect decide whether the good is normal, inferior or Giffen.

1 · Indifference curves

What one curve shows

Every point on one curve gives the same level of satisfaction, so the consumer is indifferent between them. Utility does not have to be measured in numbers, only ranked (ordinal).

An indifference map

Many curves, one for each level of satisfaction. Further from the origin means more satisfaction, because the consumer prefers more to less.

PropertyWhy
Downward slopingTo keep satisfaction the same, getting more X means giving up some Y.
Convex to the originDiminishing marginal rate of substitution: the more X you have, the less Y you will give up for another unit of X.
Never crossIf they crossed, one point would be on two levels of satisfaction, which breaks consistency (transitivity).
Higher curve = more satisfactionMore of both goods is preferred.
Slope of the indifference curve = marginal rate of substitution (MRS) = MUx ÷ MUy

2 · Budget lines

With income M and prices Px and Py, the consumer can buy any combination where Px·x + Py·y ≤ M. The budget line is the limit.

ChangeEffect on the budget line
Income rises (or falls)Parallel shift outwards (or inwards). The slope is the same.
Price of X falls (or rises)Pivots outwards (or inwards) around the Y-axis intercept. The slope becomes flatter (or steeper).
Price of Y falls (or rises)Pivots outwards (or inwards) around the X-axis intercept.
Intercepts: M ÷ Px (on the X axis) and M ÷ Py (on the Y axis). Slope = Px ÷ Py

A sales tax on X acts like a rise in Px: the budget line pivots inwards. A subsidy pivots it outwards. Preferences do not shift the budget line (a 2025 Paper 3 question).

3 · Consumer equilibrium

The consumer wants the highest indifference curve that the budget line allows. That is where the budget line is a tangent to an indifference curve.

Tangency: MRS = Px ÷ Py, so MUx ÷ Px = MUy ÷ Py

This is the same condition as the equi-marginal principle from syllabus 7.1: indifference curves reach it without needing to measure utility in numbers.

4 · A change in price: substitution effect and income effect

When the price of X falls, two things happen. Economists separate them with a compensating budget line: a dashed line parallel to the new budget line, just touching the original indifference curve.

Substitution effect (SE)

X is now relatively cheaper, so the consumer switches towards X, staying on the same indifference curve (same satisfaction). The SE is always in the same direction: a fall in the price raises the quantity of X.

Income effect (IE)

The price fall raises real income: the consumer can reach a higher curve. For a normal good they buy more X. For an inferior good they buy less X as they can afford better alternatives.

Type of goodIncome effect of a price fallNet effect on quantityDemand curve
NormalPositive: reinforces the SEQuantity rises a lotDownward sloping
Inferior (not Giffen)Negative but smaller than the SEQuantity still rises, by lessDownward sloping, steeper
GiffenNegative and larger than the SEQuantity fallsUpward sloping
Every Giffen good is inferior, but most inferior goods are not Giffen. The income effect has to be both negative and larger than the substitution effect. See the Giffen Paradox page in this chapter's Resources for a worked example.

5 · Deriving the demand curve

  1. Start at equilibrium with the original budget line. This gives the first (price, quantity) point.
  2. Change the price of X. The budget line pivots. Find the new tangency.
  3. Repeat. The path of the equilibria is the price consumption curve.
  4. Plot each price against the quantity of X bought. Joining the points gives the demand curve. It slopes down for normal and ordinary inferior goods and up for a Giffen good.

6 · Assumptions and evaluation

Assumptions

  • Rational consumers who prefer more to less
  • A two-good world
  • Constant prices of other goods, constant tastes, given income
  • Goods can be substituted, and curves are convex
  • Transitivity (consistent ranking)
  • Perfect knowledge

Strengths and limits

  • Strength: needs only a ranking, not utils; splits income and substitution effects; explains inferior and Giffen goods
  • Limit: real consumers do not carry maps of preferences in their heads; many goods, not two; bundles are hard to compare; the diagram does not show demand against price directly
  • Fits small, repeat purchases better than one-off purchases

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. The numbers are simple on purpose, to show the idea.

A student's weekly budget

A student splits a fixed amount between food and travel. Each combination that uses up all the money is on the budget line. If bus fares fall, the line pivots outwards and the student may buy more of both.

Exam use: a clear everyday description of a budget line and a pivot caused by a price change.

A sales tax on soft drinks

A tax raises the price of the drink, so the budget line pivots inwards around the intercept on the other good. The consumer moves to a lower indifference curve and buys fewer drinks.

Exam use: the Oct/Nov 2020 question asked for the effect of a sales tax with indifference curves. Describe the pivot, then the substitution and income effects, then comment on elasticity.

Cheap own-brand foods

When incomes rise, some households switch from basic own-brand products to branded goods. The basic goods are inferior for those households. When their price falls, the income effect works against the substitution effect, but usually by less.

Exam use: an example of an inferior good that is not Giffen, with a downward sloping demand curve.

The Giffen idea

The classic story is a very poor household whose diet is mostly one staple. If its price rises, the household may have to cut back on better foods and buy even more of the staple. Real-world evidence for this is very limited.

Exam use: state the conditions: inferior, very large income effect, few substitutes. Mention that it is rare and hard to prove.

A promotional price cut on a luxury and on a basic good

A shop cuts the price of both a luxury item and a cheap staple. For the luxury both effects raise demand. For the cheap staple the income effect could pull the other way.

Exam use: matches the Oct/Nov 2021 question comparing a luxury and an inferior good.

A manufacturer pricing a new line

A firm wants to know how customers react to a price rise. If the good is normal, demand is likely to fall. If it is a Giffen good, demand could rise. Knowing which type it is helps the firm to set prices.

Exam use: the Oct/Nov 2020 question on why a manufacturer cares about the type of good.

KANE

An indifference curve shows all the combinations of two goods that give a consumer the same satisfaction, and a budget line shows the combinations the consumer can afford with a given income and given prices. The consumer is in equilibrium where the budget line is a tangent to the highest indifference curve the consumer can reach. A rise in the price of a good pivots the budget line inwards, and the change in quantity demanded can be split into a substitution effect and an income effect.

A rise in price is likely to reduce the demand for a normal good because both effects work in the same direction. The substitution effect moves the consumer along the original indifference curve towards the relatively cheaper good, so less of the dearer good is bought. The fall in real income then lowers consumption of a normal good further, so the income effect also reduces quantity demanded. The demand curve slopes downward.

For a Giffen good the effects are likely to work in opposite directions. The substitution effect still reduces consumption, but the fall in real income causes the consumer to buy more of the good because it is inferior, as the consumer can no longer afford better alternatives. If this negative income effect is larger than the substitution effect, quantity demanded rises when the price rises, and the demand curve slopes upward. This is a possible outcome for a very poor household that spends much of its income on a staple food. Giffen goods appear to be very rare, so for most goods the effect of a price rise is likely to be the same as for a normal good.

The extent of the difference depends on the price elasticity of demand. For a normal good a price rise could cause a large or small fall in demand depending on how many substitutes exist. It also depends on the slope of the indifference curves, which show the marginal rate of substitution: the more easily the consumer can swap one good for another, the larger the substitution effect and the more likely it is to outweigh the income effect. Overall, a rise in price is likely to affect the demand for a normal good very differently from a Giffen good only where the income effect is large and negative. In most markets the difference is small, but it is real in theory and helps to explain why a demand curve may not always slope downward.

Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/42 · Q3(b) 13 marks Use indifference curve analysis to discuss whether the demand curve for a good will always slope downwards.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Define indifference curves and budget lines; show a price change and the new budget line.
  • Split into income and substitution effects, and say how the size and sign of each affect the slope of the demand curve.
  • Recognise that the demand curve may slope upward. A conclusion is needed for 12 or 13.
KANE

An indifference curve shows combinations of two goods giving equal satisfaction, and a budget line shows the combinations that can be bought with a given income at given prices. A fall in the price of a good pivots the budget line outwards and the consumer moves to a new equilibrium where the new line touches a higher indifference curve.

The demand curve for most goods is likely to slope downward because both effects of a price fall raise quantity demanded. The substitution effect moves the consumer along the original curve towards the cheaper good. For a normal good the rise in real income also increases consumption, so the income effect reinforces the substitution effect.

For an inferior good the income effect works the other way, because the consumer buys less of it as real income rises. If the negative income effect is smaller than the substitution effect, quantity demanded still rises when the price falls and the demand curve still slopes downward, but more steeply. If the negative income effect is larger than the substitution effect, as for a Giffen good, quantity demanded falls when the price falls and the demand curve slopes upward.

Giffen goods are very rare and may only occur where a good takes up a large share of a very poor household's budget with few substitutes, so the demand curve is likely to slope downward in nearly all markets. The result also depends on the assumptions of the theory, such as rational behaviour and perfect information, which may not hold. In conclusion, a demand curve may not always slope downward, but the exceptions are limited to special cases.

C · Multiple choice questions (Paper 3)

Choose an answer, then read the reason. Source: paper code and question number.

Top tips

  • Always draw the diagram. Several 20-mark questions cap the answer at Level 2 without one. Label axes (Good X, Good Y), IC1 and IC2, BL1 and BL2, and points A, B and C.
  • Say what the curves are. Indifference curve: same satisfaction. Budget line: affordable combinations. Equilibrium: tangency, highest curve reached.
  • A price change pivots the budget line. An income change shifts it in parallel.
  • Split the price effect in order: substitution effect first (along the original curve, dashed line), income effect second (to the new curve).
  • The SE is always in the same direction. The sign of the IE decides the type of good.
  • Giffen needs both conditions: inferior, and the negative IE bigger than the SE. Do not confuse it with an ordinary inferior good.
  • Move from the diagram to the demand curve. Join the price-quantity points and say whether it slopes up or down.
  • Evaluate with the assumptions: rational consumers, two goods, ranking of bundles, perfect knowledge, constant tastes, and one-off purchases.
