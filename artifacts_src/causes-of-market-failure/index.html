Causes of Market Failure and the Case for Intervention

Externalities, public goods, merit and demerit goods, information failure and monopoly power: what each is, why the market fails, and what a government can do. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Market failure happens when the free market fails to allocate resources efficiently, so society's welfare is lower than it could be. The main causes are externalities, public goods (non-rival and non-excludable, so free riders mean the market will not supply them), merit and demerit goods (under- or over-consumed because people misjudge the benefit or harm), information failure (one side knows more) and monopoly power. Governments can respond with taxes, subsidies, regulation, provision and information, but government failure means intervention can leave resources no better allocated. Good answers name the cause, the type of inefficiency, the policy and its limits.

1 · Causes of market failure

Market failure happens when the free market fails to allocate resources efficiently, so society's welfare is lower than it could be. The main causes:

Externalities

Costs or benefits that fall on third parties (pollution, vaccination). The market ignores them, so output differs from the social optimum. See syllabus 7.4.

Public goods

Non-rival (one person's use does not reduce another's) and non-excludable (cannot stop non-payers). The free-rider problem means no one pays, so the market does not provide them: a missing market. Examples: street lighting, national defence.

Merit and demerit goods

Merit goods (education, vaccination) are under-consumed because consumers underestimate the benefit. Demerit goods (cigarettes) are over-consumed because consumers underestimate the harm.

Information failure

One side knows more than the other (asymmetric information), such as a seller of a used car. Consumers cannot judge quality or risk, so they may make poor choices.

Monopoly power

A firm that can set price restricts output and charges more than marginal cost, so there is allocative inefficiency and possibly X-inefficiency.

Other causes

Immobility of factors (workers cannot easily change jobs), short-termism, and (as an equity issue rather than an efficiency one) income inequality.

Careful with income inequalityStrictly it is not a source of market failure in the efficiency sense (a 2022 Paper 3 question). It is an equity concern, covered in syllabus 8.2.

2 · Government intervention and government failure

Governments may use taxes, subsidies, regulation, direct provision, price controls and information campaigns to correct failures (see syllabus 8.1). But intervention can fail: the government may lack information, act too slowly, create new distortions, or be influenced by pressure groups. This is government failure, and it is the standard evaluation point for "can market failure be eliminated?" questions.

4 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Street lighting and lighthouses

No one can be stopped from using a street light, and one person's use does not reduce another's. Each household has a reason to let others pay. Without government (or local authority) provision, the market might supply too few, or none.

Exam use: the standard public good example. Name non-rival, non-excludable and free riders, then link to allocative inefficiency (the 2022 12-mark question).

A used car market

A seller knows more about the car than the buyer. Buyers may offer lower prices to protect themselves, which can drive good cars out of the market. This is asymmetric information.

Exam use: information failure. Government responses include inspections and compulsory disclosure.

Vaccination and education

People may undervalue the long-term benefit of a vaccine or of schooling, and others also gain. They are likely to be under-consumed if left to the market. This is why they are called merit goods.

Exam use: the 2023 essay on subsidies to education used a merit good and positive externalities.

May/June 2024 · 9708/42 · Q2 20 marks Market failure exists in all economies. Evaluate, with the aid of a diagram(s), the meaning of market failure and two policies a government may use to correct market failure.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Meaning of market failure through allocative inefficiency, productive inefficiency or information failure. Define allocative efficiency (maximising consumer satisfaction) with a diagram showing AR = MC or MSB = MSC.
  • Productive inefficiency: not producing at minimum average cost; imperfect competition lacks both types of efficiency.
  • Two policies: government acts to correct the failure of markets to allocate efficiently. Positive externalities cause under-consumption (subsidy), negative externalities cause overproduction (tax). Diagrams for each policy.
Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/42 · Q2 25 marks Discuss whether market failure can be successfully eliminated by governments.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Causes of market failure: imperfect competition (productive and allocative inefficiency), imperfect information, externalities (positive and negative), missing markets and public goods.
  • Methods: indirect taxes and subsidies, direct provision, legislation.
  • Problems of intervention: lack of information, time lags.
  • Level 4 (18 to 25): three methods of reducing market failure and a comment on success; conclusion needed for 22 to 25.
Watch the word "eliminated"Argue that intervention may reduce but is unlikely to eliminate market failure, because of information problems, costs and new distortions.
Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/41 · Q2 25 marks 'The cause and extent of market failure depends entirely upon the type of market structure under consideration.' Discuss this statement.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Two ways to link market failure: to the type of good (merit, demerit, public goods) or to the market structure (monopolistic competition, oligopoly, monopoly).
  • Monopoly clearly produces allocative and productive inefficiency. But failures such as externalities, public goods and information failure can occur in any market structure, so "entirely" is too strong.
May/June 2020 (not sat) · 9708/41 and 43 · Q2 25 marks Discuss whether competitive markets in the private sector are the best way to achieve an efficient allocation of resources.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explain efficiency; discuss what "competitive markets" means (not only perfect competition). Efficiency is achieved in perfect competition but not necessarily in other competitive markets, and individual actions are not always best for society.
  • Reasons for market failure, and whether government intervention is necessary or desirable.
  • No conclusion: maximum 21.
Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/41 · Q2 25 marks 'The existence of externalities is neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition for government intervention to achieve efficiency in the economy.' Do you support this opinion?

Take "necessary" and "sufficient" separately. Not necessary: intervention may be needed for other reasons (public goods, monopoly power, information failure). Not sufficient: externalities may be small, intervention may be costly, government failure may leave things worse. Conclude with a judgement.

  • The free-rider problem explains why the free market does not provide them. Market failure in the context of allocative inefficiency: if public goods are not provided, consumer satisfaction is not maximised unless the State provides them.

Top tips

  • For market failure, name the cause and the type of inefficiency, then the policy and its limits.
  • Always evaluate with government failure: poor information, time lags, wrong tax rate, cost, political pressure.
  • Check the words: "always", "eliminated", "entirely", "best". Answer on those terms and give a conclusion (many Level 4 marks need one).
