Paper 3 Multiple Choice: Technique and Practice

How to score on Paper 3: the format, a four-step method, the seven question types, calculation and wording traps, timing, and twelve past questions by type. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and practice.

This part in four sentences

Paper 3 is 30 multiple choice questions in 1 hour 15 minutes, one mark each, with no marks lost for a wrong answer, so you should answer every question. It covers the whole A Level content (topics 7 to 11) and assumes AS knowledge, and it is 17% of the A Level. Questions test knowledge, application and analysis through definitions, calculations, diagrams, tables of data and statements to judge. The marks go to students who read carefully, work out an answer before looking at the options, and use the options to eliminate, not to students who know the most facts.

The four steps

StepWhat to doTime
1 · Read the stemCover the options. Underline the qualifier (not, least likely, most likely, definitely, only) and the key concept. Note any figures.15 s
2 · PredictSay or write your own answer before you look. For a calculation do the sum. For a diagram sketch the shift. For a "not" question list what would be true.30 s
3 · EliminateRead each option and cross out those that are wrong, with a reason. Do not stop at the first one that looks right.45 s
4 · CheckRe-read the stem and make sure your answer fits the qualifier. Mark the answer sheet carefully and flag it if you are unsure.10 s

How wrong options are built

Examiners write the three wrong options (distractors) from common mistakes. Recognising the pattern speeds up elimination.

Distractor patternWhat it looks likeExample
Reversed directionRight idea, wrong way round"A rise in the interest rate raises bond prices"
True but irrelevantA correct statement that does not answer this questionAn external benefit that is actually a private benefit
Neighbouring conceptConfuses two similar termsLaffer curve, Lorenz curve, Kuznets curve, Phillips curve
Half rightThe first part is correct and the second is wrong (in combination tables)"Current account improves; financial account improves" when only one does
Calculation slipThe answer you get if you forget a stepReal income per head without dividing by population
Extreme words"always", "never", "only", "must" make a statement easy to disprove"Tariffs always raise welfare"

Seven question types and how to attack them

In the past papers roughly one question in six has a negative stem (not, least, except) and about one in five refers to a diagram, graph or chart. Know what to do for each type.

TypeHow to spot itWhat to do
1 · Negative"What is not...", "least likely", "which does not"Underline the qualifier. Mark each option T or F. The odd one out is your answer.
2 · Definition or concept"What is meant by...", "Which describes..."Say the definition in your own words first. Beware the neighbouring concept.
3 · CalculationNumbers in the stem, "what is the approximate change"Write the formula, then substitute. Check units and whether per head or in total. Estimate to the nearest whole number.
4 · Diagram or graphA figure, curve or shaded areasLabel axes and curves yourself. Find the starting equilibrium, then the shift, then the new equilibrium. Read what each area represents.
5 · Combination tableA grid of yes/no, rise/fall or high/lowDecide each column separately, then find the row that matches both (or all) columns.
6 · Data interpretation"What can be concluded from the table?"Only accept statements the table proves. Watch for totals versus per head, and cause versus correlation.
7 · Application or best exampleA short real-world scenarioMatch the scenario to the concept it illustrates. Check who is affected (third parties for externalities).

Words in stems that change the answer

WordWhat it means for you
least likely, not, exceptThree options are true or likely. You need the odd one out.
most likely, bestMore than one option may be possible. Choose the strongest.
definitelyThe conclusion must always follow. Reject options that only might be true.

Top tips

  • Answer every question. There is no penalty. Fill in any blank in the last minute.
  • Cover the options. Read the stem, predict, then look. It stops the options from steering you.
  • Underline the qualifier. Not, least, most likely, definitely: the wrong answer to a negative question is usually the one you would pick for a positive one.
  • Sketch a diagram. Even a rough supply and demand or AD and AS sketch saves mistakes.
  • Estimate numbers. Round before you calculate and check which options are near. Beware unit and per head traps.
  • Flag and move on. Do not spend more than about 3 minutes. Mark the question and come back.
  • Check the answer sheet. Make sure the question number and the letter match.
