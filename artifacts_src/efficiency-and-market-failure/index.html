Efficiency and Market Failure

What economists mean by an efficient use of scarce resources (productive, allocative, dynamic, X and Pareto), why a free market may not reach it, and the main causes of market failure. Animated diagrams built from numbers, labs, drills and the past paper questions, with mark scheme points.

The chapter in four sentences

Resources are scarce, so economists ask whether they are used efficiently. Productive efficiency means output at the lowest possible cost, allocative efficiency means producing the goods society values most (price = marginal cost, or MSB = MSC), and dynamic efficiency means cutting costs over time through investment and innovation. Pareto efficiency means nobody can be made better off without making someone else worse off. When a free market fails to reach an efficient outcome, there is market failure, which has several causes and often a case for government action.

Static versus dynamicProductive and allocative efficiency are static (a snapshot at one moment). Dynamic efficiency is about change over time. A firm can be statically inefficient yet dynamically efficient: a monopoly charging a high price now may invest in a cost-cutting innovation.

2 · The picture: allocative efficiency and welfare loss

Demand is the marginal social benefit and supply is the marginal cost. The market output where they meet gives the largest total surplus (consumer surplus plus producer surplus). Any other output wastes some surplus: the deadweight welfare loss.

  • Underproduction (output below Q*): the benefit of the next unit is greater than its cost, so society loses the gain from units not made.
  • Overproduction (output above Q*): the cost of the last units is greater than their benefit.

4 · Market failure

Market failure happens when the free market fails to allocate resources efficiently, so society's welfare is lower than it could be. The main causes:

Externalities

Costs or benefits that fall on third parties (pollution, vaccination). The market ignores them, so output differs from the social optimum. See syllabus 7.4.

Public goods

Non-rival (one person's use does not reduce another's) and non-excludable (cannot stop non-payers). The free-rider problem means no one pays, so the market does not provide them: a missing market. Examples: street lighting, national defence.

Merit and demerit goods

Merit goods (education, vaccination) are under-consumed because consumers underestimate the benefit. Demerit goods (cigarettes) are over-consumed because consumers underestimate the harm.

Information failure

One side knows more than the other (asymmetric information), such as a seller of a used car. Consumers cannot judge quality or risk, so they may make poor choices.

Monopoly power

A firm that can set price restricts output and charges more than marginal cost, so there is allocative inefficiency and possibly X-inefficiency.

Other causes

Immobility of factors (workers cannot easily change jobs), short-termism, and (as an equity issue rather than an efficiency one) income inequality.

Careful with income inequalityStrictly it is not a source of market failure in the efficiency sense (a 2022 Paper 3 question). It is an equity concern, covered in syllabus 8.2.

5 · Government intervention and government failure

Governments may use taxes, subsidies, regulation, direct provision, price controls and information campaigns to correct failures (see syllabus 8.1). But intervention can fail: the government may lack information, act too slowly, create new distortions, or be influenced by pressure groups. This is government failure, and it is the standard evaluation point for "can market failure be eliminated?" questions.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. The numbers are simple on purpose, to show the idea.

Street lighting and lighthouses

No one can be stopped from using a street light, and one person's use does not reduce another's. Each household has a reason to let others pay. Without government (or local authority) provision, the market might supply too few, or none.

Exam use: the standard public good example. Name non-rival, non-excludable and free riders, then link to allocative inefficiency (the 2022 12-mark question).

A regulated water company

A water company may be the only supplier in an area, so it has monopoly power. A regulator caps prices and sets targets for investment, because the firm could otherwise restrict output or let costs drift upwards.

Exam use: monopoly power as market failure, and regulation as a response. Evaluate with regulatory information problems (government failure).

Pharmaceutical research

Drug companies spend heavily on research and rely on patents to earn profits. This may be dynamic efficiency: new treatments and lower costs in time. But patent protection gives monopoly power and may mean high prices today.

Exam use: a case where static inefficiency (high price now) could be traded for dynamic efficiency (innovation). A good evaluation point on monopolies.

A used car market

A seller knows more about the car than the buyer. Buyers may offer lower prices to protect themselves, which can drive good cars out of the market. This is asymmetric information.

Exam use: information failure. Government responses include inspections and compulsory disclosure.

Vaccination and education

People may undervalue the long-term benefit of a vaccine or of schooling, and others also gain. They are likely to be under-consumed if left to the market. This is why they are called merit goods.

Exam use: the 2023 essay on subsidies to education used a merit good and positive externalities.

A state-owned firm without competition

A public sector firm that is the only provider may have little pressure to keep costs down. Unit costs could rise above the minimum, which is X-inefficiency.

Exam use: an example of X-inefficiency, and a government failure point when the government itself is the producer.

May/June 2024 · 9708/41 and 43 · Q2 20 marks The long-term equilibrium position in perfect competition is frequently used to illustrate efficient resource allocation in a free market economy. Explain why this is so and consider what prevents efficiency from being achieved.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (14): allocative and productive efficiency; a diagram or explanation of long-run equilibrium in perfect competition; the barriers to efficiency: market failure (excessive profits, very large investments needed, externalities, merit goods, need for public goods) and how government intervention (regulation, taxation, subsidies, ownership) might help.
  • Evaluation (6): allocative and productive efficiency are mainly theoretical and depend on precise marginal calculations; intervention does not always work (government failure).
Diagram to drawA perfectly competitive firm in the long run: horizontal AR = MR at the price, MC cutting through the minimum of AC at the same point. Label P = MC (allocative) and AC at its minimum (productive).
KANE

Allocative efficiency occurs when resources are allocated to the goods consumers value most, which happens where price equals marginal cost, so that total satisfaction is maximised. Productive efficiency occurs when output is produced at the lowest possible average cost, at the minimum point of the average cost curve.

The long-term equilibrium in perfect competition illustrates efficient resource allocation because the firm achieves both types of efficiency. Firms are price takers, so the firm's average revenue equals its marginal revenue. Profit is maximised where MR equals MC, so price equals marginal cost, which gives allocative efficiency. Supernormal profits attract new firms, which shifts supply right and lowers the price until only normal profit is made, so price equals average cost. With free entry and exit this is likely to happen at the minimum of the average cost curve, where MC cuts AC, so the firm also achieves productive efficiency. This may only hold in the long run and under the strict assumptions of perfect competition, such as homogeneous products and perfect information.

Several features of real markets could prevent efficiency from being achieved. Where there are external costs, such as pollution, the firm ignores the cost to third parties, so the market output is higher than the socially efficient output and allocative efficiency is not achieved at the social level. Public goods such as street lighting would not be provided by the market because of the free rider problem, and merit goods such as education could be under-consumed. Where a very large investment is needed, one firm may dominate the market and earn excessive profit with a price above marginal cost. A government could respond with a tax on the polluter, a subsidy for education, direct provision of street lighting or regulation of a dominant firm.

However, the concepts of allocative and productive efficiency are mainly theoretical because they depend on precise calculations of marginal changes and costs. Government intervention may also fail if the government has poor information or sets the tax at the wrong level, so it does not necessarily achieve efficiency. Overall, perfect competition is a useful benchmark that illustrates why price equal to marginal cost is efficient, but real markets are unlikely to meet its conditions, and the cures for market failure may themselves be imperfect.

Feb/March 2021 · 9708/42 · Q3(b) 13 marks Discuss the view that a firm operating in a perfectly competitive market will achieve economic efficiency but a monopoly firm will not.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explain allocative, productive and dynamic efficiency. Compare the perfectly competitive firm in the long run (allocative and productive efficiency) with a monopoly (potentially less efficient outcomes).
  • Dynamic efficiency may be possible for monopoly (supernormal profit to invest), but not for the competitive firm; and a monopoly might suffer from X-inefficiency. Assess the view.
KANE

Economic efficiency includes allocative efficiency, where price equals marginal cost, productive efficiency, where output is produced at the lowest average cost, and dynamic efficiency, where costs fall over time because of investment and innovation.

A firm in perfect competition is likely to achieve allocative and productive efficiency in the long run. As a price taker it maximises profit where MC equals the market price, so price equals marginal cost. Free entry and exit remove supernormal profit, so the firm ends at the minimum of its average cost curve. A monopoly sets MR equal to MC and charges a price above marginal cost, producing less than the allocatively efficient output, and its average cost could be above the minimum. This leads to a deadweight welfare loss.

The view may be too simple. A perfectly competitive firm earns only normal profit in the long run, so it is unlikely to have the funds for research and development, which makes dynamic efficiency less likely. A monopoly earns supernormal profit that could be reinvested, and it may enjoy economies of scale that lower its costs. A monopoly may also be X-inefficient because it faces no competitive pressure, so its costs could be higher than necessary. Overall, the view is partly correct on static efficiency, but a monopoly could be efficient in other ways, and perfect competition is rare, so the claim that a monopoly will not achieve economic efficiency is too strong.

Top tips

  • Define efficiency in terms of resources. The mark scheme rewards a link to the use of scarce resources for each type.
  • Give the condition for each: productive = minimum AC (MC = AC), allocative = P = MC (or MSB = MSC), dynamic = lower LRAC over time, Pareto = no one better off without someone worse off.
  • Draw the diagram. Perfect competition long-run, or a monopoly with the deadweight loss, or supply and demand with the welfare loss shaded.
  • Use MSB = MSC for allocative efficiency whenever externalities are mentioned. P = MC only works if there are no externalities.
  • Do not say monopoly is always inefficient. It may be dynamically efficient and benefit from economies of scale. Perfect competition is often not dynamically efficient.
  • For market failure, name the cause and the type of inefficiency, then the policy and its limits.
  • Always evaluate with government failure: poor information, time lags, wrong tax rate, cost, political pressure.
  • Check the words: "always", "eliminated", "entirely", "best". Answer on those terms and give a conclusion (many Level 4 marks need one).
