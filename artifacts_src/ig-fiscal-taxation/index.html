Taxation

Why governments tax, direct and indirect, progressive, proportional and regressive taxes, what makes a good tax, and who bears the impact. Syllabus 4.2.3.

The big idea

A tax is a compulsory payment to the government. Governments tax to raise revenue and to achieve other aims such as reducing the consumption of demerit goods or redistributing income. Taxes are classified as direct or indirect and as progressive, proportional or regressive, and each tax affects consumers, workers, firms, the government and the economy differently.

Reasons for taxation

Aims of taxes

  • Raise revenue to pay for spending (the main one).
  • Redistribute income from rich to poor.
  • Discourage demerit goods such as cigarettes and alcohol.
  • Raise firms' costs where they cause pollution or other external costs.
  • Discourage imports with tariffs to protect domestic industries.
  • Influence AD: cut taxes in a slump, raise them to cool a boom.

Link to market failure

A tax on a demerit good or a polluter pushes the price towards the full social cost, so it can correct a market failure while also raising money. A tariff does the same for protection.

Exam link: one policy often has more than one aim. Say which one the question is about.

Categories of tax

Direct taxes are on income and wealth; the person or firm that pays bears the burden. Indirect taxes are on spending; the seller pays the government but can pass some of the burden on to buyers.

Principles of taxation: a "good tax"

QualityMeaning
EquityFair: based on ability to pay
CertaintyEasy to understand and calculate
ConvenienceEasy to pay
EconomyCosts of collecting are well below the revenue raised
FlexibilityCan be changed, or changes itself, with the economy (income tax and sales tax revenue rise in a boom)
EfficiencyImproves markets or at least does not damage them (a pollution tax, a windfall tax; rates not so high that they discourage effort)

No tax is perfect. Income tax scores well on equity and flexibility, but its certainty falls if there are many allowances.

The impact of taxes

Tax base and burden

The tax base is what is taxed. A wide base can allow low rates; very high rates, especially on firms, can shrink the base if firms leave. The tax burden is the tax paid, often as a % of GDP.

Incidence of an indirect tax

If demand is inelastic consumers bear most of it (firms can pass it on). If demand is elastic producers bear most. Supply works the opposite way: the more inelastic supply is, the more suppliers bear.

Direct taxes: for and against

  • For: redistribute income and wealth, act as automatic stabilisers, raise a lot where jobs are formal and incomes are high.
  • Against: may discourage effort, enterprise and saving; high rates may push workers and firms away.

Higher tax is not always a disincentive: workers with fixed commitments such as mortgages may work harder.

Indirect taxes: for and against

  • For: cheap and easy to collect, harder to evade, less of a disincentive to effort, can target a good, people can choose not to buy, useful with a large informal economy.
  • Against: tend to be regressive, raise prices and may feed inflation.

Automatic stabilisers are spending and taxes that reduce swings in activity with no change in policy: in a boom tax revenue rises and benefit spending falls, in a slump the reverse. Flat taxes (a single rate) are simple and reduce the incentive to evade, but critics worry they are regressive; in practice flat taxes usually have a tax-free allowance.

Putting the effects together

WhoPossible impact of higher taxes
ConsumersLower disposable income (direct taxes) or higher prices (indirect taxes), so spending may fall
WorkersLower take-home pay, which may reduce the incentive to work longer hours or seek promotion (but not always)
Producers and firmsHigher costs and lower profits; may cut output, investment or jobs, or move abroad
GovernmentMore revenue to spend or repay debt, if the tax base does not shrink
The economyLower AD, which can ease inflation but may slow growth; can redistribute income and correct market failures

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Taxes on plastic bags

Ireland, the UK and several other countries tax plastic carrier bags, while others have banned them. The main aim is to cut litter, with revenue as a by-product.

Exam use: an indirect tax used to correct an externality. Draw supply shifting left, and note a second reason: it can raise revenue.

Tax on sugary drinks and tobacco

Many governments add excise duties to tobacco and alcohol, and some to sugary drinks, to discourage demerit goods. Demand for addictive goods is often inelastic, so consumers bear most of the tax and the fall in consumption may be small, but revenue is high.

Exam use: link inelastic demand to incidence, and evaluate: the tax is regressive because poorer households spend a larger share of income on it.

Moving from income tax towards VAT

Some governments, such as France in the 2010s according to coursebook figures, collect more revenue from VAT than from income tax. VAT is easier to collect and harder to evade, but makes the tax system less progressive.

Exam use: a "more or less progressive" question. Shifting towards indirect taxes tends to make the system less progressive.

Flat taxes in Eastern Europe

A number of East European countries replaced several income and corporate tax rates with one lower rate to simplify the system and cut evasion. Critics noted the regressive risk, though in practice a tax-free allowance softens it.

Exam use: arguments for and against a flat tax in a discuss question; mention the allowance as a point of evaluation.

Windfall taxes

Governments have sometimes imposed one-off taxes on very high profits, for example in banking or energy during price spikes. The aim is revenue and fairness, and it may lead firms to cut charges.

Exam use: an example for the "efficiency" and "equity" qualities, with the risk that it discourages investment if firms fear more of them.

Q1 Define 2 marks What is meant by a progressive tax?
ReminderTwo marks, two ideas. Say what is taken, and that the percentage rises with income. Do not just say "the rich pay more": that is also true of a flat tax.
A progressive tax is one that takes a larger percentage of the income (or wealth) of the rich than of the poor, so the rate rises as income or wealth rises.
Common mistakeWriting "the rich pay more tax". In every type of tax the rich usually pay more dollars; progressive is about the percentage.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain the difference between a direct tax and an indirect tax, giving an example of each.
Reminder"Difference" means a comparison: use words such as "whereas". Two marks for the difference, one for each example, and name the base (income or spending).
A direct tax is a tax on income or wealth, such as income tax, paid directly to the government by the person or firm that bears the burden. Whereas an indirect tax is a tax on expenditure, such as VAT, which firms collect and pay to the government but can pass on, at least partly, to consumers through higher prices. For example, a worker pays income tax out of pay, but the same worker pays VAT in the price of goods in a shop.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse the reasons why governments impose taxes.
ReminderPick two or three reasons and develop each with a chain: reason → how the tax achieves it → effect. A list of six undeveloped reasons scores far less than three developed ones.
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

Governments impose taxes to raise revenue to pay for spending. Tax revenue is the main source of government income in many countries, and it can fund public goods and merit goods such as defence, schools and healthcare that the private sector may not supply sufficiently. Without it, the government could need to borrow more, which increases the national debt and interest payments.

Governments also tax to discourage the consumption of demerit goods. An excise duty on cigarettes raises their price, which may reduce quantity demanded and so cut the harm to health that consumers may not fully recognise.

A third reason is to redistribute income. A progressive income tax takes a larger share from higher earners and the revenue may pay benefits to lower income groups, which could reduce inequality.

Q4 Explain 4 marks Using a demand and supply diagram, explain why consumers may bear most of the burden of a tax on cigarettes.
ReminderThe diagram earns marks: S₁ shifting left by the amount of the tax to S₂, a steep (inelastic) D, the new price P₁ above the old P, and the tax per unit marked. Then say why: inelastic demand.
A tax on cigarettes raises the costs of sellers, so supply shifts to the left by the amount of the tax. Cigarette demand is likely to be price inelastic because many smokers are addicted and have few substitutes. Because quantity demanded would fall by relatively little, sellers could pass most of the tax on to buyers by raising the price, so the price paid by consumers may rise by most of the tax and consumers could bear the greater share of the burden.
Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not an increase in income tax rates will improve economic performance.
Reminder"Whether or not" means two sides and a judgement. Define economic performance in your own words (growth, employment, inflation, equity, the budget) so the answer is about the right thing. Finish with "it depends on…".
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

Economic performance is judged by how well the government meets aims such as economic growth, low unemployment, stable prices and a fair distribution of income, and income tax is a direct tax on earnings.

An increase in income tax rates could improve economic performance if the extra revenue is spent well. The revenue could fund education, healthcare or infrastructure that may raise productivity and long-run growth, and a progressive income tax may reduce inequality. If the economy is overheating, higher taxes could also cut disposable income and consumption, which may ease inflationary pressure. However, these gains depend on how the money is used and on how long the benefits take to arrive.

On the other hand, higher rates could reduce performance. Lower disposable income is likely to reduce consumption and aggregate demand, which may slow growth and raise unemployment. Workers may be discouraged from overtime or promotion and entrepreneurs from expanding, and some highly skilled people might move abroad or work in the informal economy, which could shrink the tax base. This effect may be weaker for workers who cannot choose their hours, or who have fixed commitments such as a mortgage and may work more.

Overall, the effect is likely to depend on the size of the increase, the state of the economy (a boom or a recession), and how the revenue is spent. A modest rise on higher earners during a boom, used to fund productive spending, is more likely to improve performance than a large rise during a recession.

Top tips

  • Progressive is about the percentage. The rich pay more dollars under every tax. Compare the % of income.
  • Direct = income and wealth. Indirect = spending. Give an example of each whenever asked.
  • Tax shifts supply left by the tax amount. Draw S₁ to S₂, label the new price and quantity, and mark who bears what.
  • Incidence follows elasticity. Inelastic demand: consumers bear most. Elastic demand: producers bear most.
  • Say who is affected. Consumers, workers, firms, the government and the economy: the syllabus asks for all five.
  • Use specific context. Cigarettes, petrol, income tax bands, VAT, recession and boom.
