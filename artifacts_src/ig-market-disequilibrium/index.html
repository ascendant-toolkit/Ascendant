Market Disequilibrium

Shortages and surpluses, how to show them on schedules and diagrams, and how markets return to equilibrium. Syllabus 2.4.3.

The big idea

Market disequilibrium is when quantity demanded does not equal quantity supplied, so the market does not clear. If the price is below equilibrium, demand exceeds supply: a shortage (excess demand). If the price is above equilibrium, supply exceeds demand: a surplus (excess supply). Price changes push the market back to equilibrium.

On a diagram

  • Draw a price below equilibrium: the gap between S and D (at that price) is the shortage.
  • Draw a price above equilibrium: the gap is the surplus.
  • Label Qs, Qd and the gap.

Why disequilibrium happens

  • A shift in demand or supply makes the old price too high or too low (for example a bumper harvest in a global market for grapes creates a surplus at the old price).
  • A price set by the government away from equilibrium (a maximum or minimum price).

Markets usually move back to equilibrium. The price mechanism works by rationing and signalling until Qd = Qs again.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

A bumper harvest

A big grape or wheat harvest raises supply, creating a surplus at the old price and pushing prices down.

Exam use: Surplus.

Concert tickets

At a low face value tickets sell out immediately, so a shortage develops and resale prices rise.

Exam use: Shortage.

End-of-season sales

Shops cut prices to clear unsold stock.

Exam use: Surplus adjusting.

Face masks in early 2020

A surge in demand created shortages and price rises.

Exam use: Shortage after a demand shift.

ReminderSurplus: price falls; shortage: price rises.
If there is a surplus, sellers cut prices to sell stock. Quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls until they are equal. If there is a shortage, buyers compete and the price rises. Quantity demanded falls and quantity supplied rises until the market clears.

Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A market may be in disequilibrium after a shift in demand or supply, for example a bumper harvest raises supply. At the old price quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, so there is a surplus. Sellers cut prices, quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls. The market moves to a new, lower equilibrium price. Adjustment may be slow if prices are sticky or fixed by the government.

Top tips

  • Shortage: Qd > Qs; surplus: Qs > Qd. Say which is bigger.
  • Price above equilibrium = surplus. Price below = shortage.
  • Show the gap on the diagram. Label Qd, Qs and the gap.
  • Explain the adjustment. Price falls (surplus) or rises (shortage).
  • Calculate the size. Subtract the smaller from the larger.
