Free Trade and the Limits of Comparative Advantage

6.1.2 and 6.1.4: specialisation, the trading possibility curve, the advantages and disadvantages of free trade, and the limitations of the theories of absolute and comparative advantage. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

When countries specialise in goods in which they have a comparative advantage (a lower opportunity cost) and then trade, total output rises and each country can consume beyond its production possibility curve, shown by the trading possibility curve (TPC). Free trade (trade liberalisation) brings gains such as greater choice, lower prices, economies of scale, more competition and efficiency, access to resources and technology, and higher living standards. The theory rests on assumptions: only two goods and countries, constant opportunity costs, no transport costs, mobile factors, full employment and no trade barriers, and in practice these limit the benefits. Free trade can also bring costs: job losses in declining industries, over-specialisation and dependence on a few exports, the risk to infant industries, environmental costs and an uneven sharing of gains.

1 · Specialisation and the gains from trade (6.1.2)

  • Absolute advantage: a country can produce more of a good with the same resources. Comparative advantage: a country can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another. Even a country with an absolute advantage in everything gains from trade.
  • Specialise in the good with the lower opportunity cost, then trade at a price between the two countries' opportunity costs. Both gain.
  • The trading possibility curve lies outside the PPC: it shows the combinations a country can consume after specialising and trading at the terms of trade.

3 · Limitations of comparative advantage (6.1.4)

  • Transport costs and trade barriers reduce or remove the gains from trade.
  • Opportunity costs are not constant: as a country specialises, costs may rise (diminishing returns), reducing the advantage.
  • Factors are not perfectly mobile: workers displaced from one industry cannot easily move into another.
  • Comparative advantage changes over time (a dynamic, not a static, advantage) and can be created by policy, education and investment.
  • Over-specialisation is risky when demand or prices for the specialised good collapse, and the terms of trade may worsen for primary producers.
  • The theory ignores externalities, exchange rate movements, imperfect competition, the distribution of the gains and non-economic aims such as security.
  • Many goods are not homogeneous, and trade is also determined by tastes and brands, not just costs.

3 · Worked example: comparative advantage and the gains from trade

Two countries use all their resources for a year. Country A can produce either 100 units of wheat or 50 units of cloth. Country B can produce either 60 units of wheat or 60 units of cloth.

Country ACountry B
Maximum wheat or cloth100 wheat or 50 cloth60 wheat or 60 cloth
Opportunity cost of 1 wheat0.5 cloth1 cloth
Opportunity cost of 1 cloth2 wheat1 wheat
Comparative advantageWheat (lower opportunity cost: 0.5 versus 1)Cloth (lower opportunity cost: 1 versus 2)
  • Country A has an absolute advantage in wheat (100 versus 60) but B has an absolute advantage in cloth (60 versus 50). Even if one country had an absolute advantage in both, trade would still benefit both unless their opportunity costs were identical.
  • After specialisation: A makes 100 wheat and B makes 60 cloth. World output of wheat and cloth has risen compared with, say, each making half of each (A: 50 wheat + 25 cloth, B: 30 wheat + 30 cloth, total 80 wheat + 55 cloth, versus 100 wheat + 60 cloth).
  • Trade: suppose they swap at 1 cloth for 1.5 wheat, a price between the opportunity costs of 1 and 2 wheat per cloth. If A trades 45 wheat for 30 cloth, A ends with 55 wheat and 30 cloth, compared with 50 wheat and 25 cloth before: more of both. B ends with 45 wheat and 30 cloth (from 30 wheat and 30 cloth before): more wheat. Both consume beyond their production possibility curves.

4 · Trading blocs and trade liberalisation

TypeMembersBarriers between membersCommon external tariff?
Free trade areaTwo or more countriesRemovedNo: each sets its own tariffs on outsiders
Customs unionTwo or more countriesRemovedYes: all members apply the same tariffs on non-members
Common marketCustoms union plusRemoved, also free movement of labour and capitalYes
  • The World Trade Organization (WTO) works to reduce trade barriers through negotiations and rules and settles disputes.
  • Reasons countries move to tariff-free trade: specialisation and comparative advantage, lower prices and more choice, economies of scale, higher living standards and more employment in exporting industries.
  • Costs: loss of protection for domestic industries, trade diversion (buying from a less efficient member because outsiders face tariffs) and loss of control over trade policy.

5 · Why the gains may be less than the theory suggests

  • Transport costs: if it costs more to ship goods than the gain from specialisation, trade does not pay.
  • Rising opportunity costs: expanding wheat production in A means using land less suited to wheat, so the cost per unit rises and the advantage shrinks.
  • Immobile factors: farmers cannot easily become cloth workers, so unemployment may follow a change in the pattern of trade.
  • Imperfect competition and trade barriers: large firms or tariffs can distort the pattern of trade.
  • Dynamic changes: comparative advantage changes with technology, skills and investment, so a country that specialises in today's advantage may be locked into low-value goods.
  • Distribution of gains: the gains may go mainly to consumers and exporters, while workers in import-competing industries lose.

6 · Free trade: consequences for an economy

  • Possible benefits: economic growth, lower unemployment in export industries, better public finances (more tax revenue), a better balance of payments if exports grow.
  • Possible drawbacks: reduced domestic growth where industries cannot compete, structural unemployment, reliance on other countries for goods not made at home, and a possible current account deficit.
  • Which matters more depends on: the country's factor endowments, how open its economy is, its stage of development and whether the transition is gradual.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Tea and cars

A country with fertile land and a climate suited to tea produces it at a low opportunity cost, while a country with skilled engineers produces cars cheaply. If each specialises and trades, both can consume more tea and more cars.

Exam use: comparative advantage and gains from trade.

Transport costs

Heavy, low-value goods such as bricks cost so much to ship that trade is unprofitable. Transport costs reduce the benefits of specialisation and are a limit of the theory.

Exam use: limitations of comparative advantage.

A one-product economy

A country that specialises in exporting copper depends on its price. When copper prices fall, incomes, jobs and the exchange rate all suffer: the risk of over-specialisation.

Exam use: over-specialisation and dependence.

Infant industries

A new domestic car industry cannot compete with established foreign producers that have huge economies of scale. Free trade could kill it before it has the chance to grow, a case for temporary protection.

Exam use: disadvantages of free trade.

A free trade area

Several neighbouring countries remove tariffs on each other's goods but keep their own tariffs on imports from elsewhere. Trade among them grows, but firms must show where goods were made so that outside goods do not simply enter through the lowest-tariff member.

Exam use: a free trade area versus a customs union.

Specialisation by a small island economy

A small island exports tourism and imports most of its food and manufactures. This specialisation raises incomes, but if tourism collapses the economy has little else to fall back on.

Exam use: gains from specialisation and the risk of over-specialisation.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/23 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain why the theory of comparative advantage may not give a true account of the benefits of free trade.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): a lower opportunity cost of producing a product because of differing resources (1), which leads to specialisation and trade (1).
  • Application (up to 6): consideration of reasons why the law of comparative advantage may not lead to benefits through trade, up to 2 marks for each reason explained, for example transport costs, trade barriers, immobility of factors, changing opportunity costs and over-specialisation.
PlanDefine comparative advantage (opportunity cost). Then three developed limits: transport costs, immobility, increasing opportunity costs or over-specialisation.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A country has a comparative advantage in a good if it can produce it at a lower opportunity cost than another country because of its resources. It should specialise in that good and trade for others.

The benefits may be smaller than the theory suggests. Transport costs and tariffs raise the price of traded goods and can remove the gain. Factors are not perfectly mobile: workers who lose their jobs in one industry may not have the skills to move into another, so unemployment results. Opportunity costs may rise as a country specialises, because the best resources are used first, which weakens the advantage, and over-specialisation makes an economy vulnerable to a fall in the price of its main export.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/21 · Q5(a) 8 marks Explain the potential advantages of free trade and consider whether such advantages are always greater than the potential disadvantages of free trade.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): at least two advantages of free trade, such as an increase in world output, a wider range of products for consumers and an improved standard of living.
  • Analysis (up to 3): at least two disadvantages and how they contrast with the advantages: specialisation could cause some sectors to run down, with higher unemployment; overreliance on some commodities is risky; there is a security risk if vital resources are imported.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a valid judgement on whether the advantages are always greater than the disadvantages, with a conclusion.
PlanTwo advantages: world output, choice, living standards. Two disadvantages: unemployment in declining sectors, dependence. Judge: advantages usually bigger, but not always.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Free trade allows countries to specialise in goods in which they have a comparative advantage, which raises world output. Consumers get a wider choice of goods at lower prices, and living standards can rise.

But free trade can cause some industries to decline when they cannot compete with cheaper imports, which raises unemployment in those sectors. Countries that specialise in a few commodities depend heavily on their prices, and relying on imports of vital resources may be a security risk.

The advantages are probably greater for most countries over time, because of higher output and lower prices, but they are not always greater: the costs fall on particular workers and regions and can be severe for developing countries that depend on one export.

Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/23 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain how a free trade area differs from a customs union and explain two reasons why countries move towards tariff-free trade.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): what a free trade area is (1) and how a customs union differs from it (1): the main difference is a common external tariff in a customs union.
  • Application (up to 6): identification of a reason countries move to tariff-free trade (1) and up to 2 marks for explaining the benefits, for each of two reasons (3 maximum per reason). Benefits include specialisation, higher living standards, cheaper and better quality products and more choice, increased employment and benefiting from comparative advantage.
PlanFTA: no tariffs between members, each sets its own external tariffs. Customs union: common external tariff. Two benefits: specialisation, choice and lower prices.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

In a free trade area, members trade with each other without tariffs, but each member sets its own tariffs on trade with non-members. A customs union goes further by adopting a common external tariff.

Countries move towards tariff-free trade to benefit from specialisation: each can concentrate on goods with a comparative advantage, raising output and incomes. They also give consumers cheaper and better products, with greater choice, because competition from imports forces domestic firms to improve.

Based on Feb/March 2025 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess whether the consequences of free trade are always positive for an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): understanding of free trade; how free trade may benefit an economy, including increased economic growth, a reduction in unemployment, improved public finances and an improved balance of payments; an explanation of the drawbacks, including reduced domestic growth, increases in structural unemployment, over-reliance on other countries for goods not produced domestically and the possibility of a current account deficit. Level 3 considers both positives and negatives and is balanced; Level 2 may be one-sided.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): consideration that fully assesses the consequences and reaches a reasoned conclusion on whether free trade is always positive, which may refer to factor endowments, how open an economy is and its stage of development. A one-sided response cannot gain any marks for evaluation.
PlanBenefits: growth, jobs, revenue, BoP. Drawbacks: structural unemployment, dependence, current account deficit. Judge by factor endowments, openness, stage of development.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Free trade lets an economy specialise where it has a comparative advantage, so output and growth may rise, exporting industries create jobs, higher incomes raise tax revenue, and the balance of payments can improve if exports grow. But industries that cannot compete may close, causing structural unemployment, the economy may become dependent on other countries for goods it no longer makes, and cheaper imports may create a current account deficit.

The consequences are not always positive. Free trade is more likely to benefit an economy with strong factor endowments and a flexible labour market, and one that is already competitive, while a developing economy with young industries may suffer from competition. How open the economy already is, and how quickly it opens up, also matter.

Top tips

  • Define with opportunity cost. Comparative advantage is a lower opportunity cost.
  • Show specialisation and trade. Both countries can consume beyond their PPC.
  • List gains. Output, choice, prices, scale, competition.
  • List costs. Job losses, dependence, infant industries.
  • Give developed limits. Transport costs, immobility, rising opportunity costs.
  • Mention dynamic change. Comparative advantage changes and can be created.
  • Judge. Gains usually exceed costs overall, but not for everyone.
