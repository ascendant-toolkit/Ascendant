Income, Wealth and Inequality

3.3.1 to 3.3.3: income as a flow and wealth as a stock, measuring inequality with the Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient, and the economic reasons for inequality. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Income is a flow of earnings over a period of time (wages, rent, interest, dividends, benefits), while wealth is a stock of assets owned at a point in time (property, savings, shares, pensions) minus debts, so wealth is usually spread far more unequally than income. Inequality is shown with a Lorenz curve and summarised by the Gini coefficient, which runs from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (one person has everything); its calculation is not required. Income inequality arises from differences in employment, skills and education, occupation and sector, assets owned, discrimination, taxes and transfers, and some economists argue that a degree of inequality rewards effort and enterprise, while others say large gaps waste talent and harm growth. Wealth is hard to measure because assets are varied, change in value, can be hidden or moved to family or tax havens.

2 · Measuring inequality: Lorenz curve and Gini (3.3.2)

  • The Lorenz curve plots the cumulative share of income against the cumulative share of the population, from poorest to richest. The 45-degree line is perfect equality; the further the curve bows below it, the greater the inequality.
  • Gini coefficient = area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve ÷ the area under the line of equality. 0 = perfect equality, 1 = total inequality. (You do not need to calculate it.)
  • A fall in the Gini coefficient, for example from 0.52 to 0.44, means income is more equally distributed.
  • Limitations: it is a single number so different Lorenz curves can have the same Gini; it ignores wealth, the black economy and non-money income; and definitions of income vary between countries.
  • Difficulties measuring wealth: it has many forms (housing, savings, pensions, financial assets) and countries measure differently; values change with house and share prices; some is hidden in tax havens or transferred to relatives.

3 · Causes of inequality (3.3.3)

CauseHow it widens income or wealth gaps
Employment statusThe unemployed or part-time workers earn far less than full-time workers
Skills, education and experienceHigher skills bring higher pay; poor education and training limit access to well-paid jobs
Occupation and sectorPay differs between industries and between public and private sectors
Ownership of assets and inheritanceOwners of property and shares earn rent, interest and dividends and pass on wealth
Low savingPoorer households save little and cannot buy assets, while property prices favour existing owners
Discrimination and unequal opportunitiesGender, race or location can limit access to jobs and education
Taxes and transfer paymentsA regressive tax system or low benefits widen gaps; progressive taxes and benefits narrow them
Low-income countriesFew employment opportunities, poor investment in education and health, and a large informal sector
  • Can inequality benefit an economy? It may incentivise effort, enterprise and retraining; but extreme inequality can waste talent, reduce spending power of the poor, and cause social tension.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A teacher and a landlord

A teacher earns a salary (income, a flow). A landlord owns several flats (wealth, a stock) and earns rent from them (income). The landlord's wealth also grows if property prices rise, which helps explain why wealth is more unequal than income.

Exam use: income as a flow and wealth as a stock, and how they feed each other.

A falling Gini coefficient

A country's Gini falls from 0.52 to 0.44 after it expands education and benefits. Income is now more equally spread, though the country is still far from perfect equality, which would be zero.

Exam use: reading a change in the Gini.

Hidden wealth

Rich individuals may hold assets through trusts or overseas accounts, and families may transfer property to relatives. Official surveys therefore understate the wealth of the top few percent.

Exam use: why wealth is hard to measure.

Skills and pay

A surgeon trains for many years and earns much more than an unskilled worker. This reflects differences in skills, scarcity and the cost of training. Poor schools in some regions limit access to these careers.

Exam use: skills, education and occupation as causes of inequality.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/22 · Q2(a) 8 marks Explain how the Gini coefficient is used to measure income inequality and consider the relative strength of two economic reasons suggested for such inequality in a low-income country.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): the Gini coefficient is a numerical measure of the extent of income inequality (1); zero means no inequality and one means all income earned by one person (1); the nearer to zero the more equal, the nearer to one the more unequal (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): reasons may include a lack of employment opportunities, because industry is less developed; poor vocational training, because of a lack of finance; or a lack of investment in education and health, which lowers potential incomes (each explained, with why it may be more of a problem in a low-income country).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a judgement on the relative strength of the two reasons.
PlanGini: 0 to 1, what each end means. Two reasons for a low-income country, explained. Compare their strength.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The Gini coefficient is a number between zero and one that measures income inequality. Zero means perfect equality, with everyone earning the same, and one means one person earns all the income, so the closer to one, the more unequal the distribution.

In a low-income country a lack of employment opportunities may be a major cause of inequality, because industry is poorly developed and many people have no formal job. Poor education and health spending is another: those who cannot afford schooling stay in low-paid work, while a few with qualifications earn much more.

Of the two, a lack of education investment is probably the stronger long-term cause, since it keeps incomes low across generations, while a lack of jobs might ease if the economy grows. Both matter, and they reinforce each other.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/23 · Q3(a) 8 marks Explain two economic reasons for inequality in the distribution of income and wealth and consider why inequality in the distribution of wealth cannot easily be measured.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): inequality means an uneven distribution of income and wealth among a population (1); two reasons explained, for example poor education and training, leading to fewer marketable skills (1) and low saving, making it harder to buy property, with rising property prices benefiting existing owners (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): why wealth is hard to measure, for example because it has many sources such as housing, savings, financial instruments and pensions, measured differently in different countries (1); some may be hidden in tax havens (1); ownership may be transferred to family members (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a judgement about how difficult measurement really is.
PlanDefine inequality. Two reasons explained. Why wealth is hard to measure: many forms, hidden, transferred.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Inequality means income and wealth are unevenly shared across the population. One reason is education and training: people with fewer marketable skills cannot apply for well-paid jobs. A second is low saving: poorer households cannot save enough to buy property, while rising property prices benefit existing owners, so wealth gaps widen.

Wealth is hard to measure because it takes many forms, such as housing, savings, shares and pensions, which different countries count differently and whose values change. Wealthy individuals may also hold assets in tax havens or transfer ownership to relatives, so some wealth is never recorded.

Measurement is therefore less reliable for wealth than for income, which is mainly recorded through tax and payroll. Surveys can still give a rough guide, but they probably understate the wealth of the richest.

Based on Feb/March 2023 · 9708/22 · Q3(a) 8 marks Explain the possible economic reasons for inequality of income and consider whether inequality of income can benefit an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): a clear understanding of at least two reasons for inequality of income, such as whether a person is employed or unemployed (1), skills, qualifications and experience across occupations (1), public or private sector employment (1), full-time or part-time work (1), transfer payments (1) and the level and type of taxation (1). Two reasons well explained earn up to 3.
  • Analysis (up to 3): the focus is on whether inequality generates benefits, for example incentivising workers and raising productivity, encouraging enterprise and encouraging workers to retrain or reskill.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a judgement on whether income inequality can benefit an economy.
PlanTwo reasons explained. Benefits: incentives, enterprise, retraining. Judge: some inequality may help, extreme inequality does not.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Income inequality may result from differences in skills and qualifications, because highly qualified workers in skilled occupations earn more, and from whether people are in work at all, since the unemployed receive far less than the employed. Differences in taxation and transfer payments also change the gap.

Some inequality may benefit an economy because higher pay rewards effort and productivity, encourages enterprise and gives workers a reason to retrain or gain new skills.

However, a very large gap could waste talent, since poorer people may be unable to afford education, and reduce spending power. So a moderate amount of inequality may help, but not an extreme level.

Top tips

  • Flow versus stock. Income is a flow per period; wealth is a stock at a point in time.
  • Gini runs from 0 to 1. 0 = equality, 1 = one person has it all.
  • A fall means more equal. A rise means more unequal.
  • Explain, don't list, reasons. Two developed reasons beat five short ones.
  • Link causes to skills, assets, tax. Employment, education, ownership, discrimination.
  • Explain wealth measurement problems. Many forms, values change, hidden or transferred.
  • Evaluate incentives. Some inequality rewards effort; extreme inequality wastes talent.
