Externality Diagrams, Step by Step

Six animated walk-throughs, each in three stages: what causes the externality, why it is a problem, and which government intervention puts output back at the socially optimum quantity. Step through each diagram, build the fix yourself, then practise.

The three-stage method

Every externality diagram tells the same story. Cause: decision makers count only their private costs and benefits, so a cost or benefit lands on a third party who is not part of the deal (an external cost or benefit). Problem: the market settles at the private equilibrium (Q1), but the socially optimum quantity (Q*) is where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost, so output is too high or too low and there is a welfare loss. Solution: the government internalises the externality so that the decision maker faces the full social cost or benefit, which moves output to Q*. A correct diagram always ends at Q*.

2 · How to read the labels

Private and social

MPC and MPB are what the producer or consumer counts. MSC = MPC + MEC when production harms others, and MSB = MPB + MEB when consumption helps others. A curve shifts only if there is an externality on that side.

Q1, Q* and the welfare loss

Q1 is where the market clears (private MB = private MC). Q* is where MSB = MSC. The welfare loss is the triangle between MSB and MSC for the units between Q1 and Q*: each is either costing society more than it is worth, or is worth more than it costs but is not made.

Size the fix correctlyThe tax or subsidy should equal the marginal externality at Q*. Too small and some welfare loss is left; too large and the market overshoots Q*, creating a new welfare loss on the other side. The Build the fix tab lets you test this.

The numbers used throughout (D, S, an external cost or benefit of $20 per unit) are illustrative, chosen so that every diagram is easy to read and Q* comes out exactly.

5 · Multiple choice

Cases are described in general terms. Check current details before quoting a specific rate, date or figure in an exam answer.

London congestion charge: a charge on a negative externality

Introduced in central London in 2003, drivers pay a daily charge to enter the zone. Each driver adds to delay, noise and pollution for others, which the driver does not pay for. The charge is a tax that makes drivers face more of the social cost. Traffic entering the zone was reported to fall after the charge began, although the effect could weaken over time as the road network and traffic patterns change.

In an exam: use it for an indirect tax on a negative externality of consumption, and evaluate by noting the charge is a single price that may not match the true external cost, and that it could hit lower-income drivers hardest.

EU Emissions Trading System: a cap and tradable permits

Started in 2005, the system sets a cap on carbon emissions from power stations and heavy industry and issues permits that firms can trade. The cap fixes the quantity, much like the vertical supply in the fifth walk-through, and the market finds the permit price. Early on, too many permits were handed out and the price fell very low, which shows how much depends on setting the cap correctly.

In an exam: use it to explain why permits are a quantity-based alternative to a tax, and to evaluate the problem of knowing where to set the cap.

England's carrier bag charge

A 5p charge on single-use plastic bags began in large shops in 2015 and was later raised and extended. Giving away bags free meant that the litter and marine pollution they cause were not in the price. Use of bags in the main supermarkets was reported to fall sharply, which suggests a small charge could change behaviour a good deal when substitutes (reusable bags) are easy to find.

In an exam: use it to show that the size of the response depends on the elasticity of demand.

The UK soft drinks levy

From 2018 the Soft Drinks Industry Levy charged producers according to the sugar content of their drinks. The aim was to reduce the health costs of sugar, a cost partly borne by the NHS and so by other taxpayers. Many producers reformulated their drinks to cut sugar, which shows how a tax on producers can shift supply as well as demand.

In an exam: use it for a tax aimed at a negative externality of consumption, and for the argument that the burden may be shared between firms and consumers.

Vaccination programmes

The NHS offers some vaccines free to eligible groups. A person who is vaccinated is less likely to pass on the disease, so others gain without paying, a positive externality of consumption that leads to under-consumption if people pay the full price. Free provision is the extreme form of a subsidy: the price to the user falls to zero.

In an exam: use it for a subsidy or provision on a positive externality, and mention the opportunity cost to the taxpayer.

Research and development tax relief

The UK and many other governments give firms tax relief on qualifying R&D spending. Innovation tends to spill over to rivals and to the wider economy, so a firm's private return is lower than the social return. The relief lowers the firm's cost of research, a subsidy that moves the supply of R&D towards the social optimum.

In an exam: use it for a positive externality of production, and evaluate by pointing out that spillovers are hard to measure and that some firms may claim relief for work they would have done anyway.

Solar panel feed-in tariffs

From 2010 the UK paid households and businesses for the electricity they generated from solar panels. Cleaner generation benefits people who never pay for it, so the market on its own could be expected to install too few panels. The payments acted as a subsidy, and the cost to bill-payers and the fall in the scheme's rates as panel costs dropped show the trade-offs.

In an exam: use it to link a subsidy to the external benefit and to discuss government failure through cost and poorly targeted payments.

Q1 Explain 6 marks Explain, with the help of a diagram, why a market may produce too much of a good whose production causes pollution.
ReminderDraw MPC (the supply curve), MSC above it, and demand. Mark Q1 where demand meets MPC and Q* where demand meets MSC. Shade the welfare loss. Then explain the diagram in words: a diagram that is not referred to in the text scores less.

Production causes a negative externality when it imposes a cost on third parties, such as pollution, that the producer does not pay for. The marginal external cost (MEC) is added to the marginal private cost (MPC) to give the marginal social cost (MSC). Firms decide output using only their private costs, so the market clears where demand (MPB) meets supply (MPC), at Q1. The socially optimum output is lower, at Q*, where MSB equals MSC.

Between Q* and Q1 each unit costs society more (MSC) than it is worth to consumers (MSB). Output is therefore too high and there is a welfare loss, shown by the triangle between MSC and demand. For example, a power station that burns coal sells electricity without paying for the harm caused to the health of people nearby, so more electricity is likely to be produced than is best for society.

Common mistakeDrawing MSC below MPC. A negative externality of production always puts MSC above MPC. Another is to shade the whole area under the curves instead of the triangle between Q* and Q1.
Q2 Explain 6 marks Explain how a subsidy could correct the under-consumption of a good with a positive externality of consumption.
ReminderSay what the externality is, show why the market under-provides, then say what the subsidy does to supply and where output ends up. Finish on Q*.

A positive externality of consumption arises when consuming a good benefits third parties, so the marginal social benefit (MSB) is above the marginal private benefit (MPB) by the marginal external benefit (MEB). Consumers decide using only MPB, so the market settles at Q1 where MPB equals MPC. The socially optimum quantity, Q*, is higher, where MSB equals MSC, so the good is under-consumed and there is a welfare loss. For example, a person who has a vaccination also reduces the chance that others catch the disease.

A subsidy equal to the MEB at Q* lowers firms' costs, so supply shifts down and to the right. The price paid by consumers falls, quantity demanded extends to Q*, and the welfare loss is removed. The subsidy has an opportunity cost for the taxpayer, and it could be set wrongly if the MEB is hard to measure.

Common mistakeShifting the demand curve. A subsidy to producers shifts supply. The demand curve (MPB) stays where it is.
Q3 Explain 6 marks Explain how a government information campaign could correct the under-consumption of a good such as vaccination.
ReminderLink the campaign to information failure and the external benefit, then show it shifting demand (MPB) to MSB and ending at Q*. Do not shift supply.

A merit good is one that people tend to under-consume because they underestimate its private benefit or ignore the benefit to others. Vaccination has a positive externality of consumption, so MSB is above MPB. The market clears where MPB = MPC, at Q1, below the socially optimum Q* where MSB = MSC, so there is a welfare loss.

A government campaign that explains the benefits of vaccination could raise how much people value it, shifting demand to the right. If the campaign is successful, demand moves up to MSB and the market reaches Q*, removing the welfare loss. For example, health authorities run advertising and reminders for flu and measles vaccines. Compared with a subsidy, it does not directly pay firms and is likely to cost less per person, but how far demand would shift is uncertain.

Common mistakeSaying the campaign shifts supply, or that it lowers the price. It changes preferences, so it shifts the demand curve.
Q4 Evaluate 12 marks Evaluate whether an indirect tax is the best way for a government to deal with a negative externality of consumption.
ReminderExplain how the tax works using the diagram, then weigh it against at least one alternative (regulation, permits, information) and say what the answer depends on. Use "likely" and "could".

An indirect tax is a tax on a good that raises the cost of supplying it. For a negative externality of consumption, such as second-hand smoke, the market over-consumes because buyers ignore the harm to others (MSB is below MPB). A tax equal to the marginal external cost at Q* shifts supply upwards and to the left, raising the price and cutting quantity from Q1 to Q*, so the externality is internalised and the welfare loss removed. The tax also raises revenue that could be used to treat those who are harmed.

However, the tax may not be the best solution. The marginal external cost is hard to measure, so the tax could be set too high or too low. If demand is inelastic, as it could be for an addictive good, the quantity may fall by less than hoped and the burden of the tax would fall mostly on consumers, which could hit low-income households hardest. Regulation, such as a ban in public places, gives more certainty about the quantity, and information campaigns could change preferences, though both have costs and may be slow.

Overall, a tax is likely to be effective when demand is elastic enough to respond and the external cost can be estimated reasonably well. Where demand is inelastic or the harm is severe, a tax combined with regulation and education is more likely to reach the socially optimum quantity.

Common mistakeDescribing how the tax works and stopping there. Evaluation needs a reasoned judgement about when it works well and what it depends on.

Top tips

  • Tell the whole story. Cause, problem, solution. Many answers stop after the welfare loss and forget to show the intervention.
  • End at Q*. After the tax or subsidy, mark the new equilibrium at the socially optimum quantity and say that the welfare loss has gone.
  • Know which curve moves. Costs change with a tax or subsidy, so supply shifts. The demand curve moves only if preferences change (for example through education).
  • Match the direction. Negative externality: too much, tax pushes supply up and left. Positive externality: too little, subsidy pushes supply down and right.
  • Size it at Q*. The tax or subsidy equals the marginal externality at the socially optimum quantity.
  • Label everything. MPB, MPC, MSB or MSC, Q1, Q*, P1, P*, and the welfare loss. Refer to them in your text.
  • Evaluate the policy. Measuring the externality, elasticity, who bears the tax, the cost of a subsidy, and the risk of government failure.
