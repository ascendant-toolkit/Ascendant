The Price Effect: Substitution and Income Effects

How a price change is split into a substitution effect along the original curve and an income effect to a new curve, with the compensating budget line, for price falls and price rises. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

When the price of X falls, the total change in the quantity of X is the price effect, and economists split it into two. The substitution effect (SE) is the move along the original indifference curve to the new relative prices, found with a compensating budget line parallel to the new line; it always raises the quantity of the cheaper good. The income effect (IE) is the move from that point to the new equilibrium on a higher curve, caused by the rise in real income. For a normal good both effects raise quantity, so the demand curve slopes down.

1 · A change in price: substitution effect and income effect

When the price of X falls, two things happen. Economists separate them with a compensating budget line: a dashed line parallel to the new budget line, just touching the original indifference curve.

Substitution effect (SE)

X is now relatively cheaper, so the consumer switches towards X, staying on the same indifference curve (same satisfaction). The SE is always in the same direction: a fall in the price raises the quantity of X.

Income effect (IE)

The price fall raises real income: the consumer can reach a higher curve. For a normal good they buy more X. For an inferior good they buy less X as they can afford better alternatives.

Every Giffen good is inferior, but most inferior goods are not Giffen. The income effect has to be both negative and larger than the substitution effect. See the Giffen Paradox page in this chapter's Resources for a worked example.

2 · The numbers for a normal good

Income $120, Py = $2, and Px falls from $4 to $2. The consumer moves from A (15 X) to B (30 X), a rise of 15. The compensating line is parallel to the new budget line and touches the original curve at C, 21.2 X. So the substitution effect is +6.2 (A to C) and the income effect is +8.8 (C to B), and 6.2 + 8.8 = 15.

3 · A price rise

For a price rise the same logic runs in reverse: the budget line pivots inwards, the consumer is poorer in real terms, the SE moves along the original curve away from X, and the IE for a normal good cuts quantity further. A tax on a good works like a price rise, which is why sales tax questions use this diagram.

4 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. The numbers are simple on purpose, to show the idea.

A sales tax on soft drinks

A tax raises the price of the drink, so the budget line pivots inwards around the intercept on the other good. The consumer moves to a lower indifference curve and buys fewer drinks.

Exam use: the Oct/Nov 2020 question asked for the effect of a sales tax with indifference curves. Describe the pivot, then the substitution and income effects, then comment on elasticity.

A promotional price cut on a luxury and on a basic good

A shop cuts the price of both a luxury item and a cheap staple. For the luxury both effects raise demand. For the cheap staple the income effect could pull the other way.

Exam use: matches the Oct/Nov 2021 question comparing a luxury and an inferior good.

A rise in rent

When rent rises, tenants can afford less of everything else (the income effect) and may switch to cheaper housing or move out (the substitution effect). Both effects cut quantity for a normal good.

Exam use: a price rise: budget line pivots inwards, SE and IE both reduce quantity of a normal good.

Practice (not a past paper question) 12 marks Using an indifference curve diagram, explain the income and substitution effects of a fall in the price of a normal good.
PlanDefine both effects. Diagram: BL1, IC1, A; BL2, IC2, B; compensating line, C. Describe SE (A to C) then IE (C to B). Say both raise quantity for a normal good.
KANE

The substitution effect is the change in the quantity demanded caused by the change in relative prices, with satisfaction held constant. The income effect is the change in quantity caused by the change in real income that follows the price change.

Suppose the price of good X falls. The budget line pivots outwards around the intercept on the Y axis and the consumer moves from equilibrium A on IC1 to equilibrium B on a higher curve IC2. To separate the effects, draw a compensating budget line parallel to the new budget line that just touches IC1 at point C.

The move from A to C is the substitution effect: X is now relatively cheaper, so the consumer is likely to buy more X and less Y while staying on the same indifference curve. The move from C to B is the income effect: the price fall raises real income, so the consumer reaches a higher curve. For a normal good, such as restaurant meals, a higher real income may lead to more being bought, so the income effect also raises quantity. The two effects therefore reinforce each other and the demand curve slopes downward, although the size of each effect depends on the shape of the indifference curves, which may differ between consumers.

Top tips

  • Split the price effect in order: substitution effect first (along the original curve, dashed line), income effect second (to the new curve).
  • The SE is always in the same direction. The sign of the IE decides the type of good.
  • Label the three points A, B and C and name each move: A to C is the substitution effect, C to B is the income effect.
  • The compensating line is parallel to the new budget line and touches the original curve. Do not draw it through B.
