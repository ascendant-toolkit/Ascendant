Price Elasticity of Demand

2.2.1: calculating and interpreting PED, its determinants and its link to total revenue. Animated demand curves, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how responsive the quantity demanded is to a change in price: PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price. It is normally negative because demand slopes downward, and what matters is its size: demand is elastic if the size is above 1 (quantity changes by a bigger percentage than price), inelastic if it is between 0 and 1, unitary if it equals 1, perfectly inelastic (0) and perfectly elastic (infinite) at the extremes. It is determined by the availability and closeness of substitutes, whether the good is a necessity or a luxury, the proportion of income spent on it, habit or addiction, how narrowly the market is defined and the time period. PED tells a firm what a price change may do to total revenue: with elastic demand a price cut may raise revenue, with inelastic demand a price rise may raise revenue.

  • Total revenue (total expenditure) = price × quantity. A price cut raises revenue if demand is elastic, lowers it if inelastic, and leaves it unchanged if unitary.
  • Along a straight-line demand curve PED changes: it is elastic at high prices, unitary at the mid-point and inelastic at low prices, even though the slope is constant.

2 · Determinants of PED

  • Substitutes: the more and the closer the substitutes, the more elastic demand. A single brand has more elastic demand than the product as a whole.
  • Necessity or luxury: necessities tend to be inelastic, luxuries elastic.
  • Proportion of income: the larger the share of income, the more elastic.
  • Habit or addiction: tends to make demand more inelastic.
  • Time: demand tends to be more elastic in the long run, as people find substitutes.
  • Definition of the market: the narrower the definition, the more elastic.

3 · Why it matters

  • Firms: pricing decisions and forecasting revenue; price discrimination is easier when elasticities differ between groups.
  • Government: an indirect tax raises more revenue on goods with inelastic demand; taxes on demerit goods may do little to cut consumption if demand is inelastic.
  • Producers' limits: firms may find it hard to control the determinants of PED, for example by building brand loyalty or reducing substitutes.
Exam tipWrite the formula, say percentage change (not "large or small" changes), and always link elasticity to total revenue for both a price rise and a price cut. Use "may" and "likely".

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Petrol and fuel

Drivers need fuel to commute, and there are few close substitutes in the short run, so demand is likely to be inelastic. A price rise may raise revenue for sellers, and tax on fuel raises a lot of revenue.

Exam use: an inelastic necessity.

A named brand of coffee

If one brand raises its price, customers can switch to many other brands, so demand for that brand is likely to be elastic, even though demand for coffee in general may be inelastic.

Exam use: substitutes and the definition of the market.

Cinema ticket discounts

A cinema cuts ticket prices on quiet nights to fill seats. If demand is elastic, the rise in tickets sold outweighs the lower price and revenue could rise.

Exam use: elastic demand and revenue.

Cigarettes and addiction

Habit and addiction make demand more inelastic, so a tax increase may raise revenue but reduce consumption by less than hoped.

Exam use: habit and inelastic demand.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/24 · Q2(a) 8 marks With the help of a formula, explain the meaning of price elasticity of demand and consider the significance to a firm of having price elastic or price inelastic demand for its product.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): it measures the responsiveness of changes in demand to changes in price (1); the correct formula (1); the meaning of price elastic and inelastic in terms of the percentage or proportionate response (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): the impact of rising or falling prices on the demand for a firm's products when PED is inelastic (1) and when it is elastic (1), with both rising and falling prices considered.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a valid judgement on the importance of the distinction between elastic and inelastic demand for the effect of price changes on sales, with a mark for assessing what a firm should do to influence demand or spending.
PlanFormula and meaning. Elastic: price cut raises revenue. Inelastic: price rise raises revenue. Judge: the firm should use elasticity, but other factors matter.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Price elasticity of demand measures how responsive the quantity demanded is to a price change. It equals the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. Demand is elastic if the quantity changes by a greater percentage than price, and inelastic if by a smaller percentage.

If demand is elastic, a fall in price may raise total revenue because the rise in quantity outweighs the lower price, while a rise in price may reduce revenue. If demand is inelastic, a rise in price may raise revenue because quantity falls by less than price rises, while a price cut would reduce revenue.

Knowing whether demand is elastic or inelastic is likely to be very useful in setting prices, as it suggests whether to raise or cut prices to increase revenue. But the estimate may be inaccurate and other factors, such as competitors' responses, could change the outcome.

Price elasticity of demand is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. Demand is elastic if the percentage change in quantity is greater than the percentage change in price, and inelastic if it is smaller.

If demand is elastic, a price rise leads to a larger percentage fall in quantity, so total expenditure falls, and a price cut raises total expenditure. If demand is inelastic, a price rise leads to a smaller percentage fall in quantity, so expenditure rises, and a price cut lowers it.

PED helps an entrepreneur predict the effect on expenditure, but only to the extent that the estimate is accurate. Elasticity can change along the demand curve and over time, and other factors such as rivals' prices may alter the outcome.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/21 · Q2(a) 8 marks Explain two factors that will determine the price elasticity of demand for a particular brand of car and how this price elasticity of demand may change over time.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): a correct definition of PED, the extent to which a change in price leads to a change in quantity demanded (1), and a correct formula (1).
  • Application (up to 6): one factor affecting PED for a particular brand of car, taking into account how it may change over time (up to 3), and a second factor (up to 3). Factors include the availability and price of substitutes, price relative to income, brand loyalty, need or want, and the time period.
PlanDefine and formula. Factor 1: substitutes (many brands, so elastic; may change as brands change). Factor 2: loyalty or proportion of income. Time: more elastic in the long run.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Price elasticity of demand measures the extent to which a change in price changes the quantity demanded. It equals the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.

One factor is the availability of substitutes. With many car makers offering similar models, demand for a particular brand is likely to be elastic, and over time, as new rivals enter, it could become more elastic still. A second factor is the proportion of income spent, since a car is a large purchase, which makes demand more elastic, although as incomes rise this could fall over time. Brand loyalty could make demand less elastic, but may fade if competitors improve their cars.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/22 · Q3(b) 12 marks Discuss the difficulties that businesses might have when they try to control the factors that determine the price elasticity of demand for a product and consider whether attempts to control these factors are likely to be successful.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the difficulties a business might have controlling a factor that influences PED (up to 4 per factor; at least two factors for full marks). Factors include whether the good is a necessity, whether it is habit forming, the availability and attractiveness of substitutes, the proportion of income spent and the time period. Simply explaining the factors without reference to control is not rewarded.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): whether the difficulties can be overcome (up to 3) and a reasoned conclusion (1).
PlanFactors: substitutes, habit, proportion of income, time. Difficulties: firms cannot control rivals or incomes. Evaluate: advertising, loyalty schemes, innovation may help.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A firm may try to reduce the availability of substitutes by differentiating its product through branding, advertising and innovation, but rivals can copy it or launch alternatives, and consumers' tastes are hard to control. A firm may try to make a product habit forming through loyalty schemes or subscriptions, but it cannot easily make a product a necessity or change the proportion of income a household spends on it, which depends on incomes and the firm's own price. Time is also outside the firm's control, as demand tends to become more elastic in the long run.

Attempts to control the factors may be partly successful, especially through brand loyalty and product differentiation, which can make demand less elastic. But the success could be limited by competition and by consumer incomes, so the firm is unlikely to control elasticity fully.

Top tips

  • Write the formula. % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price.
  • Use percentages. Not 'big' or 'small' changes.
  • Link to revenue. Both a price rise and a price cut.
  • Give the determinants. Substitutes, necessity, income share, habit, time.
  • Drop the minus sign. Talk about size: above or below 1.
  • Apply it. Name a product and say why.
  • Evaluate. Estimates change; other factors matter.
