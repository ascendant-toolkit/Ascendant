Absolute and Comparative Advantage

See the numbers. Three worked examples with tables, animated charts and labs show who should make what, and why even a country that is worse at everything can gain from trade.

The big idea

Two countries trade because each is better placed to make something. But "better" can mean two different things. Absolute advantage asks who produces more. Comparative advantage asks who gives up less to produce it. Trade is based mainly on comparative advantage.

Absolute advantage

A country can produce a good using fewer resources (or produce more with the same resources) than another country.

Test: compare output per worker. The bigger number wins.

Output per worker: bigger = absolute advantage

Comparative advantage

A country can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country.

Test: work out what is given up for one unit. The smaller sacrifice wins.

Opportunity cost: smaller = comparative advantage

Singapore gives up less to make electronics, so its comparative advantage is electronics. Indonesia gives up less to make textiles, so its comparative advantage is textiles, even though Singapore makes more textiles per worker. Comparative advantage changes

Opportunity costs move as costs change: new resources, new technology, better education and training, higher productivity. The USA and the UK once had a comparative advantage in steel; now it lies with countries such as Brazil, China and Malaysia. India has one in call centres, helped by ICT skills and English.

Exam-style questions

Q1 Define 2 marks Define comparative advantage.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Comparative advantage is when a country can produce a good or service at a lower opportunity cost than another country.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain the difference between absolute advantage and comparative advantage.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Point 1 · 2 marksA country has an absolute advantage when it can produce a good using fewer resources than another country. This is found by comparing output per worker, so one country could be better at everything.
Point 2 · 2 marksA country has a comparative advantage when it can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost. This is found by working out what is given up in each country, and it may let a country that is less productive in everything still gain from specialising and trading.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Country P can produce 8 tonnes of wheat or 4 tonnes of rice per worker. Country Q can produce 6 tonnes of wheat or 2 tonnes of rice per worker. Analyse whether P and Q could both gain from trade.
ReminderWork out the opportunity cost of one product in each country. Say who has the comparative advantage, then explain the gain.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Point 1 · 2 marksP has an absolute advantage in both wheat and rice, because it produces more of each per worker. This could suggest P has nothing to gain from trading with Q.
Point 2 · 2 marksIn P, 1 tonne of rice costs 2 tonnes of wheat (8 ÷ 4), but in Q it costs 3 tonnes (6 ÷ 2). P therefore has the lower opportunity cost of rice, so a comparative advantage in rice.
Point 3 · 2 marksQ gives up only 1/3 tonne of rice per tonne of wheat, compared with 1/2 in P, so Q has the comparative advantage in wheat. If P specialises in rice and Q in wheat and they trade at a price between 2 and 3 tonnes of wheat per tonne of rice, both could end up with more of both goods than before.
Q4 Discuss 6 marks Discuss whether a country with an absolute advantage in every product should trade.
ReminderFor: opportunity costs differ so gains remain. Against or limits: transport costs, dependence, no gain if opportunity costs are equal.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A country with an absolute advantage in everything can still have a comparative advantage in only some goods. By specialising in the goods with the lowest opportunity cost and importing the rest, it could use its resources more efficiently and consume more than it could alone. For example, a country that is far better at electronics but only a little better at textiles could specialise in electronics and import textiles. However, the gains may be small or cancelled out if transport costs are high, or if the opportunity costs are almost the same. It may also become over-dependent on imports, so the benefit is likely to depend on how different the opportunity costs are and how reliable the trading partner is.

Top tips

  • Always calculate opportunity cost. Divide what you give up by what you gain. Output per worker alone only shows absolute advantage.
  • Say what is given up. "Singapore gives up 5 m of textiles for each electronics unit" is a full statement.
  • Lower opportunity cost wins. The country with the smaller sacrifice has the comparative advantage.
  • Being better at everything is not enough. Both countries can still gain if the opportunity costs differ.
  • Use a simple example. Two countries, two goods, small numbers.
  • Check the price. Both gain only if the price lies between the two opportunity costs.

Common mistakes

MistakeBetter answer
"Singapore is better at both, so it should make both."Compare opportunity costs: Indonesia gives up less to make textiles, so it should specialise there.
"Comparative advantage means the country that makes more."That is absolute advantage. Comparative advantage is the lower opportunity cost.
"A country can have a comparative advantage in both goods."With two countries and two goods, it has one in only one good, unless the opportunity costs are equal.
Dividing the wrong way round.Opportunity cost of A = amount of B given up ÷ amount of A gained.
