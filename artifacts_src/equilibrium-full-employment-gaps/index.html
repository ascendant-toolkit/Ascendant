Full Employment, Equilibrium and Inflationary and Deflationary Gaps

9.1.3 and national income determination: the Keynesian 45° diagram, the full employment level of income, inflationary and deflationary gaps and how to close them. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

National income settles where aggregate expenditure (AE) equals output, which on the Keynesian 45° diagram is where the AE line crosses the 45° line, and this equilibrium need not be at the full employment level of national income. If AE at full employment is too low, there is a deflationary gap: output and jobs are below capacity. If AE at full employment is too high, there is an inflationary gap: demand exceeds what the economy can produce, so the price level rises. The size of either gap is the vertical distance between AE and the 45° line at full employment, and the injection needed to close it is that gap, which the multiplier then magnifies into the required change in national income.

1 · How national income is determined

Aggregate expenditure is the total planned spending at each level of national income: AE = C + I + G + (X − M). If AE is greater than current output, stocks run down, firms produce more and hire more, so output rises. If AE is less than output, stocks build up, firms cut output and jobs, so output falls. Output stops changing where AE = Y, shown on the 45° line (every point on it has expenditure equal to income).

  • The AE line slopes up because consumption and imports depend on income. Its slope is the marginal propensity to spend on domestic output, MPC × (1 − mrt) − mpm in an open economy with a government.
  • Where it cuts the vertical axis is autonomous spending: spending that does not depend on income (autonomous consumption, most investment, government spending, exports).
  • A change in autonomous spending shifts the whole AE line. A change in income is a movement along it.
  • The income approach says the same thing in another way: equilibrium is where leakages equal injections (S + T + M = I + G + X), because only then is the flow of income not growing or shrinking.

2 · Equilibrium and full employment are different things

The full employment level of national income (YF) is the output the economy can produce when all resources are in use (potential output). Equilibrium is wherever AE meets the 45° line. Keynesians argue that equilibrium can sit below YF for a long time because wages and prices are slow to adjust and confidence can stay low. Classical and monetarist economists think the economy returns to YF in the long run, which is why the gap analysis is mainly a short-run, Keynesian one.

3 · The deflationary gap

What it is

Equilibrium income is below YF because AE at full employment is less than YF. The deflationary gap is the amount by which AE at YF falls short of YF: the vertical distance between the 45° line and the AE line at YF.

Signs: spare capacity, high unemployment, low or falling inflation, weak growth, a negative output gap.

The Keynesian solution

Raise autonomous spending by the size of the gap: more government spending (financed by borrowing), lower taxes, lower interest rates to lift investment. AE shifts up and the multiplier lifts income by gap × k, which closes the distance to YF.

Limits: time lags, higher government debt, crowding out, and weak confidence may mean the extra spending is saved rather than spent.

4 · The inflationary gap

What it is

AE at YF is greater than YF. The economy cannot produce more than YF in real terms, so the excess demand pulls up the price level instead of output. The inflationary gap is the vertical distance between the AE line and the 45° line at YF.

Signs: labour shortages, rising wages and prices, a positive output gap, a worsening trade balance as imports are sucked in.

The Keynesian solution

Cut autonomous spending by the size of the gap: lower government spending, higher taxes, higher interest rates (to reduce consumption and investment). AE shifts down until it cuts the 45° line at YF.

Limits: cuts and tax rises are unpopular, lags may mean the policy arrives when the economy is already slowing, and supply-side policy may be a better long-run answer than squeezing demand.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Japan from the late 1990s

For more than two decades Japan had very low growth and spells of falling prices. Households and firms saved rather than spent, even when interest rates were close to zero and the government spent heavily, which suggests a lack of confidence kept expenditure below the level needed for full employment.

Exam use: a long-lived deflationary gap. Evaluate how far more government spending could close it when confidence is weak.

Reopening after a pandemic

When restrictions ended, demand in many economies recovered faster than firms could hire workers and rebuild supply chains. Shortages and rising prices followed in several countries.

Exam use: demand running ahead of potential output, like an inflationary gap. Say that supply constraints, not only demand, may have been behind inflation.

A recession and a stimulus package

In a deep recession a government may announce extra spending on construction and public services, financed by borrowing. If it is large enough and households spend rather than save the extra income, output could return towards full employment.

Exam use: closing a deflationary gap with fiscal policy. Use the multiplier and mention debt and lags as limits.

An overheating boom

In a boom a central bank may raise interest rates and a government may reduce spending growth to cool demand. These measures reduce AE, but they work with a lag and could push the economy into a downturn if overdone.

Exam use: closing an inflationary gap, and the risk of overshooting.

Practice (not a past paper question) 8 marks An economy has equilibrium national income of $600bn, full employment income of $800bn and a multiplier of 4. Explain what type of gap exists and calculate the injection needed to close it.

A deflationary gap exists because the equilibrium level of national income is below the full employment level, so aggregate expenditure at full employment is too low and the economy has spare capacity. The gap in income is $800bn minus $600bn, which is $200bn. Because the multiplier is 4, the required change in aggregate demand is $200bn divided by 4, which is $50bn.

An extra $50bn of government spending could be injected into the circular flow. The people who receive it may spend most of it, and those who receive that spending spend again, so income could rise by four times the injection, which would be enough to reach full employment. However, this would only work if households and firms spend rather than save the extra income, and the policy may take time and add to government debt.

Top tips

  • Draw the 45° diagram. Axes: income on the horizontal, aggregate expenditure on the vertical. Label the 45° line, the AE line, the equilibrium Y and the full employment YF.
  • Mark the gap at YF. It is the vertical distance between the AE line and the 45° line at full employment, not the horizontal distance between Y and YF.
  • Use the multiplier the right way round. Required change in AD = gap in income ÷ multiplier. The multiplier turns a small injection into a larger change in income.
  • Say which direction the policy goes. A deflationary gap needs more spending, lower taxes or lower interest rates. An inflationary gap needs the opposite.
  • Do not call it a gap in output only. A deflationary gap is a shortage of expenditure. An output gap is the difference between actual and potential output. They are linked but not the same.
  • Evaluate with a Keynesian and a monetarist view. Keynesians think the economy can stay below full employment. Monetarists expect it to return to full employment, so they favour supply-side policy.
  • Use 'could' about the outcome. The effect of a policy depends on confidence, lags and the size of the multiplier.
