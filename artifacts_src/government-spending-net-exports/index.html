Government Spending and Net Exports

9.1.2: the determinants of government spending and net exports as components of aggregate demand and how a change in either feeds through the multiplier. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and exam questions.

This part in four sentences

Government spending (G) is not driven by income the way consumption is. It is set by policy choices, the stage of the business cycle, the size of the state, the population and unexpected events such as disasters or conflict. Net exports (X − M) are exports minus imports, and depend on price and quality competitiveness (productivity, relative inflation and the exchange rate) and on incomes at home and abroad. Both are injections or withdrawals that shift aggregate demand, so any change in them is magnified by the multiplier. For the exam you need the determinants of each, and a view on how far each can be controlled.

1 · Government spending

What decides how much a government spends

  • The business cycle: a government may raise spending in a downturn to lift AD and employment (and automatic stabilisers such as benefits rise on their own).
  • The view of the state: spending is a higher share of GDP where the government sees a greater risk of market failure and provides education, healthcare and pensions.
  • Technology: new equipment for schools and hospitals, and more complex treatments, push up the cost of public services.
  • Population: an ageing population raises health and pension spending. A young or growing one raises school and housing spending. Net immigration may raise spending and also raise tax revenue if the migrants find jobs.
  • Unexpected events: natural disasters, pandemics and military conflict.
  • The budget position: how much the government can raise in tax or borrow, and the cost of servicing existing debt.

Why it matters for AD

  • G is a direct injection: every dollar spent adds to AD at once, unlike a tax cut, where part of the extra income may be saved.
  • It is mostly autonomous: it does not move automatically with income (apart from benefits), so a change in G shifts the AE line.
  • The final effect is G × multiplier, which is smaller if the money goes on imports or if the extra income is heavily taxed.
  • It could crowd out private spending if it is financed by borrowing that raises interest rates.
Types of spendingCurrent spending (wages, goods and services, benefits) is used up in the year. Capital spending (roads, schools, hospitals) adds to the economy's productive capacity, so it can raise AS as well as AD in the long run. Transfer payments (pensions, benefits) are not included in G in the national accounts, because nothing is produced in exchange, but they do raise households' disposable income and consumption.

2 · Net exports

Net exports = exports (X) − imports (M). When X is greater than M there is a trade surplus and net exports add to AD. When M is greater than X, net exports are negative and subtract from it.

Price competitiveness

  • Relative productivity: if a country's output per worker rises faster than its rivals', unit costs fall and exports become cheaper.
  • Relative inflation: a lower inflation rate than trading partners makes exports cheaper in foreign markets and imports dearer at home.
  • The exchange rate: a fall in value makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home, which could raise net exports. Whether it does depends on the price elasticities (the Marshall-Lerner condition) and the lag (the J curve).

Quality competitiveness and incomes

  • Quality and design: rises with net investment, education and training, and innovation. These matter more for goods with few close substitutes.
  • Foreign incomes: higher GDP abroad raises demand for exports, especially goods with a high income elasticity of demand.
  • Domestic income: higher home income raises spending on imports (the marginal propensity to import), so a fast-growing economy may see net exports fall.
  • Trade policy: tariffs, quotas, subsidies and trade agreements.

Exports and imports are different kinds of flowExports are an injection and imports are a withdrawal. In the multiplier, imports matter through the marginal propensity to import (mpm). The average propensity to import is imports ÷ income (for example 630 ÷ 3,200 ≈ 0.2 for an economy that imports $630bn of a $3,200bn income), and the marginal propensity to import is the change in imports ÷ the change in income.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Spending after a natural disaster

A government that rebuilds roads, homes and power lines after a hurricane or earthquake spends more than planned. That raises AD and, if there is spare capacity, output and jobs may rise, though the spending may also be financed by higher borrowing.

Exam use: unexpected events as a determinant of G. Link to the multiplier and to the budget deficit.

An ageing population

In countries with a falling death rate and a shrinking working-age share, spending on pensions and healthcare tends to rise while tax revenue growth slows.

Exam use: population as a determinant. Say that spending may rise faster than GDP and that this puts pressure on the budget.

A weaker currency and exports

When a currency loses value, exporters can cut prices abroad or earn more per sale in their own currency. Imports become dearer, so households may buy fewer of them. In practice the effect on net exports may take months to appear.

Exam use: the exchange rate and the Marshall-Lerner condition. Mention the J curve.

A trading partner enters a boom

If an economy's main customers grow faster, their households and firms buy more of its goods, especially luxuries and capital goods with a high income elasticity of demand.

Exam use: foreign income as a determinant. Say that the effect may be smaller for necessities.

Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Net exports are the value of exports minus the value of imports, so a rise in net exports means exports rise by more than imports, or imports fall by more than exports. One factor is a fall in the exchange rate. A lower value of the currency makes exports cheaper for foreign buyers and imports dearer for domestic buyers, so the volume of exports could rise and the volume of imports could fall.

A second factor is higher income in the country's main trading partners. Their households and firms have more to spend, so demand for the country's exports rises, especially for products with a high income elasticity of demand. However, a fall in the exchange rate only raises net exports if the sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports is greater than one, and the effect may take time to appear.

B · Multiple choice questions

Choose an answer, then read the reason. Reworded from the paper and question number shown.

Top tips

  • Give G its determinants in groups. Policy and the cycle, the size of the state, population and technology, and unexpected events.
  • State the direction of each factor. Say whether the change raises or lowers net exports, and why.
  • Split net exports into exports and imports. A change in the exchange rate could change both, so say which effect is larger.
  • Use the right propensity. The marginal propensity to import is the change in imports divided by the change in income. It reduces the multiplier.
  • Mention the elasticity condition. A depreciation may not raise net exports if demand is inelastic, at least in the short run.
  • Be careful with transfer payments. They are not part of G in the national accounts, though they affect consumption.
  • Say what you cannot control. Governments do not decide the foreign income or the exchange rate on their own, so the effect of policy on net exports is uncertain.
