Price Discrimination

The conditions for price discrimination, third degree discrimination in two markets, and who gains and who loses. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Price discrimination is selling the same product at different prices to different groups for reasons not related to cost. It needs four conditions: the firm can set its own price, it can separate the markets, the markets have different price elasticities of demand, and there is no resale between them. In third degree discrimination the firm charges a higher price where demand is inelastic and a lower price where it is elastic, setting MR = MC in each market. Profit rises, some consumers gain access at a low price, and others pay more, so the effect on consumers is mixed.

1 · How price discrimination works

Selling the same product at different prices to different groups, for reasons not related to cost.

Needed: (1) the firm can set its own price, (2) it can separate the markets, (3) different price elasticities of demand, (4) no resale between markets

How it works (third degree)

Charge a higher price where demand is inelastic (business travellers, adults) and a lower price where demand is elastic (students, early bookers). Set MR = MC in each market. Profit may be higher than with one price.

Forms

  • First degree: each buyer pays the most they will pay (rare)
  • Second degree: price depends on quantity (bulk, tariffs)
  • Third degree: different prices for different groups or times

Possible benefits

  • Higher profit, which may fund R&D
  • Some consumers (low price market) can now afford the product
  • Output may rise, and a loss-making service may survive
  • Can help exporters (low prices abroad)

Possible costs

  • Consumer surplus is transferred to the producer
  • Higher price for those with inelastic demand
  • Seen as unfair, may need monitoring and separation costs
  • Possible misallocation of resources

2 · Two markets in numbers

A cinema has adults with demand P = 90 − Q and students with demand P = 50 − Q, and a marginal cost of $10. With MR = MC in each market:

Adults (inelastic)Students (elastic)Total
One price of $40Output 50, profit $1,500Output 10, profit $300Profit $1,800
Two pricesPrice $50, output 40, profit $1,600Price $30, output 20, profit $400Profit $2,000

Profit rises by $200. Students pay less and buy twice as many tickets; adults pay more and buy fewer. Total consumer surplus falls, because the price rise for adults costs more than the price fall gives to students.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Cinemas, trains and airlines

Students, children and pensioners often pay less than adults for the same seat. Airlines charge more for last-minute bookings and less for early ones. Business travellers tend to have more inelastic demand and often cannot choose dates, so the firm may set a higher price for them.

Exam use: a ready example of third degree price discrimination. State the conditions (set price, separate markets, different PED, no resale) and link to MR = MC in each market.

Rail fares by time of day

Commuters who must travel at peak times have inelastic demand and pay more, while leisure travellers can choose off-peak times and pay less. The train company separates the markets by the time on the ticket and cannot resell between them.

Exam use: third degree discrimination by time, with a separable market and different elasticities.

Student and senior discounts

Students and pensioners often have lower incomes and more elastic demand, so many firms offer them a discount that is checked with an ID card, which keeps the markets separate.

Exam use: the condition of separating markets and the effect on consumers and profit.

KANE

Price discrimination is the practice of charging different prices to different groups of consumers for the same product, where the difference is not due to differences in cost. It requires the firm to have some market power so it can set its own price, to be able to separate the markets so that the product cannot be resold, and for the price elasticity of demand to differ between the markets.

Producers are likely to benefit more from price discrimination because it allows them to capture consumer surplus. An airline could charge business travellers, whose demand is inelastic, a high price and students, whose demand is elastic, a low price. Setting MR = MC in each market is likely to give a higher total profit than a single price. The extra profit may be used for research and development or to keep a service running that would otherwise close. However, the gain depends on the cost of separating the markets and on the risk of resale, which could remove the benefit.

Some consumers may also benefit. Students in the elastic market pay a lower price than under a single price and may be able to buy a product they could not afford before, so total output could rise. Consumers in the inelastic market pay more and lose consumer surplus, so the effect on consumers as a whole is mixed and could be negative if the firm has strong market power.

Overall, producers are likely to benefit more because they gain revenue from both markets and capture more of the consumer surplus, while consumers are split between winners and losers. The balance could change if regulation limits the practice or if competition forces the firm to pass on some of its gains.

May/June 2021 · 9708/41 and 43 · Q4(b) 13 marks 'Price discrimination is always possible but never desirable.' Do you agree with this opinion?
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explain price discrimination, then test "always possible" (separate markets, different elasticities of demand: not always met).
  • Test "never desirable": potential benefits, for example a firm that could otherwise make a loss may keep operating and avoid shutting down.
  • Top level: clear analysis of the possibility and the benefits.
Two strong words"Always" and "never" are both wrong in most cases. Show one case where it is not possible (no separation, resale) and one where it is desirable (a service survives).
Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/42 · Q3(b) 13 marks Discuss the extent to which a firm's ability to operate a policy of price discrimination is determined by the market structure in which that firm operates.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Define and explain price discrimination and the necessary conditions: ability to set price, separate markets, different PED.
  • Link to market structures: monopoly and oligopoly (and monopolistic competition) have price-setting power. A perfectly competitive firm is a price taker, so it cannot.
  • A conclusion for 12 or 13.
PlanDefine, then conditions. Perfect competition: cannot. Monopoly: can. Oligopoly and monopolistic competition: some ability. Evaluate: market structure is not the only factor. Separation of markets and different elasticities matter as well.
