Trade, Investment, MNCs and FDI

11.5.2 to 11.5.4: trade and investment between rich and poor countries, what multinational companies do, foreign direct investment and its benefits and costs for host and home countries. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Trade and investment link economies at different levels of development: trade lets countries specialise, but rich-country barriers and falling primary prices can hold poor countries back, while investment flows as portfolio investment (shares and bonds) or foreign direct investment (FDI), which is the purchase or building of real productive assets abroad. A multinational company (MNC) is a firm that owns or controls production in more than one country, and its FDI can bring capital, jobs, technology, skills, exports and tax revenue to the host country. The costs can include profit repatriation, transfer pricing, environmental damage, pressure on local firms and dependence, so the net effect depends on the host's policies and conditions.

1 · Trade and investment between rich and poor countries (11.5.2)

  • Gains from trade: specialising by comparative advantage, access to larger markets, economies of scale, cheaper imports of capital goods and technology, and competition that raises efficiency. For many developing countries, exports are a main source of foreign exchange.
  • Obstacles for developing countries: tariffs and quotas, and especially subsidies to farmers in high-income countries that depress world prices; tariff escalation (low tariffs on raw materials but high tariffs on processed goods, which discourages processing); dependence on primary products with falling or volatile prices; weak transport and infrastructure; and a lack of finance and skills.
  • Helps: preferential access schemes (lower tariffs for poorer countries), regional trade agreements, fair trade schemes and trade-related technical assistance.
  • Investment flows: portfolio investment (buying shares and bonds) can be sold quickly and is volatile ("hot money"); FDI is long-term, builds productive capacity and is less volatile. Emerging economies attract more of both than low-income countries.

2 · Multinational companies (11.5.3)

  • Definition: a firm that has operations (factories, offices, mines or shops) in two or more countries, usually controlled from a home country headquarters.
  • Activities: producing and selling in several countries; moving production to low-cost locations; research and development; raising finance worldwide; managing global supply chains; transfer pricing (setting prices for transactions between subsidiaries); lobbying governments.
  • Why they locate abroad: lower labour and land costs, access to raw materials, access to large markets, lower taxes and fewer regulations, avoiding trade barriers, and following customers.

3 · Foreign direct investment (11.5.4)

  • Definition: an investment by a firm in one country in real assets or a lasting controlling interest (often 10% or more of the shares) in a business in another country. Greenfield FDI builds a new plant; brownfield FDI buys an existing firm.
  • Consequences for a developing host country: Capital inflow (financial account improves) and higher investment, a multiplier effect on income and jobs; technology and skills that raise productivity (shifting AS right); export growth and import substitution that may improve the current account; higher tax revenue. But profit repatriation and imports of machinery and materials worsen the current account (primary income and goods), the investment may be in enclave industries with few links to the local economy, local firms can be crowded out, and environmental and social costs may rise.
  • What attracts FDI: infrastructure, a skilled and low-cost workforce, political and economic stability, market size, access to resources, tax incentives, open trade and secure property rights.
  • Balance of payments effect over time: in the short run the capital inflow improves the financial account and imports of capital goods worsen the current account; later, exports may rise, but repatriated profits flow out.
Evaluation pointWhether MNCs and FDI help growth and development depends on the sector (resource extraction versus manufacturing), how much is bought locally, the terms the host negotiates (tax, local content, training, environmental rules) and whether the host has the skills and institutions to absorb the technology. FDI is often one of several routes to growth, along with domestic investment, aid and export promotion.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Car factories in emerging economies

Vehicle makers have built plants in countries such as Mexico, Thailand and Turkey. They bring jobs, supplier industries, training and exports, but also profits sent abroad, imports of parts and a risk of closure if costs change.

Exam use: FDI in manufacturing, the multiplier, exports and the risk of dependence.

Mining and extraction

Foreign mining and oil firms often invest in resource-rich low-income countries. They can bring export earnings and tax revenue, but with few local linkages, profits sent overseas, pollution and little lasting benefit if the resources run out or the contracts are generous.

Exam use: enclave investment and sustainability issues in resource-based FDI.

Palm oil plantations

A multinational food company expanded palm oil plantations in Malaysia and Indonesia, which raised rural incomes and exports. It also led to large-scale clearance of rainforest and loss of habitats, showing how private benefits and external costs can differ.

Exam use: the link between MNC investment, growth and sustainability, as in a recent Paper 4 data set.

Transfer pricing and tax

Some multinationals set the prices of goods and services passed between subsidiaries so that profits show up in low-tax countries. Governments in host countries lose revenue and have tried to counter this with rules on arm's-length pricing.

Exam use: a cost of MNCs to the host country's government revenue.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/41 · Q7(b) 13 marks Assess the view that foreign direct investment remains the key to economic growth in developing countries.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explain FDI, referring to the role of private multinational corporations and how they improve labour skills, build factories and promote research and development.
  • Define economic growth clearly as the increase in real GDP over a period, and consider alternative strategies such as export-led growth, direct government investment and structural change.
  • Compare the importance of FDI with those alternatives and reach a conclusion.
PlanWhat FDI does. How it raises growth. Alternatives. Compare. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

FDI is investment by a foreign firm, usually a multinational, in factories and other real assets in a developing country. It adds to capital, and may bring technology, training and management skills, so productivity could rise and aggregate supply could increase. The extra investment also raises demand through the multiplier, creating jobs, and may raise exports and tax revenue.

However, FDI is not the only route. Export-led growth, government investment in infrastructure and education, and structural change from farming to industry can also raise growth. FDI may have limited benefits if profits are repatriated or if few local suppliers are used. So FDI is probably an important route but not the only key, and works best alongside domestic investment and good institutions.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/42 · Q6 25 marks 'Investment by multinational corporations (MNCs) in a developing country always promotes sustainable economic growth in that country.' Discuss.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Investment and the multiplier effect on the demand for labour and production of goods and services; new techniques and technology and effects on output and the demand for labour.
  • Sustainability, especially resource-based MNCs; effects on growth, the current and capital accounts of the balance of payments, inflation, the rural-urban balance and exchange rates; a production possibility frontier may be used.
  • Level 4 (18 to 25) develops the advantages and disadvantages of MNC investment for sustainable growth and draws a conclusion (maximum 21 without one).
PlanBenefits: investment, multiplier, technology, skills, exports. Costs: profits sent home, resource depletion, pollution, local firms, urban-rural imbalance. Sustainability. Conclude: not always.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

An MNC investing in a developing country may raise growth. The investment adds to capital and demand, and through the multiplier could raise output, income and employment. New technology and training could raise productivity and potential output, and exports may improve the balance of payments. Tax revenue from the firm could fund infrastructure, health and education, which may support growth over a longer period.

However, growth may not be sustainable. Resource-based investment such as mining or palm oil may use up non-renewable resources, damage the environment and leave little once the resources are gone. Profits may be repatriated and transfer pricing may reduce tax revenue, and the firm may drive out local producers or create jobs mainly in cities, widening regional inequality. The result depends on the sector, the host government's rules and how much is bought locally, so the statement is too strong: investment by MNCs does not always promote sustainable growth.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/42 · Q7 25 marks Discuss the ability of multinational corporations to increase living standards in their home country and the other countries in which they operate.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Define an MNC and the standard of living.
  • Analyse the effect of an MNC on its host and its home country and the effects on living standards through employment, investment, exports and imports, remittances, transfer of externalities, urbanisation, tax revenue and spending.
  • Level 4 (18 to 25) discusses effects on both home and host countries; with a conclusion for 22 to 25 marks.
PlanDefine both terms. Host country: jobs, income, technology, tax; costs. Home country: profits and cheaper goods; lost jobs. Compare. Conclude.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A multinational corporation is a firm with operations in more than one country, and the standard of living is the material and non-material well-being of people. In host countries an MNC may create jobs, raise incomes and bring technology and skills, so real incomes and access to goods may rise. It may also pay taxes that finance public services. In the home country the profits sent back raise incomes of shareholders, and cheaper imported goods raise real incomes.

However, host countries may suffer from profit repatriation, pollution, poor working conditions or damage to local firms, and an MNC may use transfer pricing to avoid tax. In the home country, jobs may be lost when production moves abroad. The net effect on living standards depends on the sector, the terms the host negotiates and the strength of regulation, so MNCs may raise living standards, but not for everyone and not automatically.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/42 · Q4 20 marks The presence of multinational corporations in a low-income country always promotes economic growth in that country. Evaluate this statement.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Transfer of skills and knowledge, both managerial and workshop; a larger tax base so the government can invest; short-term (actual) and long-term (potential) growth.
  • The impact of FDI on aggregate supply or the PPF, using the multiplier, circular flow or AD/AS; better education and health and long-term growth.
  • Evaluation: MNCs may export 'dirty' industries and evade legal limits at home; they may destroy indigenous industry, with a negative effect on employment that exceeds the jobs created.
PlanBenefits with a diagram (AS shift). Costs. When it works and when it does not.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

An MNC may raise actual growth by adding investment, which increases aggregate demand through the multiplier, and raise potential growth by transferring technology and skills that shift the PPC outwards or aggregate supply to the right. A bigger tax base may allow the government to spend more on education and health. Over time, trained workers and local suppliers could raise the productivity of the whole economy.

On the other hand, an MNC may avoid strict environmental or employment laws in its home country by moving polluting production abroad, transferring negative externalities to the host. Large-scale production may also destroy local industries, so the loss of jobs could outweigh those created. Profits may be repatriated too. Therefore the statement is too strong: MNCs may promote growth, but the effect depends on the host country's policies and the type of investment.

Top tips

  • Define MNC and FDI separately. MNC: operates in more than one country. FDI: investment in real assets abroad.
  • Draw the AS shift. Technology and training shift AS to the right (or the PPC outwards).
  • Use the multiplier. FDI is an injection that raises income by more than the original amount.
  • List costs too. Profit repatriation, transfer pricing, pollution, crowding out local firms.
  • Split host and home. Effects differ for the country receiving the investment and the country the MNC comes from.
  • Link to the balance of payments. Financial account up, imports of machinery down on the current account, later exports and profit outflows.
  • Conclude with 'depends on'. The sector, local linkages, host policies and governance.
