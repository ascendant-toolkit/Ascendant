Mobility of Labour

9.3.6: geographical and occupational mobility, the factors that affect them, the consequences of low mobility and policies to improve it. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Mobility of labour is how easily workers can move between places (geographical mobility) and between jobs or occupations (occupational mobility). Geographical mobility depends mostly on housing, transport, family ties and the cost and risk of moving; occupational mobility depends mostly on skills, education and training, qualifications and information. Low mobility causes structural and regional unemployment alongside labour shortages, keeps wage differentials wide and raises the natural rate. Governments can raise mobility with retraining, housing, transport and information policies.

1 · Two forms of mobility

Geographical mobility

The ability and willingness of workers to move to a different area or country to take a job.

Example: a worker moving from a high-unemployment region to a city with vacancies.

Occupational mobility

The ability and willingness of workers to change from one type of job or industry to another.

Example: a car-factory worker retraining as an electrician.

3 · Consequences of low mobility

  • Structural and regional unemployment alongside vacancies: unemployment and job vacancies can both rise at the same time because the unemployed do not have the skills or location the vacancies need.
  • Higher natural rate of unemployment and a more rigid labour market.
  • Wage differentials persist between regions and occupations because workers cannot move to where pay is highest. A less elastic supply of labour to an occupation gives a larger wage response to a rise in demand.
  • Misallocation of labour: workers stay in declining sectors while growing sectors have shortages, which holds back growth.
  • Regional inequality: depressed areas lose businesses and tax base.
Is more mobility always better?More mobility can lower unemployment and raise output, but geographical mobility can empty rural areas (brain drain, ageing populations) and put pressure on housing and services where people arrive. Greater occupational mobility needs spending on training and may not be enough if demand for labour is weak.

4 · Policies to improve mobility

  • Retraining and education: training subsidies, apprenticeships and recognised qualifications raise occupational mobility.
  • Housing: build more affordable housing in high-demand areas, give relocation grants and make renting easier.
  • Transport and broadband: shorter commutes and remote working widen the area a worker can reach.
  • Information: job centres, online vacancy platforms and careers guidance.
  • Remove barriers: easier recognition of qualifications, fewer licensing and union restrictions, immigration rules that match skills.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Coal towns and housing

Miners who lost their jobs often owned cheap homes in towns with few other jobs. Selling up and moving to a city meant giving up a house that was worth little and renting somewhere far dearer. Many stayed unemployed.

Exam use: geographical immobility caused by housing and family ties. Link to structural unemployment and regional policy.

Retraining in the energy transition

Programmes in Germany and the US have retrained oil, gas and coal workers as installers of solar panels and wind turbines. Welders, electricians and engineers often have skills that transfer.

Exam use: occupational mobility through retraining. Evaluate on cost, time, the quality of new jobs and whether pay matches the old jobs.

Free movement of labour in the EU

After EU enlargement in 2004, large numbers of workers from Poland and other eastern states moved to the UK, Ireland and Germany where wages were higher. Home countries lost workers; receiving countries filled vacancies.

Exam use: geographical mobility across borders with benefits (higher output and tax receipts) and costs (brain drain, pressure on services). A common theme in Paper 4 data questions.

Remote working

After 2020 many firms allowed staff to work from home. Workers could take jobs in distant cities without moving, which raised effective geographical mobility for some roles.

Exam use: technology that cuts mobility barriers. Limit: it helps only some occupations, and may reduce demand for city-centre services.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/43 · Q1(d) 8 marks Using the information in an article about labour migration, assess whether increasing numbers of migrant workers are an advantage or a disadvantage to the receiving country.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Advantages: fills vacancies and skill shortages, raises potential output (LRAS shifts right), migrants spend and pay taxes (higher AD and revenue), supports public finances, often younger workers, new businesses.
  • Disadvantages: possible downward pressure on wages in low-skill jobs if migrants are substitutes for existing workers, pressure on housing, schools and healthcare, remittances sent abroad, possible fall in unemployment benefits spending in the home country but not the receiving one.
  • A reasoned conclusion that depends on the skills of migrants and the state of the economy.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/42 · Q1(d) 8 marks Migration into a high-income economy was restricted for several years and then returns to its earlier level. Evaluate the likely effect on the economy's macroeconomic performance.
What the mark scheme wants

  • New businesses and jobs may be created. GDP rises, so growth is likely to improve.
  • Against: remittances sent abroad lower net exports and the current account, there may be pressure on wages for competing workers (the crowding out argument) and on public services.

Mobility of labour is the ease with which workers can move between areas or between jobs. One barrier to geographical mobility is the high cost of housing in areas where vacancies exist. A worker who lives in a region with few jobs may be unable to afford to rent or buy a home in a city with vacancies, so they stay unemployed even though firms elsewhere have shortages. This is structural, regional unemployment and it raises the natural rate.

A government could build more affordable housing in high-demand areas, give grants to cover relocation costs or support social housing that is not tied to a place, so that more workers can afford to move.

A second barrier is a lack of transferable skills, which limits occupational mobility. A coal miner whose mine has closed may not have the qualifications to work in other industries. Subsidised retraining and nationally recognised qualifications would let workers move into growing sectors, which reduces structural unemployment and fills vacancies. However, housing takes years to build and retraining is costly, and the retrained workers still need enough vacancies to be hired, so the effect on unemployment may be slow and depends on demand for labour.

Top tips

  • Name the form of mobility. Geographical is moving place. Occupational is changing job or sector. Use the right factors for each.
  • Housing and transport for geographical; skills and training for occupational. Most mixed-up answers swap these.
  • Link immobility to unemployment. Immobility causes structural and regional unemployment and keeps vacancies unfilled.
  • Use a labour market diagram where you can. A fall in demand for a declining industry's workers shifts D left and lowers wage and employment.
  • Mention both sides of migration. Output, tax revenue and filling vacancies against pressure on wages, services and housing.
  • Evaluate policies on time and cost. Retraining and housing take years, and training only helps if there are suitable vacancies.
  • Use 'could'. The effect of migration on wages depends on whether migrants are substitutes or complements for existing workers.
