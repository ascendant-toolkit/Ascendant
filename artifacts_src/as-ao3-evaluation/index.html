AS Level Economics · 9708 · Exam technique

AO3 Evaluation: How Each Mark and Level Is Reached

Exactly what earns 1, 2, 3 and 4 evaluation marks: Table B explained, the levers and the Condition, If, Then method, with examples at every mark for 12 mark, 8 mark and data response questions.

This part in five sentences

AO3 is evaluation: recognising assumptions and limitations of economic information and models, assessing the strengths and weaknesses of arguments, recognising that some decisions involve priorities and value judgements, and communicating reasoned judgements and conclusions based on the arguments. In the 12 mark question it is marked out of 4 in Table B in two levels: Level 1 (1 to 2) is a vague or general conclusion and simple evaluative comments with no development, and Level 2 (3 to 4) is a justified conclusion that addresses the specific requirements of the question together with developed, reasoned and well-supported evaluative comments. In the 8 mark part it is worth 2 marks, in a 6 mark data response part 2 marks, and in a 4 mark part 1 mark. Evaluation marks are only available when the analysis is two-sided: mark schemes say "a one-sided response cannot gain any marks for evaluation". This page shows exactly what earns each mark, with examples.

3 · How to get each mark: 1, 2, 3 and 4

MarkWhat is neededTypical length
1One simple evaluative comment, or a very general conclusion1 sentence
2A simple comment plus a general conclusion, or two simple comments: nothing is developed or supported2 to 3 sentences, about 30 words
3One evaluative point that is developed and reasoned (a condition and why it matters), with a conclusion that addresses the question but is thinly justified; or two developed points with a weak conclusion4 to 6 sentences, about 60 to 80 words
4At least two developed, supported evaluative points and a conclusion that answers the specific question and says why, using the question's own words8 to 10 sentences, about 120 to 150 words

The lengths are a guide to what it takes to develop the points, not a mark scheme rule. A long list of unsupported comments stays at Level 1; a short, well-reasoned judgement can reach Level 2.

4 · One question, four evaluations

Question: Assess whether fixing a minimum price is likely to be the best policy to reduce the consumption of a demerit good. (The analysis is assumed to be two-sided, with alternatives, as the AO2 review shows.)

1 mark (Level 1, about 15 words)

A minimum price may not work if demand for the demerit good is inelastic.

Why 1: a relevant evaluative comment, but it is a bare statement: no reason why elasticity matters and no judgement.

2 marks (top of Level 1, about 35 words)

A minimum price may not work if demand is inelastic, and there are other policies such as taxes. Overall a minimum price is quite a good policy but it has some problems.

Why 2: two simple comments and a conclusion, but the conclusion is vague ("quite good", "some problems") and does not say which policy is best or why. Nothing is developed.

3 marks (Level 2, about 70 words)

Whether a minimum price is the best policy depends on how responsive drinkers are to price. If demand for cheap alcohol among dependent drinkers is price inelastic, a 20% price rise may cut quantity by only 5%, so consumption of the demerit good falls very little while their spending rises. A minimum price is therefore not the best policy for these drinkers.

Why 3: one point is developed and reasoned (condition, figure, consequence) and the conclusion addresses the question. But there is only one evaluative point, and the conclusion does not weigh the policy against the alternatives.

4 marks (top of Level 2, about 140 words)

First, the answer depends on elasticity. If demand is inelastic, as with dependent drinkers, a minimum price cuts consumption very little and the extra revenue goes to retailers; if the main buyers are young or low-income drinkers whose demand is more elastic, consumption falls more. Second, it depends on whether the cause of over-consumption is price or information. Where people underestimate the harm, a price rise alone leaves the information failure in place, so education may have a longer-lasting effect even though it works slowly. Third, an indirect tax would apply to all alcohol and give the government revenue, which a minimum price does not.

Therefore a minimum price is unlikely to be the best policy on its own to reduce consumption of a demerit good. It is most effective for cheap, strong drinks where demand is elastic and is best combined with information and a tax, because the policy that works depends on whether over-consumption is driven by price or by lack of information.

Why 4: at least two developed evaluative points (elasticity, price versus information), each reasoned and supported; the conclusion uses the wording of the question ("best policy", "reduce consumption of a demerit good"), makes a judgement and gives the reason. It does not repeat the analysis; it introduces conditions.

5 · The 8 mark part and data response: 1 or 2 evaluation marks

QuestionWhat earns the evaluation marks (from the mark scheme)Example

6 · The evaluation levers

LeverQuestion to askExample
MagnitudeIs the effect big enough to matter?"A 2% tax rise will change little if demand is inelastic."
TimeDoes the short-run effect differ from the long-run?"Training raises AD at once but raises productivity only after several years."
ElasticityDoes the outcome depend on PED, PES, YED or XED?"A tariff cuts imports only if demand for imports is elastic."
Spare capacityIs the economy near full capacity?"Extra AD raises output if there is spare capacity but mainly raises prices if not."
Context or typeDoes it differ by economy, market or group?"Free trade may suit an economy with strong factor endowments more than one with infant industries."
Trade-off and prioritiesDoes fixing one problem create another? Who gains and loses?"Higher interest rates reduce inflation but may raise unemployment."
AlternativesIs there a better policy for the same aim?"Information tackles the cause; a tax raises revenue."
Assumptions and data limitsWhat does the model or data assume or leave out?"The circular flow model ignores the size and timing of the effects."

7 · The CIT method: Condition, If, Then

  • Condition: name the factor the judgement depends on (a lever above), using the good or the economy in the question.
  • If: say what happens if that condition holds, and what happens if it does not.
  • Then: give the outcome for the claim in the question, with the reason.
  • Repeat for a second condition, then write one sentence that weighs one against the other and answers the question.
Vague (Level 1)Developed (Level 2)
"It depends on many factors.""It depends on the price elasticity of demand for cigarettes among long-term smokers. If it is inelastic, a 20% tax raises revenue but barely reduces consumption, because addicted smokers keep buying; if it is elastic, consumption falls by more than the price rises."
"There are advantages and disadvantages.""The advantage of the tax is revenue and the disadvantage is that it is regressive: poorer smokers pay a larger share of their income, so the policy reduces the externality at a cost to the poorest. The cost matters more if the tax is large."
"Overall it is quite effective.""Overall a minimum price is not the best policy for dependent drinkers, because demand is inelastic, but it can work for cheap drinks where substitutes exist."

8 · Writing the judgement

  • Answer the question that was asked. If it says "best", say which is best. If it says "always", say when it is not. If it says "to what extent", give an extent ("to a limited extent, because...").
  • Use the question's wording in the conclusion so that the examiner can see it addresses the specific requirements.
  • Give the reason and the condition: "...because...; this would change if...".
  • No new analysis and no summary of what you have already said.
  • Keep the scope word in mind: "all", "always", "only" ask you to find a case where the claim fails.

9 · Common ways to lose evaluation marks

  • One-sided analysis, so no evaluation marks are available at all.
  • "It depends" with no named factor, mechanism or judgement.
  • Repeating the analysis under the heading "evaluation".
  • Evaluating a different question: for example discussing whether a policy works when the question asks whether it is the best.
  • Two lists (for and against) never weighed against each other.
  • A conclusion that does not conclude: "overall there are pros and cons".
  • Generic conditions ("the economy", "consumers") instead of the named good, group or country.
Exam tipSave the last 5 or 6 minutes of a 12 mark answer for evaluation. Use two conditions and a conclusion in the question's words. Check each evaluative sentence: does it introduce a condition that has not been mentioned before? If not, it is probably summary.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

The conclusion carries a mark

Mark schemes for 12 mark questions say 'reserve 1 mark for a conclusion' and ask for a judgement that addresses the specific question, such as 'which policy is best' or 'whether a rise or a fall benefits the economy'.

Exam use: writing a conclusion in the question's words.

One-sided means no evaluation marks

A question on whether free trade is always positive had the note 'a one-sided response cannot gain any marks for evaluation'. The same note appears for a surplus on the current account and for tools of protection.

Exam use: both sides before evaluating.

Conditions the mark scheme looks for

Examples of evaluation points in mark schemes: spare capacity, the elasticity of demand, the time period, the size of the change, whether other countries are doing the same, and how open the economy is.

Exam use: choosing levers that examiners expect.

Context-based evaluation

A question about a developing economy carried the note that if the analysis is not in the context of a developing economy, Level 2 is the maximum. Name the type of economy in your conditions and your conclusion.

Exam use: naming the economy in the condition.

Short evaluation in data response

In a 6 mark part examiners award up to 2 marks for evaluative comment, for example that a policy might make the problem worse, or for a conclusion on the extent. A single sentence with a reason can earn a mark.

Exam use: being brief but specific.

Based on Feb/March 2024 · 9708/22 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain what is meant by a depreciation of the exchange rate and consider whether a depreciation is likely to increase domestic real output.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): an accurate definition of the exchange rate (1); a depreciation is likely if the demand for a currency falls (1) or its supply increases (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): a depreciation reduces the perceived price of exports and increases the perceived price of imports (1), which raises net exports (X - M) and aggregate demand (1) and so the level of domestic real output (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): this assumes the economy is not at full capacity (1) or depends on the elasticity of demand for traded goods (1); the second mark is for a justified conclusion.
PlanDefine the exchange rate and depreciation. Cheaper exports, dearer imports: X-M up, AD up, output up. Evaluate: spare capacity, elasticities.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. A depreciation is a fall in the value of a floating currency, which may be caused by a fall in demand for it or an increase in its supply.

A depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home. If demand for them is elastic enough, exports rise and imports fall, so net exports increase. This raises aggregate demand, which could increase real output and employment.

The rise in output depends on spare capacity: if the economy is already near full capacity, the extra AD may raise prices rather than output. It also depends on elasticities, since if the sum of the price elasticities is below one, net exports may fall. Overall, a depreciation could raise output, but not always.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/22 · Q3(b) 12 marks Assess whether fixing a minimum price is likely to be the best policy to reduce the consumption of a demerit good.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): use of minimum pricing to raise the price of a demerit good and reduce consumption; problems of minimum pricing, such as inelastic demand, the persistence of information failure and the emergence of informal markets; and alternative policies such as indirect taxes, education and the provision of information, with their advantages and disadvantages, including ease of introduction, time and cost and reception by consumers.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether a minimum price is likely to be the best policy, with a conclusion.
PlanMinimum price: raises price, cuts consumption if demand elastic; inelastic demand, informal markets. Alternatives: tax, education, information. Judge: a combination may be best.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A minimum price raises the price of cheap, strong products, which should reduce quantity demanded, particularly if demand is elastic. But if demand is inelastic, because the good is addictive, consumption may fall little, and the information failure that causes over-consumption remains. Informal markets may develop, and the extra revenue goes to sellers rather than the government. An indirect tax would raise revenue for the state, education and information tackle the cause of the problem but work slowly.

A minimum price is unlikely to be the best policy on its own. It may work for goods with elastic demand, but a combination of price measures and information is likely to be more effective at reducing consumption.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/22 · Q5(b) 12 marks Assess whether the achievement of a balanced budget should always be a main government macroeconomic objective.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the question asks whether a balanced budget should always be a main objective rather than the main objective, so analysis of other objectives is not essential; a direct comparison of the strengths, weaknesses and uses of a balanced budget with a budget in surplus and in deficit may reach Level 3; a balanced budget may bring stability and encourage domestic and inward investment but may not be flexible enough to deal with unemployment and inflation; a deficit may enable growth or reduce unemployment but may be unsustainable in the long run; a surplus may be needed to reduce inflation but has an opportunity cost in lost government spending.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether a balanced budget should always be a main objective, with a conclusion.
PlanBalanced: stability, confidence, but inflexible. Deficit: growth, jobs, but debt. Surplus: lower inflation, but lost spending. Judge: balance over the cycle.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A balanced budget gives stability and may encourage domestic and foreign investors, since it does not add to debt. But it is inflexible: in a recession the government may need a deficit to raise AD and reduce unemployment, while in a boom a surplus may be needed to control inflation. A deficit can support growth but may be unsustainable, with rising interest costs and crowding out, and a surplus involves the opportunity cost of lower spending.

A balanced budget should not always be a main objective. It is better seen as a long-run aim, with deficits in recessions and surpluses in booms, than a goal in every year, since the right budget position depends on the state of the economy and the level of existing debt.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/23 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess the extent to which economic growth is always beneficial for the people and government in an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): potential benefits, for example higher real GDP per capita and a better quality of life, more jobs as firms need more workers, more tax revenue without raising tax rates so the government can invest in public services such as healthcare and education, and more investment and consumption as expectations about future income and profits rise; potential costs, for example growth that disproportionately benefits the wealthy and widens the gap between rich and poor, and higher pollution and depletion of natural resources.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on the extent to which growth is beneficial to the people and the government, with a conclusion.
PlanPeople: income, jobs, quality of life; inequality, pollution. Government: revenue, spending; costs of services, deficit. Judge: depends on distribution and sustainability.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

For the people, growth raises average incomes, creates jobs and widens choice, which can improve quality of life. For the government, higher incomes raise tax revenue without higher rates, which can fund public services and reduce borrowing. But the gains may go mainly to richer households, widening inequality, and extra production can cause pollution and use up resources. Rapid growth may also raise inflation and the demand for infrastructure.

Growth is usually beneficial, but not always for everyone. The extent depends on how it is shared, whether it is sustainable and whether it comes with inflation or environmental cost.

Based on Feb/March 2024 · 9708/22 · Q5(b) 12 marks Assess whether economic growth is always desirable for an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the meaning of economic growth; the potential benefits for an economy: higher incomes, a reduction in unemployment, improved government finances, increased international competitiveness, increases in consumer and business confidence, increased life expectancy; the costs of economic growth: inflation, current account deficits, inequality, pollution.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): consideration that fully assesses the relative perspectives of economic growth for an economy and arrives at a reasoned conclusion on which viewpoint is most likely.
PlanBenefits: incomes, jobs, tax revenue, competitiveness. Costs: inflation, current account deficit, inequality, pollution. Judge: depends on source and how it is shared.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Economic growth is an increase in real GDP. It usually raises incomes and living standards, creates jobs, and lifts tax revenue, which governments can use to improve health and education, and it may improve confidence and competitiveness. But growth can cause inflation if demand outpaces capacity, raise imports and a current account deficit, widen inequality if the gains go to a few, and cause pollution and resource depletion.

Growth is not always desirable. It is most beneficial when it is supply-led, sustainable and widely shared, and least beneficial when it is demand-led near full capacity or damages the environment. For low-income countries the benefits are likely to outweigh the costs.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q1(c) 4 marks In an attempt to bring about disinflation, a central bank used a contractionary monetary policy. Consider whether disinflation is more harmful than deflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 3): disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation (1), so although the problems of inflation are less they remain, such as the effect on the distribution of income, purchasing power, the competitiveness of exports, menu costs, shoe leather costs and investment planning (1); deflation is a fall in the general price level (1), which can bring problems such as a fall in AD leading to a recession, lower output and higher unemployment, and negative effects on investment (1). A maximum of 2 if only one of the two is considered.
  • Evaluation (up to 1 or 2): for example, the extent of the disinflation, that is, by how much the rate is falling.
PlanDisinflation: prices still rise, smaller problems. Deflation: delayed spending, recession, debt. Judge: deflation is usually more harmful, unless disinflation is deep.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation, so prices still rise and the problems of inflation, such as menu costs and redistribution, remain though they are smaller. Deflation is a fall in the general price level, which can make consumers delay spending, causing a fall in AD, lower output and higher unemployment, and raising the real burden of debt. Deflation is generally more harmful because it can cause a spiral of falling output, whereas disinflation lowers the cost of inflation, unless the fall in inflation is so large that it risks pushing the economy into deflation.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q1(e) 6 marks Assess whether a central bank setting an inflation target as part of its monetary policy is likely to be helpful for its economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 4): possibly helpful, because it helps to create and maintain stability and confidence at a time of significant price increases, informing consumers and firms that action will be taken to stop inflation becoming too high, and it can influence inflation expectations; if these stay stable, it may help to prevent a wage-price spiral (up to 3); possibly unhelpful, because the bank may not be willing to take the measures needed to keep inflation at the target, knowing there is a delicate balance between holding inflation close to a target and the risk of an economic downturn (up to 3).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): relevant evaluation, for example in relation to the particular target adopted, and a reserved mark for a conclusion on whether it is helpful.
PlanHelpful: confidence, expectations, no wage-price spiral. Unhelpful: bank may not act; target may risk a downturn. Judge: credibility decides.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

An inflation target could help by giving consumers and firms confidence that the central bank will act to stop prices rising too quickly, which keeps inflation expectations stable and may prevent a wage-price spiral. But the target may not help if the bank is reluctant to raise interest rates enough, because that could cause a slowdown and higher unemployment.

The target is likely to be helpful if the central bank is credible and independent, and if the target level is realistic. Its benefit depends on whether the bank is willing to meet it at the cost of lower growth.

Top tips

  • Do both sides first. One-sided analysis earns no evaluation marks.
  • Name the condition. Elasticity, time, spare capacity, size, context.
  • Use Condition, If, Then. Reason and consequence, not a bare 'it depends'.
  • Two developed points beat four thin ones. Level 2 needs development.
  • Conclude in the question's words. Best, always, to what extent.
  • Give the reason for the judgement. And say what would change it.
  • No new analysis in the conclusion. Introduce conditions, not summary.
