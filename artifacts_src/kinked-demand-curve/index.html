The Kinked Demand Curve

Why an oligopolist's demand curve bends, why marginal revenue breaks into two pieces with a gap between them, and why a firm's marginal cost can move up and down inside that gap without the price changing. Animated diagrams, labs, drills, real markets and business costs, and exam practice.

The whole idea in five sentences

In an oligopoly a firm's sales depend on how rivals react. The model assumes rivals ignore a price rise (so the firm loses many customers: demand is elastic above the current price) but match a price cut (so the firm gains few customers: demand is inelastic below it). That bends the demand curve at the current price, the kink. Because the two segments have different slopes, marginal revenue is different on each side, and at the kink output it drops vertically: a gap in MR. Marginal cost can rise or fall anywhere inside that gap and still cut MR at the kink output, so the profit-maximising price stays put.

It is MR that drops, not MCMarginal cost does not drop at the kink. It is an ordinary upward-sloping curve that runs straight through. What drops, vertically, is marginal revenue. The MC curve matters because it is cut by MR somewhere in that vertical gap, which is why changes in MC inside the gap have no effect on price or output.

1 · Why the demand curve bends

Suppose a firm charges $10 and sells 5 units. That point is the kink. The firm asks what would happen if it changed price.

Price rise: rivals do not follow

If the firm raises price and rivals keep theirs, its product is now relatively expensive. Customers switch to rivals, so quantity falls by a lot. Demand above the kink is relatively elastic (a flatter line) and the price rise lowers revenue.

Price cut: rivals match

If the firm cuts price, rivals fear losing their own customers and cut too. Nobody gains share, and the extra sales come only from the market growing a little. Demand below the kink is relatively inelastic (a steeper line) and the cut lowers revenue as well.

ResultNeither move pays, so the firm has no incentive to change price. The kink is at the price already being charged, and the model says that price will tend to be sticky.

2 · Where the gap in MR comes from

Marginal revenue is the extra revenue from selling one more unit. It is the price of that unit minus the revenue lost because the price had to fall on all the units already being sold. The steeper the demand curve, the more the price must fall per extra unit, so the bigger the loss and the lower the MR.

MR = price on the extra unit − (fall in price per unit × units already sold)

The two are joined by a vertical dashed line, which is the gap. Each MR line has twice the slope of its demand segment.

3 · Why price stays put when MC changes

A profit-maximising firm produces where MC = MR, with MC cutting MR from below. With a gap in MR, MC can cut MR at the kink output in a whole range of positions.

  • MC passes through the gap: it cuts the vertical part of MR at the kink output (5 units). Output stays at 5, and reading up to the demand curve, price stays at $10.
  • MC shifts up or down but still passes through the gap: same answer. The firm's costs have changed but its price has not. Profit changes, price does not.
  • MC shifts above the gap: now MC cuts the upper MR segment at a lower output, so the firm cuts output and raises price. A large enough cost increase breaks the stickiness.
  • MC falls below the gap: MC cuts the lower MR segment, so output rises and price falls. This needs MC to fall a long way.

4 · What the model can and cannot do

What it explains

Why prices in some oligopolies change rarely, why firms prefer non-price competition (advertising, quality, loyalty schemes) to cutting price, and why a firm may leave price unchanged when its costs move a little.

Limitations to evaluate with

It does not explain how the original price was set. Rivals do not always behave as assumed: when costs rise for the whole industry they are likely to follow a rise. Price leadership and tacit collusion often fit real behaviour better, and studies have found oligopoly prices are not always more rigid than prices elsewhere.

Which business costs sit in MC?

The kink protects price from changes in marginal cost: the cost of producing one more unit. Fixed costs do not change with output, so they never move the MC curve. A rise in a fixed cost therefore leaves the profit-maximising price and output unchanged, with or without a kink. It only squeezes profit.

UK supermarkets

A handful of large chains dominate grocery sales, and each watches the others' prices closely. Several run schemes that match the prices of discounters such as Aldi on selected items: a rival's price cut is copied almost immediately. A chain that lifted prices on its own, with no cost increase to point to, could expect shoppers to move to a rival.

Cost angle: the main MC is what the store pays suppliers for each extra item, plus a little handling. Wage rises that follow the annual minimum wage increase, and energy for chillers, are largely shared by every chain, so they tend to raise shelf prices together instead of sitting inside one firm's gap.

Exam use: price matching shows rivals following a cut. Use it to justify the inelastic lower segment, then evaluate: when all chains face the same cost increase they are likely to raise prices together.

Airlines on the same route

When one carrier discounts fares on a busy route, rivals often respond quickly with their own offers, because airline fares are visible and compared online. A single airline raising fares without a cost reason risks losing passengers to the others, especially where the products are close substitutes.

Cost angle: with empty seats, the MC of one more passenger is very low (a meal, airport fees, a little extra fuel, a passenger tax). That puts MC near the bottom of the gap, so large swings in fuel prices still move the average cost of a flight a lot, but do not reach a seat that would otherwise fly empty. When jet fuel rises sharply for every airline, they tend to add surcharges or lift fares at about the same time.

Exam use: a good example of why the lower side of the gap is bounded by MR near zero, and of how an industry-wide cost shock breaks the assumption that rivals ignore a rise.

Chocolate and confectionery

A few multinational firms supply most of the branded chocolate bought in shops. Cocoa prices reached record highs in 2024, and the major brands raised prices and also reduced pack sizes. When the input cost rises for all of them at once, the model's assumption that rivals will not follow a rise no longer fits.

Cost angle: cocoa is a direct cost per bar, so a large increase lifts MC well above the gap. Reducing pack size ("shrinkflation") lets a firm adjust its cost per unit sold without changing the headline price that consumers compare.

Exam use: a large cost shock that lifts MC above the gap, for a whole industry. Contrast it with a small cost drift that would leave price unchanged.

Petrol retailing

Neighbouring stations post their prices on large signs, so a price cut is spotted and copied within hours. Small changes in a station's running costs rarely change the pump price; changes in the wholesale price of fuel, which affect every station, do.

Cost angle: the wholesale price paid for each litre is the dominant part of MC. Station rent and staff salaries are fixed costs and do not appear in MC at all.

Exam use: an example of price visibility that helps price matching, and of why MC changes common to all firms spread through the market.

Mobile phone networks

A small number of networks supply most UK mobile contracts. Several have applied annual price rises at similar times, which is closer to tacit collusion or following a price leader than to rivals ignoring a rise.

Cost angle: the cost of carrying one more customer is small compared with the large fixed cost of building and maintaining the network. Network costs are fixed, so they should not explain price rises in the kinked demand model.

Exam use: a limitation. If rivals follow rises, the upper segment is not elastic and there is no kink.

Q1 Explain 6 marks Explain, with the help of a diagram, why a firm facing a kinked demand curve has a gap in its marginal revenue curve.
ReminderDraw D with a kink at P₀ and Q₀, the flatter segment above and steeper below. Draw MR for each segment starting from the vertical axis and mark the vertical gap below Q₀. Link the size of the gap to the change in slope.

A kinked demand curve shows demand for an oligopolist's product that is more elastic above the current price P₀ than below it. Above P₀ rivals are assumed not to follow a price rise, so quantity falls sharply and the segment is relatively flat; below P₀ rivals are assumed to match a price cut, so quantity rises by little and the segment is steeper.

Marginal revenue is the price of an extra unit less the revenue lost on units already sold, and this loss is greater the steeper the demand curve. At output Q₀ the MR on the flatter upper segment is therefore higher than the MR on the steeper lower segment, so MR falls vertically at Q₀ and the curve is discontinuous. The size of the gap could depend on how much the slopes differ: the more strongly rivals react to a cut, the bigger the gap. For example, if demand above the kink is P = 14 − 0.8Q and below it is P = 20 − 2Q, MR at Q₀ = 5 is $6 on the upper segment and $0 on the lower one.

Common mistakeSaying that "MC drops" or "the demand curve has a gap". The gap is in MR, not in D or MC. Also: drawing a single MR line instead of two separate segments.
Q2 Analyse 8 marks An airline operates on a route served by three other carriers. Analyse, using a kinked demand curve diagram, why it might leave its fares unchanged when its costs rise slightly.
ReminderDraw the full diagram, then shift MC up and show it still passes through the gap. Say "slightly": the explanation fails if MC leaves the gap. Use the route as application.

The airline is an oligopolist, so its sales depend on how rivals react to its fares. On a route with only a few carriers, rivals could be expected to match a fare cut to protect their passengers, but may leave their fares alone if the airline raises its own. Demand above the current fare is therefore likely to be elastic and below it inelastic, giving a kink and a gap in marginal revenue.

Profit is maximised where MC = MR, and a slight rise in costs shifts MC upward but it could still cut the vertical part of the MR curve at the same output. Output and the fare are then unchanged, and the airline avoids a fare rise that could lose it passengers to rivals, even though its profit per passenger is lower. With empty seats the extra cost of one more passenger is low, which may keep MC inside the gap even when fuel prices move.

If the cost rise were large, such as a sharp rise in jet fuel affecting every carrier, MC could leave the gap and rivals face the same increase, so fares may rise across the route.

Common mistakeExplaining the kink well but not applying it to a cost change. The marks are for MC moving within the gap and price not changing.
Q3 Evaluate 12 marks Evaluate the usefulness of the kinked demand curve model in explaining how firms in oligopoly set prices.
ReminderStart by explaining what the model does explain (about 4 marks), then two or three limitations (about 8 marks including a judgement). A judgement that says "it depends on..." and states on what, scores better than "it is somewhat useful".

The model assumes rivals ignore a price rise but match a price cut, so demand is elastic above the current price and inelastic below it, which breaks the MR curve. Because MC can move within the gap without changing output, price is likely to stay the same for long periods, and firms could prefer non-price competition such as advertising or loyalty schemes. This fits markets where prices are visible and easy to compare, such as supermarkets or petrol stations, where price cuts tend to be copied quickly.

However, the model only explains why price might stay the same, not how the original price was set, so it is an incomplete theory of pricing. Its main assumption may not hold: when the whole industry faces a cost increase, rivals are likely to follow a price rise, so the upper segment is not elastic. Large cost shocks, such as a jump in raw material prices, can move MC out of the gap. In some markets firms follow a price leader or collude tacitly, which can raise prices together; and studies have not always found oligopoly prices to be more rigid than elsewhere.

Overall, the model is likely to be most useful for explaining short-run price stability when costs change a little and rivals are watching closely, but less useful where costs are shared across the industry or where firms co-ordinate prices.

Common mistakeListing the limitations without a judgement, or giving a long description of the model with no evaluation.

Top tips

  • Say whose reaction matters. The kink exists because of how rivals react. Name both reactions: ignore a rise, match a cut.
  • Draw two MR segments and the gap. Each MR line starts on the vertical axis and has twice the slope of its demand segment. The vertical gap sits directly below the kink.
  • The gap is in MR, not MC. Never write that MC drops. Say that MC passes through the gap in MR.
  • Show a cost change. Shift MC within the gap to prove price does not move, then shift it beyond the gap to show the limit.
  • Fixed costs do not shift MC. A rise in rent or head office salaries leaves MC, output and price unchanged.
  • Hedge your analysis. Say rivals are "likely to" match a cut and price "could" stay the same. The model is a prediction, not a certainty.
  • Evaluate with the original price, shared costs and price leadership. These are the three standard limits.
