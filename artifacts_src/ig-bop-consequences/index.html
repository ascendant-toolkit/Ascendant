Consequences of Current Account Deficits and Surpluses

How a deficit or surplus affects GDP, employment, inflation and the exchange rate, and when it is a problem. Syllabus 6.4.3.

The big idea

A current account deficit or surplus affects GDP, employment, inflation and the foreign exchange rate. Net exports (X − M) are part of aggregate demand, so a surplus adds to AD and a deficit subtracts from it. A deficit must be financed by borrowing, selling assets or using reserves, and tends to put downward pressure on the exchange rate. A surplus tends to push the exchange rate up. Whether a deficit or surplus is a problem depends on its size, how long it lasts and what causes it.

Effects of a current account deficit

Possible harms

  • Lower AD: net exports are negative, so GDP and jobs in export and import-competing industries may be lower
  • Exchange rate pressure: more of the currency is supplied than demanded, so it tends to fall; a fall raises import prices and inflation
  • Financing: the country must borrow, sell assets or use reserves; debt and interest payments build up
  • May signal an uncompetitive economy
  • The government may be forced to cut spending or raise interest rates, slowing growth and raising unemployment

Possible benefits or not a problem

  • If caused by imports of capital goods, it can raise future output and growth
  • Cheap imports can lower prices and raise living standards
  • If small, or financed by foreign investment, it may be sustainable
  • A fast-growing country may attract investment and run a deficit safely

Effects of a current account surplus

Possible benefits

  • Higher AD: net exports add to GDP and jobs in exporting industries
  • Builds up reserves and overseas assets; can lend abroad
  • Supports a strong exchange rate, making imports cheaper and reducing inflation
  • Reduces dependence on foreign borrowing

Possible harms

  • Exchange rate pressure upwards: more demand for the currency raises it, which makes exports dearer and reduces the surplus
  • Inflation: extra demand may raise demand-pull inflation, especially near full capacity
  • Low spending by residents: the country is exporting resources instead of enjoying more goods
  • Trading partners may complain or retaliate; trade tensions

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

The US deficit and the dollar

Despite large US deficits, foreigners have been willing to lend to the US and hold dollars, so the deficit has been financed without a collapse of the currency.

Exam use: A deficit that is financed and sustainable.

Greece and Spain after 2008

Large deficits financed by borrowing left Greece and Spain exposed when lenders lost confidence, forcing cuts in spending and rising unemployment.

Exam use: A deficit becoming a problem.

Germany's surplus

Germany's large surplus has been criticised for reflecting low domestic demand, and it adds to pressure on other countries' trade balances.

Exam use: Surplus: benefits and tensions.

Thailand 1997

A large deficit and lost confidence led to a sharp fall in the baht and a wider financial crisis.

Exam use: Deficit, exchange rate and financing.

Q2 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a current account surplus could affect GDP, employment and the exchange rate.
ReminderNet exports add to AD; export jobs; demand for currency up; appreciation.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A surplus means exports exceed imports, so net exports are positive. Net exports are part of aggregate demand, so AD shifts right and real GDP could rise. Firms in export industries need more workers, so employment could rise. Foreigners need more of the country's currency to pay for exports, so demand for it increases and the exchange rate tends to rise. A higher exchange rate makes exports dearer and imports cheaper, which could reduce the surplus.
Q3 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not a current account deficit on its balance of payments harms an economy. (May/June 2020, P21)
ReminderHarm: lower AD, currency falls, inflation, debt, uncompetitive. Not harm: capital imports, cheap imports, financed by FDI, small, temporary.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A current account deficit means payments to the rest of the world exceed receipts. It could harm an economy because net exports reduce aggregate demand, so growth and jobs may be lower, and the deficit puts downward pressure on the exchange rate, raising import prices and inflation. It has to be financed by borrowing or selling assets, building up debt and interest payments. A large, persistent deficit is more harmful. However, a deficit may not be a problem if it is small, temporary, or caused by imports of capital goods that raise future output, or if it is financed by foreign investment. Cheap imports can also raise living standards. Overall, a large persistent deficit is likely to harm an economy, but a small one financed by investment may not, so it depends on its size, cause and financing.
Q4 Discuss 6 marks Discuss whether or not a surplus on the current account of its balance of payments would benefit an economy. (Feb/Mar 2024, P22)
ReminderBenefit: AD, jobs, reserves, strong currency. Not: inflation, exchange rate rise hurts exporters, low living standards, trade tensions.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A current account surplus means receipts exceed payments. It could benefit an economy because net exports add to AD, raising GDP and employment in exporting industries, and builds up reserves and overseas assets. A stronger currency would make imports cheaper and reduce inflation. The benefit is larger if the surplus reflects competitive industries. However, the extra demand could raise demand-pull inflation, a rising exchange rate could make exports dearer and reduce the surplus, and residents may enjoy fewer goods because output is exported. Overall, a surplus is likely to benefit an economy, particularly if caused by competitiveness, but a very large surplus may bring problems and trade tensions.
Q5 Discuss 6 marks Discuss whether or not India is likely to experience a deficit on the current account of its balance of payments. (Feb/Mar 2020, P22)
ReminderUse evidence from the data: growth, imports, oil, exports, remittances, services. Judge likelihood.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A current account deficit means imports and payments abroad exceed exports and receipts. India could experience a deficit because fast growth raises incomes and imports, and it imports large amounts of oil and machinery. Heavy dependence on imports and a high exchange rate could make exports less competitive. However, India earns large amounts from services exports such as IT, and receives large remittances, which could offset the goods deficit. Overall, a deficit in goods is likely, but the overall current account could be smaller depending on services and remittances.

Top tips

  • Net exports are part of AD. Surplus raises AD; deficit lowers it.
  • Deficit: exchange rate pressure down; surplus: up. Say why: supply and demand for the currency.
  • A deficit has to be financed. Borrowing, selling assets, reserves.
  • Is it a problem? 'It depends on…' Size, how long it lasts, what causes it, how it is financed.
  • Cover GDP, employment, inflation and exchange rate. The four the syllabus names.
