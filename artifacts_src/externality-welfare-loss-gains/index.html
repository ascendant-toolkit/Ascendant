Welfare Loss and Gains in the Four Externality Diagrams

What the shaded triangle means, why it is called a deadweight loss, how to work out its size, and how much society gains when a tax, subsidy or other policy removes it. Built unit by unit for all four externality diagrams, with labs and practice.

The big idea

Welfare is the total net benefit to society. A market is allocatively efficient when the last unit made is worth exactly what it costs society: MSB = MSC. With an externality the market stops at the wrong quantity, Q1 instead of Q*, and the units between the two are either worth less than they cost society (too many made) or worth more than they cost (too few made). Add up that mismatch, unit by unit, and you get a triangle. That triangle is the welfare loss, also called the deadweight loss.

"Welfare loss", "deadweight loss" and "net loss of social welfare" are used for the same thing in 9708 answers. Use the phrase in the question, and say it is where MSB and MSC differ.

2 · Why the loss is a triangle

One unit at a time

For any unit, compare what it is worth to society (MSB) with what it costs society (MSC). At Q* they are equal, so the next unit adds nothing. Move one unit away from Q* and the gap opens a little. At Q1 it equals the full marginal external cost or benefit.

Adding the gaps

The gap grows steadily from zero at Q* to the marginal externality at Q1, so the sum of all the gaps is a triangle. Base = the distance from Q* to Q1. Height = the marginal externality at Q1.

Welfare loss = ½ × (Q1 − Q*) × marginal externality
The calculation, with this page's numbersMarginal external cost or benefit = $20. Q1 = 50 and Q* is 37.5 (negative externalities) or 62.5 (positive ones). So the base is 12.5 units and the welfare loss is ½ × 12.5 × $20 = $125.

3 · The four triangles

The triangle always has its point at Q* and its vertical edge at Q1, and it sits between the MSB and MSC curves. What changes is the side of Q1 it is on, and which curve is on top.

4 · Where else welfare appears in 7.4

Over-correcting

A tax or subsidy larger than the marginal externality at Q* overshoots Q*. A new triangle appears on the other side. A policy can therefore produce a welfare gain or a welfare loss, depending on its size. The Size the fix tab lets you test it.

No externality at all

If MSB = MPB and MSC = MPC already, the market is at the efficient quantity. Any tax or subsidy then moves output away from it and creates a deadweight loss. The No externality tab shows the same $20 policy that gains $125 with an externality and loses $125 without one.

Tax revenue and subsidy cost

These are transfers between groups, not losses. Revenue is counted as a gain to the government, and a subsidy as a cost to taxpayers. Only the triangle is a net loss. Weighing the revenue against the lost surplus is how you judge who gains and who loses.

Cost-benefit analysis

CBA applies the same logic to a project: add up social benefits, subtract social costs (including external ones) and look at the net social benefit. A project is worth doing if it is positive. It is the welfare calculation for a decision about whether to build, not a market that has settled at the wrong quantity.

Why the revenue is not the loss

Part of the consumer and producer surplus that disappears turns into tax revenue, which the government can spend. That part is moved, not destroyed. The deadweight loss is only the part nobody receives: the units that were worth more than they cost but are no longer traded.

Why this matters for 7.4

It shows why a policy must target a real externality, and be sized to it. A tax on a good with no external cost, or a tax far above the external cost, tends to reduce welfare.

Short answers

(a) Explain 4 marks Explain what is meant by a welfare loss when there is a negative externality of production.
ReminderDefine it, then say why it arises (1 + 1, twice). Mention MSB, MSC and the two quantities.

A welfare loss is the net loss of benefit to society that occurs when output is not at the socially optimum level, where MSB = MSC [1]. With a negative externality of production, firms ignore the cost to third parties, so the market produces at Q1 where MPB = MPC, which is above Q* [1]. Between Q* and Q1, each unit costs society more (MSC) than it is worth to buyers (MSB), so the extra units reduce welfare [1]. The sum of these differences is shown by a triangle on the diagram, and no one gains it, so it is a deadweight loss [1].

Common mistakeDescribing the externality but never saying that the extra units cost more than they are worth. The welfare loss is about the units between Q* and Q1.
(b) Explain 6 marks A government puts a tax equal to the marginal external cost on a polluting good. Using a diagram, explain the welfare gain.
ReminderDiagram with D = MSB, S = MPC, MSC, Q1, Q*, the welfare-loss triangle and S + tax. Then explain what is regained.

The tax raises the cost of supply at every output by the marginal external cost, so supply shifts upwards and to the left, from S to S + tax, which lies on MSC [1]. Output falls from Q1 to Q* and the price rises [1]. The units between Q* and Q1 cost society more than they were worth, so no longer making them avoids that loss [1]. The welfare gain is the triangle that was the welfare loss, with its base from Q* to Q1 and its height equal to the marginal external cost [1]. Consumers and producers lose some surplus, but the government gains revenue and the pollution falls, so net social welfare is likely to rise [1]. With a $20 cost and a fall in output from 50 to 37.5 units, the gain is ½ × 12.5 × 20 = $125 [1].

Common mistakeSaying the gain is the tax revenue. The revenue is a transfer; the gain is the triangle that is recovered.
(c) Assess 6 marks Assess whether a subsidy always increases welfare when there is a positive externality of consumption.
ReminderSay when it does (subsidy up to the MEB at Q*), then when it may not (too large, poor targeting), then conclude.

If consumers ignore the benefit to others, the market produces at Q1, below Q*, and units that are worth more than they cost are not made. A subsidy lowers the price, so quantity rises towards Q* and a welfare gain is made up of the units between Q1 and Q* [1]. A subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit could remove the whole welfare loss [1].

A subsidy does not always raise welfare. If it is larger than the marginal external benefit, quantity rises beyond Q* and units that cost more than they are worth are bought, creating a new welfare loss [1]. If it is much larger than the MEB, the new loss could outweigh the original gain [1]. The cost to the taxpayer has an opportunity cost, and the subsidy may go to people who would have bought the good anyway [1].

Conclusion: a subsidy is likely to increase welfare if it is close to the marginal external benefit, which has to be estimated, so the gain depends on how accurately that can be done [1].

Common mistakeConcluding "yes" because the externality is positive. The size of the subsidy decides whether welfare rises.

Essays (20 marks)

Essay 1 Discuss 20 marks With the help of a diagram, discuss the welfare effects of a government subsidy on a good that has a positive externality of consumption.
What Level 3 needsA correct labelled diagram (MPB, MSB, MPC, Q1, Q*, welfare loss triangle, S − subsidy) explained in the text, developed analysis of the gain and who pays, and evaluation of the size of the subsidy and its alternatives.

A positive externality of consumption is a benefit to third parties when a good is consumed, so the marginal social benefit (MSB) is above the marginal private benefit (MPB). A welfare loss is the net benefit to society that is lost when output is not at Q*, where MSB = MSC. Vaccination is an example: a vaccinated person is less likely to infect others.

Consumers decide using MPB, so the market clears at Q1, where MPB = MPC, below the socially optimum Q*. Between Q1 and Q* each unit is worth more to society than it costs, but it is not bought, so there is a welfare loss shown by the triangle with its point at Q* and its base at Q1. It is a deadweight loss because nobody gains the benefits that are given up.

A subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit lowers firms' costs at every output, so supply shifts down and to the right, from S to S minus the subsidy. The price consumers pay falls, quantity rises to Q* and the welfare loss is removed. That triangle is the welfare gain. Consumers gain from the lower price, producers receive the price plus the subsidy, and the taxpayer pays the subsidy on every unit. The subsidy bill ($1,250 in the illustration) is far larger than the welfare gain ($125) because most of it pays for units the market would have made anyway, so it is a transfer from taxpayers. The net gain to society is the triangle.

The gain depends on getting the size right. The marginal external benefit is hard to measure, and if the subsidy is too small a welfare loss remains. If it is too large, quantity goes past Q* and a new welfare loss appears on the other side, so welfare could be lower than with no subsidy. If supply is inelastic, a large part of the subsidy could raise producers' incomes and not quantity. The opportunity cost of the money matters too, as the funds could have gone to other uses. Alternatives such as an information campaign, which shifts demand, may be cheaper per head but have uncertain effects. Overall, a subsidy is likely to raise welfare where the external benefit is large, where it can be estimated with reasonable accuracy and where demand and supply are responsive. Otherwise it may be wasteful. A combination with information could be best.

Common mistakeCounting the subsidy payment as the welfare gain. The gain is the triangle that is recovered. The payment is a transfer from taxpayers.
Essay 2 Discuss 20 marks With the help of a diagram, discuss whether a government that overestimates the marginal external cost of a good could leave society worse off than if it had done nothing.
What Level 3 needsA diagram showing Q1, Q* and the over-sized tax with the new triangle, the logic of the break-even size, and evaluation that weighs the risk against the benefits.

With a negative externality of production, the marginal social cost (MSC) is above the marginal private cost (MPC) by the marginal external cost (MEC). The market over-produces at Q1, and the welfare loss is the triangle between MSC and MSB from Q* to Q1. A tax equal to the MEC moves output to Q* and removes the loss.

Suppose the government believes the MEC is larger than it is and sets the tax at that higher figure. Supply shifts further to the left than MSC, so output falls below Q*. For each unit between the new output and Q*, MSB is above MSC: the unit was worth more to society than it cost, but it is no longer made. These units form a new welfare-loss triangle on the other side of Q*. In the illustration used in class, with a true MEC of $20, a tax of $40 cuts output from 50 to 25. The new triangle is ½ × 12.5 × 20 = $125, the same size as the original loss, so the policy gains nothing, and a tax above $40 leaves society worse off than doing nothing.

The loss from overshooting grows with the square of the error, since the triangle's base and height both rise. A small overshoot therefore costs very little, and a tax of $25 would still leave a gain of about $117 of the possible $125.

The risk depends on how wrong the estimate is. The tax would need to be about double the true MEC to cancel the gain, so moderate errors still leave a gain. If the tax is underestimated by the same amount, some welfare loss is left but the policy is still beneficial. The risk is greater when demand and supply are elastic, because output responds strongly. The effects of an inaccurate tax on revenue, jobs and competitiveness also matter, and where the damage is uncertain, a cap on output (permits) may be safer since it fixes the quantity. Overall, a government that overestimates the MEC is likely to make society worse off only if it overshoots by a large margin. Governments tend to be uncertain about the external cost, so it is likely better to act with an estimate than to do nothing, and to adjust over time.

Common mistakeSaying any wrong tax makes things worse. The answer depends on how far from the MEC it is: a small error still gains, a very large one loses.

Top tips

  • Point at Q*, edge at Q1. The welfare-loss triangle always has its point at the socially optimum quantity and its vertical side at the market quantity.
  • Say why it is a loss. "Each unit between Q* and Q1 costs society more than it is worth." For positive externalities: "each unit is worth more than it costs but is not made."
  • It is the gap that counts. The triangle is bounded by MSB and MSC, not by D and S. Use the social curve that is not coinciding with D or S.
  • Do not shade a rectangle. Tax revenue and subsidy cost are rectangles and are transfers. Only the triangle is a net loss.
  • After the policy, say the gain. The welfare loss triangle has gone, so society has regained that area.
  • Size matters. A tax or subsidy larger than the marginal externality overshoots Q* and creates a new, smaller or larger, triangle on the other side.
  • Calculate it. Welfare loss = ½ × (Q1 − Q*) × marginal externality. Show the formula and the working.
