Policies to Promote Economic Growth

The range of fiscal, monetary, supply-side, investment and trade policies that could promote growth, and how effective each might be. Syllabus 4.5.5.

The big idea

Governments can use a range of policies to promote economic growth: fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy, plus encouraging investment and trade. Demand-side policies work quickly but can cause inflation. Supply-side policies raise capacity but are slow. How effective each is depends on the cause of weak growth and the state of the economy.

The range of policies

PolicyExampleHow it promotes growth
FiscalRaise government spending, cut taxesAD shifts right; infrastructure and education also raise capacity
MonetaryCut interest rates, increase money supply, lower the exchange rateCheaper borrowing raises C and I; a lower exchange rate raises exports
Supply-sideEducation and training, infrastructure, deregulation, lower direct taxes, privatisationRaises quantity or quality of resources: AS shifts right
Investment policiesTax breaks for investment, subsidies, attracting multinational firmsMore capital, jobs and technology
Trade policiesFree trade agreements, export support, fewer trade barriersLarger markets, more exports, competition and technology

How effective?

Demand-side policies

  • Pros: relatively quick; helpful when there is spare capacity.
  • Cons: risk of inflation near full capacity; bigger deficit (fiscal); effect of rate cuts weak if confidence is low; higher imports.

Supply-side policies

  • Pros: raise capacity, so growth lasts and inflation is lower.
  • Cons: slow, costly, uncertain; some measures widen inequality.

Investment and trade

  • Pros: bring capital, skills and bigger markets.
  • Cons: exposes firms to competition; dependence on other countries; foreign firms may take profits out.

Choosing a policy

If growth is low because demand is weak, demand-side policy fits. If it is low because resources are scarce or low quality, supply-side policy fits. Often a mix is best.

Evaluating policy choices

  • Time lags and size of the multiplier or response.
  • Spare capacity: with little spare capacity extra demand mostly raises prices.
  • Costs: deficits, debt, opportunity cost.
  • Conflicts with other aims: inflation, current account, inequality, environment.
  • Sustainability: growth that damages the environment cannot be sustained.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Stimulus after 2008

Governments cut interest rates close to zero and raised spending to restart growth after the financial crisis. Growth returned, though slowly in many countries, and debts rose.

Exam use: Demand-side policy to end a recession, with a cost.

Singapore and skills

Singapore invests heavily in education and training and attracts foreign firms with low business taxes and strong infrastructure.

Exam use: Supply-side and investment policy raising capacity.

Trade-led growth

Vietnam has grown quickly by joining trade agreements and attracting foreign manufacturers, with exports a large share of output.

Exam use: Trade and FDI policy, with dependence on world demand as a risk.

Infrastructure spending in India

India has increased spending on roads, rail and ports to cut costs and raise capacity, though projects are large and slow.

Exam use: A supply-side policy with a long time lag.

Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain two policy measures a government could use to increase its economic growth rate.
ReminderTwo different measures, each with a chain: fiscal, monetary, supply-side, investment, trade.
A government could cut interest rates. Borrowing becomes cheaper and saving less attractive, so consumption and investment could rise, shifting aggregate demand right and increasing real GDP. It could also spend more on education and training. Workers would become more productive and aggregate supply could shift right, so productive capacity and output may grow.
Q2 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how a cut in interest rates could increase economic growth.
ReminderChain: rate cut, borrowing, C and I, AD, real GDP. Add a second link: exchange rate or confidence.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A cut in the interest rate reduces the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving. Households are likely to spend more and borrow more, so consumption could rise, and firms may find more investment projects profitable. Lower interest rates may also reduce the exchange rate, making exports cheaper and increasing net exports. Higher C, I and X − M shift AD to the right, so real GDP rises. However, if confidence is low households and firms may not respond.
Q3 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not increased international trade can promote economic growth. (Oct/Nov 2020, P21)
ReminderFor: larger markets, exports, competition, technology, specialisation. Against: competition hurts firms, dependence, imports, environment.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
International trade is the exchange of goods and services between countries. More trade could promote growth by giving firms access to larger markets, so exports and output could rise, and firms may employ more workers. Competition from imports may push firms to cut costs and improve quality, and trade can bring in cheaper inputs and new technology, which could raise productivity. These gains are larger if the country specialises in goods in which it is efficient. On the other hand, domestic firms may struggle against imports and some workers could lose jobs, and a country that depends on a few exports may be hurt by a world slowdown. Higher incomes could also raise imports and worsen the current account. Overall, trade is likely to promote growth in the long run, but it depends on the country's industries, the kind of trade and whether workers can move to growing sectors.
Q4 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether fiscal policy or supply-side policy is better for promoting economic growth.
ReminderCompare speed, inflation risk, cost and sustainability. Choose a side with 'it depends on…'.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Fiscal policy changes government spending and taxes to affect aggregate demand, whereas supply-side policy aims to raise productive capacity. Fiscal policy can work fairly quickly: higher spending or lower taxes raise AD, so real GDP and employment could rise, especially when there is spare capacity. However, it can raise the budget deficit and, near full capacity, cause inflation. Supply-side policy such as education and infrastructure raises the quantity and quality of resources, so growth can last with lower inflation. However, it is slow and may be costly, and some measures could widen inequality. Overall, fiscal policy may be better for a short-term boost during a slump, while supply-side policy is likely to be better for long-term growth. A government may achieve most by combining both.
Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not supply-side policy measures always promote economic growth. (May/June 2022, P23)
Reminder'Always' invites 'not always'. See also 4.4.3.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Supply-side measures aim to raise productive capacity, which should increase growth. Training could make workers more productive, infrastructure could cut costs and tax incentives could encourage investment. However, they do not always work. Training may not match employers' needs, firms and workers may not respond to tax cuts, and projects can be late or costly. Overall, supply-side policy is likely to promote growth in the long run if well targeted, but it is not guaranteed.

Top tips

  • Five policy groups: fiscal, monetary, supply-side, investment, trade.
  • Match the policy to the cause. Weak demand: demand-side. Low capacity: supply-side.
  • Short run against long run. Demand-side is quick; supply-side is slow but lasting.
  • Always give effectiveness. Time lags, inflation, deficit, confidence, side effects.
  • Combine policies in your conclusion. A mix is often the best answer.
