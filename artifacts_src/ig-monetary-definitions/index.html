Money Supply and Monetary Policy

What the money supply is, what monetary policy is, what a central bank does, and how policy is passed to the economy. Syllabus 4.3.1.

The big idea

The money supply is the total amount of money in an economy. Monetary policy is the use of the money supply, interest rates and the foreign exchange rate to influence aggregate demand and inflation. It is usually run by the central bank, which sets the main interest rate and has the tools to change how much money is available.

What is in the money supply?

Notes and coins

Cash in circulation, issued by the central bank.

Bank deposits

The money households and firms hold in current and saving accounts, which they can spend by card or transfer. This is most of the money supply in many countries.

Commercial banks create money when they lend: a loan becomes a new deposit in the borrower's account. This is why the central bank can influence the money supply by influencing how much banks lend.

Money supply and monetary policy

The money supply is the total amount of money in an economy. It includes notes and coins in circulation and the deposits people and firms hold in bank accounts, which they can use to buy things.

Monetary policy is usually run by the central bank, the government's banker and the country's monetary authority. In many countries the central bank is independent, so decisions on interest rates are made by its own committee rather than by politicians.

What a central bank does

  • Issues notes and coins
  • Acts as banker to the government and to commercial banks
  • Sets the main (base) interest rate and controls the money supply
  • Holds the country's reserves of foreign currency and can manage the exchange rate
  • Can lend to banks that run short of cash (lender of last resort)

Expansionary or contractionary?

Expansionary (loose) monetary policy aims to raise AD: lower interest rates, a bigger money supply, a lower exchange rate.

Contractionary (tight) monetary policy aims to reduce AD: higher interest rates, a smaller money supply, a higher exchange rate.

AD = C + I + G + (X − M). Monetary policy works mainly on C and I (interest rates, money supply) and on X − M (exchange rate)

The chain of control

  1. The central bank sets the base interest rate and the conditions for the money supply.
  2. Commercial banks follow: they adjust the rates they charge borrowers and pay savers.
  3. Households and firms borrow, save and spend in response.
  4. Aggregate demand changes, and so do output, jobs and prices.

Central banks fighting inflation, 2022–23

After prices rose quickly in many countries, central banks such as the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and the Bank of England raised interest rates in a series of steps. Mortgage payments rose and borrowing slowed.

Exam use: contractionary monetary policy against demand-pull and cost-push inflation. Evaluate: time lags, higher unemployment risk, and pain for borrowers.

Rates near zero after 2008 and COVID-19

Central banks cut rates to very low levels after the 2008 financial crisis and again during the pandemic, and several bought large amounts of government bonds (quantitative easing) to increase the money supply.

Exam use: expansionary monetary policy in action. Evaluate: effects depend on confidence and on whether banks lend, and savers earn less.

Printing money and runaway prices

Zimbabwe in the 2000s and Venezuela in the 2010s are widely cited cases where governments created large amounts of money to cover spending. Prices rose very fast and the currency lost most of its value.

Exam use: the danger of too big an increase in the money supply: demand-pull inflation and a falling value of money.

Independent central banks

In many countries, including the UK and the eurozone, the central bank sets interest rates independently of politicians, with a target for inflation.

Exam use: A reason why monetary policy may be more consistent than fiscal policy: decisions are less tied to elections.

Q1 Define 2 marks Define monetary policy. (May/June 2022, P23)
ReminderTwo marks: name the tools, and say what they are used to influence. A common slip is to describe only interest rates.
Monetary policy is the use of the money supply, interest rates and the foreign exchange rate by a government or central bank to influence aggregate demand and inflation.
Common mistakeMixing it up with fiscal policy. Taxes and government spending are fiscal; interest rates, money supply and exchange rates are monetary.
Q2 Define 2 marks What is meant by the money supply?
ReminderTwo ideas: the total amount of money, and what it includes (notes, coins and bank deposits).
The money supply is the total amount of money in an economy, including notes and coins in circulation and the money held in bank accounts.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain two functions a central bank performs for its government. (May/June 2022, P22)
ReminderPick two clear functions and develop each: banker to the government, manages the national debt, holds reserves, sets interest rates, implements monetary policy.
A central bank acts as the government's banker. It holds the government's accounts and handles payments, and may help the government to borrow by selling its bonds. It also carries out the government's monetary policy. It sets the base interest rate and controls the money supply, which could be used to influence aggregate demand and inflation.
Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain one difference between monetary policy and fiscal policy.
ReminderUse 'whereas'. Compare the tools, or who decides.
Monetary policy uses interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate, whereas fiscal policy uses government spending and taxation. Interest rate decisions are often made by an independent central bank, whereas a budget is decided by the government, so fiscal policy can be targeted at a group or a project while a change in interest rates affects the whole economy.

Top tips

  • Three tools: interest rates, money supply, exchange rate. Name all three.
  • Say what the policy is for. To influence aggregate demand and inflation.
  • Central bank, not government. In most countries the central bank sets interest rates.
  • Do not mix up fiscal and monetary. Tax and spending are fiscal.
  • Money supply includes deposits. Not just notes and coins.
