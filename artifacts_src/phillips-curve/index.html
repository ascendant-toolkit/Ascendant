Inflation and Unemployment: the Phillips Curve

10.2.5: the traditional Phillips curve, stagflation, the expectations-augmented short-run and long-run Phillips curves, and the policy implications. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

The traditional Phillips curve shows an inverse relationship between unemployment and inflation (or wage growth): lower unemployment goes with higher inflation, so a government faces a trade-off. In the 1970s stagflation (high inflation with high unemployment) broke the pattern. The expectations-augmented Phillips curve explains why: each short-run curve depends on expected inflation, so it shifts when expectations change, and the long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment. Demand policy can reduce unemployment below the natural rate only temporarily, at the price of accelerating inflation.

1 · The traditional Phillips curve

  • Origin: A.W. Phillips (1958) found an inverse link between wage inflation and unemployment in UK data from 1861 to 1957. Later economists replaced wage inflation with price inflation.
  • Why: low unemployment means a tight labour market, so workers gain bargaining power and wages rise; firms pass higher costs on, and demand for goods is strong (demand-pull). High unemployment weakens both.
  • Shape: downward sloping and convex: reducing unemployment further costs more inflation.
  • Policy menu: a government could choose a point on the curve, such as lower unemployment with higher inflation, or the reverse.

Axes: inflation rate (%) on the vertical axis, unemployment rate (%) on the horizontal axis.

2 · The breakdown in the 1970s

After the 1973 oil price shock many countries had both high inflation and high unemployment: stagflation. The curve seemed to have shifted outwards. Two explanations: supply shocks (cost-push) shift the curve up, and expectations of inflation raised wage demands whatever the level of unemployment.

3 · The expectations-augmented Phillips curve

inflation = expected inflation + a × (natural rate − unemployment)
  • Short-run Phillips curve (SRPC): drawn for a given level of expected inflation. Moving up it, the economy has lower unemployment and higher inflation. Workers wrongly expect lower inflation than actually happens, so their real wage falls and firms hire.
  • Shift of the SRPC: when expected inflation rises, workers bargain for higher wages and the SRPC shifts up. Unemployment returns to the natural rate but with higher inflation.
  • Long-run Phillips curve (LRPC): a vertical line at the natural rate of unemployment, because in the long run expectations adjust and there is no trade-off.
  • Accelerationist view: to keep unemployment below the natural rate the government must keep raising demand, so inflation accelerates.
  • Disinflation: to cut inflation the economy has to move down the SRPC (unemployment above the natural rate) until expectations fall. The sacrifice ratio measures the cost.

4 · Evaluation

  • The natural rate is hard to estimate and changes over time (see 9.3.4).
  • Hysteresis: long spells of unemployment can raise the natural rate, so the LRPC is not truly fixed.
  • Since the 1990s the curve has often looked flat: low and stable inflation over a wide range of unemployment, perhaps because expectations are anchored by central bank targets and globalisation holds down prices.
  • Rational expectations: if workers anticipate policy, even the short-run trade-off may vanish, so credible policy is crucial.
  • Keynesians accept a short-run trade-off but think the long run is not vertical when there is spare capacity.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

The 1970s stagflation

After the oil price rises of 1973 and 1979 many economies had inflation of 10% or more at the same time as unemployment rising to record post-war levels. Governments that stimulated demand to reduce unemployment saw inflation take off.

Exam use: the standard evidence for stagflation and expectations. Say that both supply shocks and expectations were involved.

The Volcker disinflation in the US

In 1979 to 1982 the US Federal Reserve raised interest rates sharply to bring down inflation of over 10%. Unemployment rose to around 10% before inflation fell to below 4%.

Exam use: an example of the cost of disinflation: moving down the short-run curve until expectations fall. Use it for the sacrifice ratio.

A flat Phillips curve after 2010

After the financial crisis unemployment fell from 10% to under 4% in the US in the 2010s, but inflation stayed close to 2%. Economists asked whether the curve had flattened because inflation expectations were anchored.

Exam use: evidence against a strong short-run trade-off. Use it in evaluation and add that the 2021 to 2022 inflation surge revived the debate.

Labour market reform and the natural rate

Reforms to benefits, job-matching services and wage bargaining in several European countries are credited with lowering the unemployment rate at which inflation stays stable, shifting the long-run curve left.

Exam use: supply-side policy shifting the LRPC. Link to 9.3.4.

The traditional Phillips curve shows an inverse relationship between the rate of unemployment and the rate of inflation, so that lower unemployment is associated with higher inflation. It suggested a stable trade-off from which a government could choose a combination, for example accepting 4% inflation in return for lower unemployment. The expectations-augmented Phillips curve says that this trade-off holds only in the short run, for a given level of expected inflation. If the government pushes unemployment below the natural rate, inflation rises above what workers expected, so real wages fall and firms hire more. Once workers see prices rising faster they demand higher wages and the short-run curve shifts up, and unemployment returns to the natural rate with higher inflation.

The long-run Phillips curve is therefore vertical at the natural rate of unemployment, and to keep unemployment below that rate inflation would have to keep accelerating. This explains stagflation in the 1970s, when supply shocks and rising expectations shifted the curve up, and it implies that only supply-side policies that lower the natural rate can reduce unemployment permanently. However, the natural rate is difficult to estimate, hysteresis can raise it after a recession, and since the 1990s the curve has often looked flat because expectations are anchored by central bank targets, so the relationship is less reliable than the theory suggests.

Practice (not a past paper question) 8 marks Explain why the short-run Phillips curve might shift upwards.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Higher expected inflation: workers bargain for higher wages to keep real wages, so the same unemployment rate goes with more inflation.
  • A supply shock (higher oil prices or import costs) raises costs and prices whatever the level of unemployment (cost-push).
  • A rise in the natural rate (for example after hysteresis) shifts the curve to the right.
  • Evaluation: whether expectations are adaptive or forward-looking determines the speed of the shift.

The short-run Phillips curve shows the trade-off between inflation and unemployment for a given level of expected inflation. It shifts upwards if expected inflation rises. Workers who expect prices to rise faster bargain for bigger pay rises, firms pass the costs on, and so at every level of unemployment inflation is higher. It also shifts up after a supply shock such as a sharp rise in oil prices, because firms' costs rise and they raise prices whether or not unemployment changes, which is how stagflation can arise.

A rise in the natural rate of unemployment, for example because long-term unemployment has led workers to lose skills, would shift the curve to the right. However, how quickly the curve shifts depends on whether expectations adapt slowly to past inflation or respond immediately to announced policy, so credibility of the central bank may reduce the shift.

Top tips

  • Label the axes correctly. Inflation rate on the vertical axis and unemployment rate on the horizontal axis.
  • Say 'short run' and 'long run'. The trade-off holds in the short run. The long-run curve is vertical at the natural rate.
  • Movement or shift? A change in unemployment along the curve is a movement. A change in expected inflation or a supply shock is a shift.
  • Name stagflation. High inflation and high unemployment together. Cause: supply shock and expectations.
  • Always use a diagram in a 20 mark question. Without one the answer is capped at Level 2.
  • Link to policy. Demand policy: temporary. Supply-side policy: lowers the natural rate. Credible central bank: lowers expectations.
  • Evaluate with data. The natural rate is uncertain, and the curve has looked flat since the 1990s.
