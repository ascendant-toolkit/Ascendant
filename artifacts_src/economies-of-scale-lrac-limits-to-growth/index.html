Economies of Scale, LRAC and the Limits to Growth

The long-run average cost curve, economies and diseconomies of scale, minimum efficient scale and how many firms a market can hold. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

The long-run average cost (LRAC) curve is usually U-shaped. While it falls, the firm enjoys economies of scale; at the lowest point it reaches minimum efficient scale (MES); beyond that it may face diseconomies of scale from problems of management and communication. The size of MES compared with the market decides how many firms can survive at the lowest cost: a small MES means room for many firms, a large MES leaves room for only a few. Growth is therefore not always good: costs can rise, and several things limit how far a firm can grow.

1 · What limits growth?

Diseconomies of scale

Past a certain size average cost may rise: poorer communication and coordination, lower worker motivation, slow decisions, and layers of management. These are internal diseconomies. External ones come from the industry growing too big, for example congestion or higher wages in the area.

Other limits

  • Finance: banks and shareholders may not lend
  • The size of the market: not enough customers
  • Competition law and regulators
  • Owner's wishes: some owners want to stay small and keep control

2 · The picture: long-run average cost and minimum efficient scale

The LRAC curve is U-shaped. Falling LRAC means economies of scale. The lowest point is the minimum efficient scale (MES), the smallest output at which average cost is as low as it can get. Rising LRAC means diseconomies of scale.

3 · Types of economy of scale

EconomyMeaningExample
TechnicalLarger machines and more efficient processes at high outputA bigger oven that bakes more loaves for the same fuel
PurchasingDiscounts on bulk buyingA supermarket chain negotiating prices with suppliers
FinancialCheaper borrowing for a large firmLower interest rates for a well-known company
ManagerialSpecialist managers become affordableA separate finance director
MarketingAdvertising costs spread over more outputA national campaign costs little per unit

Diseconomies of scale come from slower decisions, poorer communication, weaker motivation and difficulty coordinating, so LRAC rises beyond MES. Economies of scale are internal when they come from the firm's own size; external economies come from the growth of the industry (for example a skilled labour force nearby).

4 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Supermarket buying power

A large supermarket chain buys in huge quantities and can negotiate discounts from suppliers, which is a purchasing economy of scale. A small corner shop cannot match that unit cost on the same goods.

Exam use: a purchasing economy of scale. Evaluate: savings may not be passed on, and suppliers may be squeezed.

A very large organisation

A company with many divisions in many countries may find decisions slow, communication poor and staff less motivated. Some large firms have been broken up or have sold divisions to cut costs.

Exam use: diseconomies of scale, and a reason why growth may not lower unit costs.

Car plants and minimum efficient scale

Making cars needs very large plants to reach the lowest cost, so MES is a big share of the market. Only a few firms can reach it, which is a barrier to entry.

Exam use: a large MES relative to the market means few firms; a small MES means many.

B · Practice question with a model answer

Practice (not a past paper question) 8 marks Explain what is meant by economies of scale and why long-run average cost may rise beyond minimum efficient scale.
PlanDefine economies of scale and MES. Give two types with examples. Explain diseconomies and say what happens to LRAC.
KANE

Economies of scale are falls in long-run average cost as a firm increases its output. The minimum efficient scale is the lowest output at which long-run average cost is at its minimum.

A supermarket chain may buy in bulk and negotiate lower prices from suppliers (a purchasing economy), and a large manufacturer may use specialised machinery (a technical economy), so each loaf or product costs less to produce. Beyond minimum efficient scale, long-run average cost may rise because of diseconomies of scale. As the firm becomes very large, communication may become slower and decisions more complicated, and workers may feel less motivated, so costs per unit could increase. Whether a firm reaches diseconomies may depend on how well it is managed, because firms with good systems might delay them.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/42 · Q1(b) 5 marks Firms in the same industry often locate close to each other, for example technology firms in one city. Explain, with the aid of a diagram, how this can reduce the average costs of each firm.
What the mark scheme wants
  • External economies of scale: shared pool of skilled labour, nearby suppliers of parts and materials, local training courses, specialist services (legal, finance, transport).
  • These benefits come from the industry growing in the area, not from any one firm growing.
  • Result: LRAC falls for every firm in the area. Marks: about 2 + 2 for explanation, 1 for the diagram.
  • Diagram: a downward shift of the LRAC curve (not a movement along it).
PlanSay external, not internal. Give two linked reasons. Show LRAC1 shifting down to LRAC2 with cost and output on the axes.
Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/41 · Q1(b) 5 marks A producer of rechargeable batteries expects demand for its product to rise sharply over the next ten years, but its main raw materials are in limited supply. Discuss what might happen to its long-run average cost.
What the mark scheme wants
  • LRAC is average fixed plus average variable cost, and it falls when economies of scale are reached (about 2 marks).
  • Higher output could spread fixed costs over more units, so AFC may fall (1).
  • But if the key inputs are scarce, rising demand may push input prices up, so AVC could rise and cancel the saving (2).
  • The best answers give a balanced view: costs may fall at first, then rise unless technology changes.
PlanDefine LRAC. Economies of scale (fixed costs, bulk buying). Then the counter-force: scarce inputs. Short judgement: depends on technology and the supply of materials.
Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/42 · Q1(b) 5 marks A large regional bus company says its size keeps its costs per passenger low. With the use of an example and a diagram, explain how a large bus company could benefit from economies of scale.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explain economies of scale as a fall in LRAC as output rises (1).
  • Give a specific type with a bus example, such as bulk-buying fuel and vehicles (purchasing), or specialist maintenance depots (technical) (1).
  • Link the example to falling LRAC (1).
  • Diagram: LRAC against output with axes labelled (1) and the curve labelled (1).
PlanDefinition, one example fully applied to buses, then draw a downward-sloping LRAC. Keep it to one economy and do it well: five marks does not need five types.
Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/42 · Q1(a) and (b) 3 + 3 marks A firm can make a component using Technology X or Technology Y. The table shows average cost per unit at different annual outputs.

Output (million units): 1, 3, 6, 12.
Technology X ($ per unit): 90, 78, 70, 70.
Technology Y ($ per unit): 100, 92, 85, 77.

(a) Explain what is meant by internal economies of scale, using an example. [3]
(b) Explain minimum efficient scale and consider whether the figures suggest it has been reached with each technology. [3]
What the mark scheme wants
  • (a) A fall in LRAC as the firm itself expands (1 + 1), with a valid example such as buying materials in bulk or using larger specialised machines (1).
  • (b) MES is the lowest output at which LRAC stops falling (1).
  • Technology X: cost stops falling at 6 million, so MES looks to have been reached (1).
  • Technology Y: cost is still falling at 12 million, so MES has not yet been reached from these figures (1).
PlanUse the numbers: X flat from 6 to 12 million, Y still falling. Note that more data could change the judgement for Y.
Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/43 · Q4(b) 13 marks Discuss why costs per unit might fall as an industry's output grows, and consider whether consumers are likely to benefit.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explanation of why LRAC falls: internal economies (technical, purchasing, financial, managerial, marketing) and external economies.
  • Link to price: lower costs may raise the profit-maximising output and could lower price, if demand is large enough to keep that output sold.
  • Whether consumers gain depends on the firm's attitude to profit and on competition: savings may be kept as extra profit.
  • Level 4 (9 to 13) needs both the causes and a judgement on consumer benefit. Little on consumers is held to Level 2 or 3.
PlanTwo or three economies of scale with an LRAC diagram. Then a cost-to-price link using MC and MR. Evaluate: competition, regulation, and diseconomies at larger sizes. Conclude with when consumers are most likely to gain.
Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/42 · Q3 20 marks Evaluate, with the help of a diagram or diagrams, how the size of the total market and the minimum efficient scale of production may decide how many firms an industry can support.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge and analysis (14): economies of scale and their effect on the LRAC curve; MES as the output where LRAC first stops falling; market demand divided by MES gives roughly how many firms can operate at MES.
  • Worked examples: if MES is half the market, room for about two firms (duopoly); if MES is 2% of the market, room for about 50 (competition).
  • Link to market structures: many, few or one firm, matching perfect or monopolistic competition, oligopoly and monopoly.
  • Diagram: LRAC with MES marked, against the market demand curve.
  • Evaluation (6): MES changes with technology; the definition of the market matters (a village shop suits a village market); niche products keep small firms alive even where the main market is concentrated.
PlanDefine MES, draw LRAC with demand. Do two numerical cases (large MES, small MES). Link each to a market structure. Evaluate with technology change, market definition and niche markets, then give a judgement on which factor matters most.

Top tips

  • Do not assume costs fall. Economies of scale are possible, but diseconomies are too. Say "may" and "could".
  • Label the LRAC curve: economies of scale on the falling part, MES at the minimum, diseconomies on the rising part.
  • Say which type of economy it is, technical, purchasing, financial, managerial or marketing, and give an example.
