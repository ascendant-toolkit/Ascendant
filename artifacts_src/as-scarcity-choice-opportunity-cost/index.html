Scarcity, Choice and Opportunity Cost

1.1.1 to 1.1.4: the fundamental economic problem of scarcity, why choice is needed at every level, opportunity cost and the three basic questions of resource allocation. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

The fundamental economic problem is scarcity: human wants are unlimited but the resources to satisfy them are limited, and this is true for rich and poor alike. Scarcity forces choice at every level, by individuals, firms and governments, because choosing one thing means going without another. The opportunity cost of a choice is the value of the next best alternative that is given up. Every economy has to answer three basic questions: what to produce, how to produce it and for whom to produce.

1 · Scarcity (1.1.1)

  • Wants are unlimited: people always want more or better goods and services. Resources (land, labour, capital and enterprise) are limited. Scarcity is the gap between the two.
  • Scarcity applies to every society, rich or poor, because wants are never completely satisfied. It is a permanent problem, not a temporary one.
  • Scarcity is not the same as a shortage. A shortage is a situation in a market where demand is greater than supply at the current price and can be removed by a price rise. Scarcity cannot be removed.
  • Scarce resources have a cost. A free good is a rare case with no scarcity and no opportunity cost (see syllabus 1.6).

2 · Choice (1.1.2)

Who choosesExample of a choiceWhat is given up
IndividualsHow to spend a month's income; how to spend an eveningThe next best use of the money or time
FirmsWhich product to make, whether to buy a machine or hire workersThe profit from the alternative use of the same resources
GovernmentsSpending on hospitals or on schools; cutting taxes or building roadsThe services that the money could have funded

3 · Opportunity cost (1.1.3)

  • Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made. It is not the total of all alternatives, only the single best one that is given up.
  • It is often not the money price. The opportunity cost of studying all evening is the best other use of the evening. For a firm it may be the profit from the next best product.
  • It can be measured in units of the other good. If a firm produces 100 units of Y and 200 of X, and moving to 120 of Y leaves only 170 of X, the opportunity cost of the extra 20 Y is 30 units of X.
  • For a government, spending $800,000 of a $1.2m buildings budget on cancer care means the opportunity cost is the lost new buildings (the next best use), not the cancer care itself.
  • Opportunity cost explains why every choice has a cost, even when the good seems free.

4 · The basic questions of resource allocation (1.1.4)

QuestionWhat it asksExample
What to produce?Which goods and services, and how much of eachMore food or more cars; more hospitals or more roads
How to produce?Which methods and resources: labour-intensive or capital-intensive, which technologyHand-picking crops or using harvesting machines
For whom to produce?Who gets the output: how income and goods are sharedWhether goods go to those who can pay, or are shared by need

Different economic systems answer these questions in different ways (syllabus 1.4): the price mechanism in a market economy, central planning in a planned economy, and a mixture in most economies today.

Exam tipDo not write that scarcity means "not enough". Say that wants are unlimited and resources are limited, so choices must be made, and that every choice has an opportunity cost.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A pandemic lockdown

In 2020 governments ordered non-essential businesses to close to save lives. The benefit was fewer infections; the opportunity cost included the output, jobs and incomes of the closed businesses, and the services that the extra spending could have funded.

Exam use: opportunity cost at the level of a government, where both the benefits and the cost are large.

A hospital budget

A hospital board has a fixed budget and must decide between new buildings and cancer treatment. Spending more on one leaves less for the other. The opportunity cost of the choice is the buildings that are not built, which is the next best use of the money.

Exam use: opportunity cost is the next best alternative, shown in the form of what is lost.

A doctor becoming a teacher

A doctor with long hours and high stress could become a teacher instead. Staying a doctor has the opportunity cost of the salary and the lifestyle she would have as a teacher, which is the next best job, not every possible job.

Exam use: opportunity cost for an individual, in terms of income and satisfaction.

Fossil fuels

Oil and coal are limited in supply, while demand for energy grows. They are scarce because supply is not enough to meet all wants at a zero price. Using more fossil fuels for electricity leaves less for plastics and transport, so each use has an opportunity cost.

Exam use: scarcity of a natural resource and the choice of how to use it.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/21 · Q2(a) 8 marks Explain, with the aid of a production possibility curve diagram, why scarcity makes choice inevitable for firms and how each choice has an opportunity cost.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Diagram: both axes labelled with named goods (for example consumer goods and capital goods) and the PPC drawn as a curve or straight line showing a trade-off (2 marks).
  • Use the curve to explain scarcity (up to 2 marks), choice (up to 2) and opportunity cost (up to 2).
PlanPPC with labelled axes. Scarcity: only points on or inside are possible. Choice: pick a point. Opportunity cost: movement along the curve.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A production possibility curve shows the maximum combinations of two goods a firm can produce with its limited resources. Points on the curve are the most the firm can produce, and points outside are unattainable, which shows scarcity. Because resources are scarce, the firm must choose a point, for example more of good A means fewer of good B. Moving along the curve from one point to another is a choice, and the units of B that must be given up to get more A are the opportunity cost.

Based on Feb/March 2025 · 9708/22 · Q2(a) 8 marks With the help of a PPC diagram, explain the terms scarcity and choice and consider the extent to which every choice has an equal opportunity cost.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Accurate diagram: concave or straight line meeting both axes with labelled goods (1); explanation of scarcity and opportunity cost (1) and how scarce resources and infinite wants result in choices (1).
  • Analysis: scarcity is a point on the PPC; the opportunity cost is shown by a movement along it; for a straight line the cost is equal, for a concave PPC it increases the more resources are moved (3 marks).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): whether opportunity cost is equal depends on the shape of the PPC, or on whether there are unused resources at the time of the choice; reserve one mark for a justified conclusion.
PlanPPC. Define scarcity and choice. Straight line: constant cost. Concave: rising cost. Judge: depends on shape and on unused resources.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Scarcity is the problem that wants are unlimited while resources are limited. On a PPC with consumer goods and capital goods on the axes, the curve shows the most that can be produced, so scarcity forces a choice of one point. Moving along the curve towards more capital goods means fewer consumer goods, and this loss is the opportunity cost. If the PPC is a straight line the opportunity cost is equal for each unit, but on a concave curve the cost rises as more resources are moved because they are less suited to the new use.

So not every choice has an equal opportunity cost: it depends on the shape of the PPC. Also if the economy is inside the curve with unused resources, the opportunity cost of using them could be very small or zero.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/22 · Q3(a) 8 marks Explain how a production possibility curve can be used to demonstrate scarcity, choice and opportunity cost.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge of a PPC (up to 2): a curve showing the maximum output with current resources and technology; correct labels for two goods and the curve touching both axes.
  • Application: scarcity understood and shown as the frontier, with a position outside the curve unattainable (2 marks); choice as a movement along the curve (2); opportunity cost as the next best alternative given up as one product is sacrificed for the other (2).
  • A diagram is likely but is not essential if the idea is clear; each of the three ideas should be clearly separated.
PlanThree short paragraphs: scarcity, choice, opportunity cost, each tied to the diagram.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A production possibility curve is a line showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce given its resources and technology. With the axes labelled as agricultural goods and manufactured goods, the curve touches both axes. It shows scarcity because the frontier is a limit: points beyond it are not attainable with the existing resources. It shows choice because the economy has to pick one point on the curve, and moving from one point to another is a choice about what to produce. It shows opportunity cost because producing more manufactured goods means moving along the curve and giving up some agricultural goods, which is the next best alternative forgone.

Top tips

  • Define scarcity properly. Unlimited wants and limited resources. Not 'not enough'.
  • One alternative only. Opportunity cost is the next best alternative, not a list of everything given up.
  • Use a PPC. Label both axes with named goods and make the curve touch both axes. Show scarcity, choice and opportunity cost on it.
  • Give three levels of choice. Individuals, firms and governments, each with an example.
  • Say what is given up. In context, name the specific alternative: schools, buildings, wages, time.
  • Do not confuse scarcity and shortage. A shortage can be removed by a price change. Scarcity cannot.
  • Learn the three questions. What, how and for whom.
