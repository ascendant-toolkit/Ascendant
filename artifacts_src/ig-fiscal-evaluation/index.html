Fiscal Policy: Evaluation Technique

How to turn a good fiscal policy analysis into a Level 3 discussion: two developed sides and a conclusion that gives a reason. Every example, question and model answer here uses only fiscal policy (4.2) and the topics taught before it.

The big idea

Every fiscal policy question that carries 8 marks starts with Discuss whether or not…. To reach the top level you need two sides, each developed with a chain of reasoning, and a conclusion that gives a reason. That is all. Everything else on this page is optional help for developing a point.

What the mark scheme rewards

Part (d) of the Section B questions is marked in levels, not by ticking points. Fiscal policy questions are marked on exactly the same table as every other topic.

LevelMarksWhat it looks like on a fiscal policy question
36–8Both sides developed with clear cause and effect (for example tax cut → disposable income → consumption → AD). Real use of terms such as multiplier, deficit, progressive, elastic. A conclusion that gives a reason and recognises that the outcome could differ.
23–5Sensible analysis but often one-sided, or both sides listed with little development. The conclusion is missing or has no reason.
11–2Definitions and loose statements: "tax is money the government takes". Little or no chain of reasoning.
What changes between Level 2 and Level 3Not length. A Level 3 answer develops both sides with a reason at each step. "A tax cut might not raise spending" is Level 2. "A tax cut might not raise spending because households may save the extra income if they expect to pay higher taxes later" is Level 3.

Optional: seven ways to develop a point

If you are stuck for a second side, or for a reason in your conclusion, ask these seven questions. You do not need to use them all, or any of them if your reasoning is already developed.

AngleAsk yourselfSentence starter
1 · SizeHow big is the change?"A small cut in income tax may be saved rather than spent, but a large cut…"
2 · What it is spent onWhich type of spending or which tax?"This depends on whether the money is spent on education or on benefits…"
3 · Time lagsShort run or long run?"Spending on schools may take years to raise productivity, so in the short run…"
4 · Spare capacityIs the economy in a slump or close to full capacity?"If firms have spare capacity, output may rise; if not, the extra demand could mainly raise prices."
5 · MultiplierHow much leaks into saving, taxes and imports?"If much of the extra income is spent on imports, the multiplier may be small."
6 · ElasticityHow responsive are buyers (or firms) to the price or tax change?"If demand is inelastic, as it often is for tobacco, quantity may fall by only a little."
7 · Cost and conflictWhat is the opportunity cost, the effect on the deficit, or the other aim that suffers?"The spending could widen the deficit, so the government may need to borrow or raise taxes later."

Two sides for each fiscal policy

Past questions come back to the same six policies. Learn the reasons on each side. The "depends on" line gives you something to build your conclusion on.

Cut income tax

Why it might work: more disposable income → more consumption → AD rises → output and jobs; may encourage people to work more or enter the labour force.

Why it might not: may be saved or spent on imports; lower revenue limits spending on public services; may widen the deficit; benefits the rich more under a proportional cut.

Depends on: size of the cut, confidence, spare capacity, how revenue loss is covered.

Cut corporation tax (lower taxes on firms)

Why it might work: higher after-tax profit → more ability and incentive to invest → more output and jobs; may attract foreign firms.

Why it might not: firms may keep the profit instead of investing; less revenue for the government; foreign firms could replace local ones; more output may mean more pollution.

Depends on: business confidence, whether other conditions (skills, infrastructure) are good, what else the revenue would have funded.

Raise an indirect tax (demerit goods, external costs)

Why it might work: raises cost of production and price → quantity demanded falls → output moves towards the level where social cost equals social benefit; revenue can fund campaigns or merit goods.

Why it might not: inelastic demand (addiction); hard to measure the external cost; may fall hardest on the poor (regressive); avoidance, illegal markets, cross-border shopping; may be over-taxed.

Depends on: price elasticity of demand, size of the tax, available substitutes.

Increase government spending (health, education, housing, infrastructure)

Why it might work: raises AD, output and jobs directly through G and the multiplier; may correct under-consumption of merit goods; may improve productivity in the long run; supports equity.

Why it might not: opportunity cost; may need higher taxes or borrowing; bigger deficit; quality may be poor or the private sector may do it better; time lags; external costs during construction.

Depends on: what it is spent on, how efficiently, spare capacity, how it is financed.

Cut government spending

Why it might work: smaller deficit and less borrowing; lower AD can ease inflationary pressure; may free resources for the private sector.

Why it might not: AD falls so output and jobs may fall; public services and the poor could be hit; may raise poverty; savings may be small if the economy shrinks and revenue falls.

Depends on: which spending is cut, the size of the cut, the state of the economy.

Progressive tax and redistribution

Why it might work: takes a higher percentage from high earners to fund benefits and services for lower earners; acts as an automatic stabiliser.

Why it might not: may reduce effort, enterprise and saving; may encourage evasion or emigration of skilled workers; costs of collecting.

Depends on: the rates, how revenue is used, how easy it is to avoid.

The answer in three parts

PartWhat to writeExample on "an increase in government spending on healthcare"
Side 1: why it mightA developed reason, with a chain."Healthcare is a merit good that may be under-consumed. More spending means more provision, so people are healthier and may be more productive."
Side 2: why it might notA different developed reason."The money has an opportunity cost: it could have been spent on education. It may also need higher taxes or borrowing, which could widen the deficit."
ConclusionWhich side is stronger, and why."Overall, extra spending is more likely to benefit the economy if it is spent efficiently, because the productivity gain could outweigh the opportunity cost."

What the ten tell us

Two families appear. Tax questions (six of the ten): the effect of a tax on firms, on demerit goods or on the whole economy. Spending questions (four): government spending in general, on housing, on healthcare or to reduce unemployment. In every mark scheme the points are grouped as reasons "why it might" and "why it might not", and Level 3 needed both developed.

Model 1 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not lower taxes on firms will be beneficial for an economy.

Lower taxes on firms means a cut in corporation tax, the tax on firms' profits. A firm that pays less tax keeps more of its profit after tax.

Lower taxes on firms could benefit an economy because higher after-tax profits give firms both the ability and the incentive to invest in new capital goods. More investment is likely to raise aggregate demand and productive capacity, so output could rise and firms may take on more workers, reducing unemployment. A lower rate could also attract foreign firms to set up in the country, creating further jobs.

Lower taxes on firms may not benefit the economy because the government receives less tax revenue. That revenue could have paid for education, healthcare or roads, which can also raise productivity, so there is an opportunity cost and the budget deficit may widen. More output from firms could also create external costs such as pollution, and foreign firms attracted by low taxes might replace local ones.

Overall, lower taxes on firms are more likely to benefit an economy if the cut is moderate, firms are confident about the future and the government can still afford vital public services. In a weak economy where firms do not expect to sell more, the benefit is likely to be small and the loss of revenue could outweigh it.

Model 2 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not indirect taxation can reduce market failure.

Market failure occurs when the price mechanism fails to allocate resources efficiently, for example when demerit goods are over-consumed and create external costs. An indirect tax is a tax on spending, such as an excise duty.

Indirect taxation can reduce market failure by correcting over-consumption of demerit goods. An excise duty on cigarettes raises firms' costs and shifts supply to the left, so the price is likely to rise and quantity demanded to fall, moving output closer to the level where social cost equals social benefit. The revenue could also be spent on public goods or merit goods such as healthcare and education, which may be under-provided, reducing market failure a second way.

Indirect taxes may fail to reduce market failure for other reasons. It is difficult to measure the external cost, so the tax might be set too low to matter or too high, pushing consumption below the socially efficient level. Indirect taxes also tend to be regressive, falling more heavily on poorer households, and consumers might switch to untaxed imports or illegal markets.

Overall, indirect taxes are most likely to reduce market failure where demand is fairly price elastic and substitutes exist, and where the revenue is used for the good of those who pay it. For addictive goods, taxation is probably better combined with other measures such as education campaigns.

Model 3 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not an increase in government spending on housing would benefit an economy.

Government spending on housing is public spending to build or subsidise homes, and it is part of fiscal policy. An increase could benefit an economy because it adds directly to aggregate demand. Building firms would need more workers, so employment may rise, and the incomes they earn could be spent elsewhere, creating a multiplier effect and higher output. More homes could lower the price of housing and reduce homelessness, which may improve living standards and let more workers move to where jobs are, raising productivity.

An increase in spending on housing may not benefit the economy because it has an opportunity cost. The same money could have been spent on education or healthcare, which might raise productivity by more. If the spending is financed by higher taxes, disposable income and consumption could fall, and if it is financed by borrowing, the budget deficit and future interest payments may rise. The quality of government-built housing may also be low and construction can create external costs such as noise and loss of green space.

Overall, extra spending on housing is likely to benefit an economy if there is spare capacity, a genuine shortage of homes and an efficient way of building them. If the economy is already close to full capacity, or the spending is poorly targeted, the opportunity cost and the extra borrowing could outweigh the gains.

Level 2 versus Level 3, side by side

Question: Discuss whether or not high government spending can help a government achieve its macroeconomic aims.

A typical Level 2 answer

High government spending can help a government achieve its macroeconomic aims. It can create jobs because the government builds roads and hires workers. It can help economic growth because spending increases demand. It can help redistribute income through benefits. However, it might not work because the government might not have enough money. It could cause inflation. It could also make the deficit bigger. So I think high government spending is good.

Why Level 2: both sides appear but every point is a bare statement with no chain. "Inflation" and "deficit" are named, never explained. The conclusion gives no reason.

The same answer at Level 3

High government spending could help a government achieve its aims because it adds directly to aggregate demand. Spending on infrastructure creates demand for workers, and with the multiplier effect their extra income may be spent again, so real GDP and employment could rise. Spending on benefits and healthcare may also redistribute income towards the poor. However, the effect depends on spare capacity: if the economy is near full capacity, the extra demand could mainly raise the price level. Spending may also need to be paid for by borrowing or higher taxes, which could widen the deficit or reduce private spending, so the benefits may be smaller than the cost. Overall, high spending is more likely to help when there is a lot of unemployed capacity and the money goes to productive projects; at full capacity it could conflict with price stability.

Why Level 3: each effect is developed with a link (multiplier, AD, price level), the second side is developed with its own reasons (borrowing and taxes), and the conclusion says when spending is likely to help.

A 6-mark discussion

Model 4 Discuss 6 marks Discuss whether or not higher indirect taxes can reduce the market failure caused by demerit goods.
ReminderSix marks is shorter: one developed point each side and a one-line conclusion with a reason.

Higher indirect taxes could reduce the market failure because demerit goods are over-consumed and create external costs. A higher excise duty raises firms' costs and the price, so quantity demanded is likely to fall, moving output closer to where social cost equals social benefit. Fast food, alcohol and tobacco are examples. On the other hand, many demerit goods are addictive, so demand may be price inelastic and consumption could fall only slightly while consumers pay more. Some producers might also avoid the tax or sell on illegal markets. Overall, the tax is more likely to work for demerit goods with substitutes and elastic demand, and it may need to be combined with other policies, such as advertising bans or education, for addictive goods.

Top tips

  • Two sides, every time. "Whether or not" in the question means a paragraph for and a paragraph against.
  • Say what the policy is first, in one line. "A cut in corporation tax is a fall in the tax on firms' profits." Then move on.
  • Run the chain. Policy → who has more or less money → what they do → AD → output, jobs, prices, budget.
  • Give a reason at every step. "…because…", "…so…", "…which could…". A bare statement earns little.
  • Make side 2 a different argument, not the reverse of side 1.
  • Finish with a conclusion that gives a reason. Say which side is stronger and why, for example "more likely if the economy has spare capacity, because…".
  • Never conclude "it depends" and stop. Say what it depends on.
  • Hedge. "Could", "may", "is likely to". Fiscal policy outcomes are uncertain.
  • Stay inside the question. Use the multiplier or AD in one sentence; do not write an essay on them.
  • Aim for about 12 minutes. Two minutes to plan, ten to write.
