Cross Elasticity of Demand

2.2.3: the sign and size of XED, substitutes and complements, and its uses for businesses. Animated demand shifts, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Cross elasticity of demand (XED) measures how responsive the demand for one good (X) is to a change in the price of another good (Y): XED = % change in quantity demanded of X ÷ % change in price of Y. The sign shows the relationship: positive for substitutes (a rise in the price of Y raises demand for X) and negative for complements (a rise in the price of Y lowers demand for X); a value near zero means the goods are unrelated. The size shows how close the relationship is: the closer the substitutes or the stronger the complements, the bigger the number. Businesses use XED to predict the effect of rivals' price changes, to plan pricing and to understand joint demand for complementary products.

2 · Demand curves and related goods

  • If the price of a substitute rises, demand for X shifts right; if the price of a complement rises, demand for X shifts left.
  • Determinants of the size of the coefficient: how close the substitutes are, how important the complement is to using the good, and how broadly the market is defined.
  • XED also underlies joint demand, alternative demand, and derived demand ideas covered in the supply and demand chapter.

3 · Why it matters

  • Pricing: a firm with close substitutes (high positive XED) knows a rival's price cut could take away many customers.
  • Product strategy: firms selling complements (printers and ink) may price one low and the other high.
  • Competition policy: XED helps define the market, since close substitutes show who competes.
  • Limits: estimates may be unreliable, and other factors change at the same time.

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Two rival streaming services

If one raises its monthly fee, some customers switch to the other. The rival's demand rises, so XED is positive and fairly large.

Exam use: close substitutes.

Printers and ink

A fall in the price of printers raises demand for ink cartridges, since they are used together. XED is negative, and sellers may price printers low and ink high.

Exam use: complements.

Bread and butter

A rise in the price of butter may reduce the demand for bread a little. The two are weak complements, so XED is small and negative.

Exam use: weak complements.

Bread and rice

A rise in the price of rice may increase demand for bread somewhat. They are substitutes, but not very close ones.

Exam use: substitutes with a moderate XED.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/21 · Q3(a) 8 marks With the help of a formula, explain the meaning of cross elasticity of demand and consider which determinants are most important in establishing the size and sign of its coefficient.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): what cross elasticity of demand measures (1), the formula (1), and the distinction between positive and negative XED (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): why XED can be positive, for substitutes (1); why it can be negative, for complements (1); and the determinants of its magnitude, where the closer the relationship between two products the greater the size of XED (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a valid judgement on the relative importance of the determinants of the size and sign of XED (1) and a conclusion (1).
PlanFormula and meaning. Positive: substitutes. Negative: complements. Size: closeness of the relationship. Judge: closeness of relationship matters most for size; the nature of the link for sign.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Cross elasticity of demand measures how responsive the demand for one good is to a change in the price of another. It equals the percentage change in quantity demanded of good X divided by the percentage change in the price of good Y. It is positive for substitutes and negative for complements.

XED is positive when goods are substitutes, because a higher price for Y makes consumers switch to X, and negative for complements, because a higher price for Y lowers demand for the good used with it. The closer the relationship, whether substitutes or complements, the larger the size of the coefficient.

The nature of the relationship, whether the goods are used together or in place of each other, is likely to determine the sign, while how close the substitutes are, and how the market is defined, may be most important for the size. A narrowly defined market is likely to give a larger coefficient.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/21 · Q3(a) 8 marks The cross elasticity of demand for bread with respect to the price of rice is +1.4, while the cross elasticity of demand for bread with respect to the price of butter is -0.7. Explain why the cross elasticity of demand figures for bread differ.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): what is meant by XED (1) and the formula, the percentage change in the quantity demanded of good A divided by the percentage change in the price of good B (1).
  • Application (up to 6): application to goods as substitutes or positive XED (1), to bread and rice as substitutes (1) and to the figure showing a strong relationship (1); application to goods as complements or negative XED (1), to bread and butter as complements (1) and to the figure showing a weaker relationship (1).
PlanXED formula. Bread and rice: positive, substitutes, +1.4 strong. Bread and butter: negative, complements, -0.7 weaker.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Cross elasticity of demand is the percentage change in the quantity demanded of one good divided by the percentage change in the price of another good.

The figure for rice is positive because rice and bread are substitutes: if the price of rice rises, consumers buy more bread. The value of 1.4 is above one, which suggests a strong relationship, so a given rise in the price of rice leads to a larger percentage rise in demand for bread. The figure for butter is negative because butter and bread are complements, as they are used together, so a rise in the price of butter lowers demand for bread. The value of 0.7 is smaller in size, which suggests a weaker relationship.

Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/21 · Q3(b) 12 marks Discuss the extent to which knowledge of the income elasticity of demand for its product is likely to be more useful to a business than knowledge of the cross elasticity of demand for its product.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the usefulness of YED to a business, with appropriate scenarios (up to 4), such as normal and inferior goods, the development of the economy and planning during a boom or recession; and of XED (up to 4), such as substitutes or alternative demand (positive XED) and complements or joint demand (negative XED).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether YED is likely to be more useful than XED, with a conclusion (1 mark reserved).
PlanYED: boom, recession, normal and inferior goods, planning. XED: rivals' prices, substitutes, complements. Judge: depends on whether income or competitors drive the business.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

YED helps a business forecast how demand will respond to changes in income, so it can plan production through a boom or a recession and decide whether its product is normal or inferior. XED shows how demand will respond to a change in the price of a substitute or complement, so a firm can predict the effect of a rival's price cut or of price changes of a complementary good.

YED may be more useful for long-term planning, especially where the product is income elastic, such as holidays or cars, while XED is likely to be more useful in a competitive market with close substitutes, where rival pricing matters most. Which is more useful depends on the product and the market.

Top tips

  • Formula first. % change in Q of X ÷ % change in price of Y.
  • Check which price. It is the price of the other good.
  • Sign gives the link. Positive substitutes, negative complements.
  • Size gives closeness. Bigger number, closer relationship.
  • Use examples. Rival brands; printers and ink.
  • Say what a firm does. Predict rivals' effects, set prices.
  • Evaluate. Estimates change; markets are defined differently.
