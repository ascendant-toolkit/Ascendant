Effects of Exchange Rate Changes

6.4.5: how a depreciation or appreciation affects trade, AD, AS, inflation and employment. AD/AS animations, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

A depreciation makes exports cheaper in foreign currency and imports dearer in domestic currency. If the combined price elasticities of demand for exports and imports exceed one (the Marshall-Lerner condition), net exports rise, so AD shifts right, raising real output, employment and, close to full capacity, the price level (demand-pull); but dearer imported raw materials and components raise costs, so SRAS may shift left (cost-push). The effect on the current account may be delayed, because demand adjusts slowly (the J-curve), and the terms of trade usually worsen. An appreciation has the opposite effects: lower import prices and downward pressure on inflation, but less competitive exports, lower net exports, and possible falls in output and employment. The net result depends on elasticities, spare capacity, reliance on imported inputs and time.

2 · Why the effects may be limited

  • Elasticity: if demand for exports and imports is inelastic (sum below one), a depreciation may worsen the trade balance, as import spending rises more than export revenue.
  • J-curve: in the short run, contracts and habits mean the balance may get worse before it improves.
  • Spare capacity: if the economy is near full capacity, the extra demand mainly raises prices rather than output.
  • Imported inputs: a high share of imported raw materials makes cost-push inflation more likely.
  • Retaliation: other countries may respond by depreciating too.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A weaker currency helps exporters

When a currency falls, exporters can cut their prices abroad, or keep the same price and earn more at home. Orders may rise, but only if buyers respond to the lower prices.

Exam use: a depreciation and export competitiveness.

Dearer imported fuel

A country that imports most of its fuel finds petrol and heating costs rise after a depreciation. Firms and households face higher costs, adding to inflation.

Exam use: imported inputs and cost-push inflation.

The J-curve

After a currency falls, existing import and export contracts continue for months. The trade deficit may widen at first and only narrow later as volumes adjust.

Exam use: the J-curve.

A strong currency squeezes manufacturers

When a currency rises, manufacturers selling abroad face dearer exports and may lose orders, while consumers enjoy cheaper imports and lower inflation.

Exam use: an appreciation and its effects.

Based on Feb/March 2024 · 9708/22 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain what is meant by a depreciation of the exchange rate and consider whether a depreciation is likely to increase domestic real output.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): an accurate definition of the exchange rate (1); a depreciation is likely if the demand for a currency falls (1) or its supply increases (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): a depreciation reduces the perceived price of exports and increases the perceived price of imports (1), which raises net exports (X - M) and aggregate demand (1) and so the level of domestic real output (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): this assumes the economy is not at full capacity (1) or depends on the elasticity of demand for traded goods (1); the second mark is for a justified conclusion.
PlanDefine the exchange rate and depreciation. Cheaper exports, dearer imports: X-M up, AD up, output up. Evaluate: spare capacity, elasticities.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. A depreciation is a fall in the value of a floating currency, which may be caused by a fall in demand for it or an increase in its supply.

A depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home. If demand for them is elastic enough, exports rise and imports fall, so net exports increase. This raises aggregate demand, which could increase real output and employment.

The rise in output depends on spare capacity: if the economy is already near full capacity, the extra AD may raise prices rather than output. It also depends on elasticities, since if the sum of the price elasticities is below one, net exports may fall. Overall, a depreciation could raise output, but not always.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/22 · Q5(a) 8 marks Explain the difference between cost-push and demand-pull inflation and consider which is more likely to occur if there is a depreciation in the exchange rate of a country with few natural resources.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): cost-push inflation is caused by rises in the costs of production (1); demand-pull inflation is caused by increases in AD not matched by increases in AS (1); a clear explanation of the differences (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): a depreciation lowers the price of exports for trading partners and raises the price of imports. Demand-pull: the effect on X - M and AD, with reference to the PED of exports and imports (maximum 2 if only this is considered). Cost-push: the importance of imports, and that a country with few natural resources probably must import its inputs (maximum 2 if only this is considered).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): considers that the country has few natural resources (1) and reaches a justified conclusion on which type of inflation is more likely (1).
PlanDefine both types. Depreciation: AD up (demand-pull) and import costs up (cost-push). Few resources: imported inputs, so cost-push is likely.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Cost-push inflation is caused by a rise in the costs of production, which shifts SRAS to the left. Demand-pull inflation is caused by a rise in aggregate demand that is not matched by a rise in aggregate supply.

A depreciation raises the price of imports and lowers the price of exports for foreigners. Cheaper exports and dearer imports may raise net exports, increasing AD and creating demand-pull pressure, depending on the elasticities of demand. At the same time a country with few natural resources probably has to import raw materials and fuel, so its costs of production rise, which shifts SRAS left and creates cost-push inflation.

Cost-push inflation is probably the more likely outcome, because the country relies on imported inputs, so the rise in costs is likely to be large and immediate. Demand-pull pressure may be smaller if demand for exports is inelastic or if there is spare capacity.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/21 · Q5(a) 8 marks With the help of a diagram(s), explain the likely causes of a depreciation of a floating exchange rate and consider whether such a depreciation is always beneficial to an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): a correctly labelled diagram of the demand for and supply of a currency (1), with appropriate shifts (1) and a fall in the exchange rate (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): uses the diagram to explain what could shift demand for the currency left and/or supply right, and analyses the potential advantages and disadvantages of a depreciation.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a valid judgement on whether the benefits of a depreciation always outweigh the disadvantages (1) and a conclusion (1).
PlanDiagram: D left or S right. Causes: lower exports, lower interest rates, more imports. Benefits: exports, AD. Costs: import prices, inflation. Judge: not always.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The diagram shows the exchange rate against the quantity of the currency, with demand and supply curves. The demand curve shifts left or the supply curve shifts right, so the equilibrium exchange rate falls.

Demand for the currency may fall if foreign demand for exports falls or interest rates are cut, and supply may rise if imports increase. A depreciation makes exports cheaper, which could raise AD, output and employment, and may help reduce a current account deficit. But imports become dearer, which may raise costs and prices and reduce living standards.

A depreciation is not always beneficial. It is more likely to help if demand for exports and imports is elastic and there is spare capacity, and less beneficial if the economy relies on imported inputs.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/21 · Q4(b) 12 marks Discuss whether an appreciation of a country's foreign exchange rate is likely to cause both a rise in inflation and a decrease in employment in its economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the effect of an appreciation on the level of inflation (up to 4); the effect of an appreciation on the level of employment (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether an appreciation is likely to cause both a rise in inflation and a fall in employment, with a conclusion (1 mark reserved).
PlanAppreciation: imports cheaper, so inflation may fall, not rise. Exports dearer, so net exports and employment may fall. Judge: not both.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

An appreciation makes imports cheaper in domestic currency, which lowers the cost of imported raw materials and goods, so cost-push inflation could fall rather than rise. It also reduces the price of imported goods in the price index. Exports become dearer abroad, so demand for them may fall, and imports become cheaper so more are bought. Net exports fall, AD may fall, and output and employment in export industries could decrease.

An appreciation is likely to decrease employment, especially in export-led industries, but it is unlikely to cause a rise in inflation, because import prices fall and AD is lower. So the two effects are unlikely to occur together. The effect on employment depends on elasticities and spare capacity.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/24 · Q5(b) 12 marks Assess whether the depreciation of an exchange rate is always beneficial to an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): what a depreciation means; the potential advantages, such as an increase in exports as prices fall, which can help reduce a current account deficit and raise AD; and the potential disadvantages, such as dearer imports of finished goods and raw materials, higher costs of production and cost-push inflation.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): balance, considering alternatives and the conditions in which a depreciation may or may not be beneficial, with a conclusion.
PlanDefine. Benefits: cheaper exports, deficit narrows, AD up. Costs: dearer imports, cost-push inflation. Judge: depends on elasticities, spare capacity, imported inputs.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A depreciation is a fall in the value of a floating currency. It makes exports cheaper abroad, which could increase export sales, narrow a current account deficit and raise AD, output and employment. But it also makes imports dearer, including raw materials, so costs of production could rise and cause cost-push inflation, and households may see their living standards fall because imported goods are dearer.

A depreciation is not always beneficial. It is more likely to help when demand for exports and imports is elastic, there is spare capacity, and the economy does not depend heavily on imported inputs. If those conditions do not hold, the costs may outweigh the benefits.

Top tips

  • Follow the chain. Rate, relative prices, X and M, AD, output.
  • Mention elasticity. Sum above one (Marshall-Lerner) for a better trade balance.
  • Mention time. J-curve: it may worsen before it improves.
  • Both AD and AS. AD right, but SRAS may shift left.
  • Use the context. Few natural resources: imported inputs.
  • Spare capacity. Near full capacity, prices rise more than output.
  • Judge. Not always beneficial; depends on conditions.
