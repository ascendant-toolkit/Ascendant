Links between the Value of Money, the Balance of Payments, Inflation and Growth

10.2.1 to 10.2.4: the internal and external value of money, inflation and the balance of payments, growth and inflation, and growth and the balance of payments. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Macroeconomic problems are interrelated. The internal value of money (its purchasing power at home, the inverse of the price level) and its external value (the exchange rate and purchasing power abroad) move together: higher inflation at home tends to weaken the currency, and a weaker currency raises import prices and inflation. Inflation worsens the balance of payments by hurting competitiveness. Fast growth raises inflation once the economy nears capacity, and raises imports, so it can worsen the current account. A good answer traces these chains and says when each link is strong or weak.

1 · Internal and external value of money (10.2.1)

Internal value

What a unit of the currency buys at home. It falls when the price level rises: inflation reduces the internal value.

External value

What the currency buys abroad, shown by the exchange rate. A depreciation lowers the external value, an appreciation raises it.

  • Inflation to the exchange rate: if prices rise faster at home than abroad, exports become dearer and imports cheaper. Demand for the currency falls and supply rises, so the currency tends to depreciate. The theory of purchasing power parity says the exchange rate should adjust until the same goods cost the same in both currencies.
  • Exchange rate to inflation: a depreciation raises the price of imported goods and raw materials, which raises the price level (imported inflation). An appreciation lowers import prices and helps cut inflation, but makes exports dearer.
  • Vicious and virtuous circles: inflation, depreciation and more inflation can feed on one another. Low inflation, a stronger currency and cheaper imports can do the same in reverse.
  • Fixed rates: under a fixed or managed rate a high-inflation country's currency becomes overvalued, which causes a deficit and pressure to devalue.

2 · The balance of payments and inflation (10.2.2)

  • Inflation leads to a deficit: if domestic prices rise faster than those of trading partners, price competitiveness falls, exports fall and imports rise. The size of the effect depends on price elasticities (Marshall-Lerner condition).
  • A deficit can lead to inflation: a falling currency raises import prices. Persistent deficits also lead to depreciation and cost-push inflation.
  • Tackling inflation can help the current account: deflationary policy lowers spending on imports and prices at home. But higher interest rates may attract hot money and raise the exchange rate, which could hurt exports.
  • Low inflation and a surplus may reinforce each other, as in some export-led economies.

3 · Growth and inflation (10.2.3)

  • Demand-led growth: when AD rises with spare capacity, output rises with little inflation. Near full capacity (the vertical LRAS), extra demand raises prices, so growth above the sustainable rate brings demand-pull inflation.
  • Supply-led growth: growth from productivity and capacity (an LRAS shift) allows faster growth without inflation.
  • Inflation can harm growth: uncertainty raises the risk of investment, savings lose value, and tight policy to curb inflation slows growth. Moderate stable inflation is consistent with growth.
  • Cost-push shocks (oil prices) cause inflation and lower growth together (stagflation).

4 · Growth and the balance of payments (10.2.4)

  • Growth raises imports: higher incomes increase demand for imports, especially if the marginal propensity to import or the income elasticity of demand for imports is high, so the current account worsens. This is the import suction effect.
  • Export-led growth: growth driven by exports improves the current account and raises income through the multiplier.
  • Supply-side growth raises competitiveness, which can improve the current account.
  • Developing economies may need to import capital goods to grow, which widens the deficit until the new capacity raises exports. FDI inflows can finance the gap.

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Turkey's currency and inflation

In the early 2020s the Turkish lira lost most of its value against the dollar while inflation rose above 50%. Import prices rose with the falling currency, and the higher prices added to pressure on the lira.

Exam use: a vicious circle between the external and internal value of money. Add that policy choices on interest rates mattered.

The UK after the 2016 vote to leave the EU

The pound fell sharply after the referendum. Import prices rose, which added to inflation over the next two years, while exports became cheaper to buyers abroad.

Exam use: depreciation raising inflation and the slow effect on the current account (J curve). Note that other factors also moved.

China's rapid growth and imports

As incomes rose, China's demand for raw materials, energy and capital goods rose strongly. Its surpluses stayed large because exports grew even faster, but countries that sold it commodities benefited.

Exam use: growth raising imports, and why export growth can offset it. Link to the marginal propensity to import.

Overheating in an emerging economy

When growth is well above the trend rate, firms and workers can raise prices and wages, inflation rises and imports surge. Central banks often respond by raising interest rates.

Exam use: growth above capacity causing inflation and a deficit together, which links all three objectives.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/42 · Q6(a) 12 marks Explain how changes in the internal value of a country's currency can affect the external value of that currency.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Define the internal value (purchasing power at home, linked to inflation) and the external value (the exchange rate). Define both terms clearly for the top levels.
  • Link: higher inflation than trading partners makes exports dearer and imports cheaper, which reduces demand for the currency and raises its supply, so it depreciates. Use purchasing power parity.
  • Comment on the exchange rate regime: under a floating rate the rate adjusts; under a fixed rate pressure builds for devaluation or reserves fall.
  • Level 4 (9 to 12) needs the relationship analysed, not just the two definitions.
PlanDefine both. Chain from inflation to competitiveness to demand for the currency. Fixed versus floating. Possible reverse link.
Based on Feb/March 2021 · 9708/42 · Q7(b) 13 marks To what extent do you agree that it is not possible to solve a persistent balance of payments deficit without sacrificing other key macroeconomic aims?
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explain what a persistent deficit is and emphasise the word 'persistent'.
  • Policies: expenditure-reducing (higher taxes, higher interest rates) which cut imports but may hurt growth and employment while helping inflation; expenditure-switching (depreciation, tariffs) which may raise inflation or provoke retaliation; supply-side policy which improves competitiveness without those costs but works slowly.
  • A conclusion that weighs the conflicts: it is largely true in the short run, less so if supply-side policy raises competitiveness.
PlanPersistent deficit. Three policy types and what each costs. Time horizon. Judgement.
Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/42 · Q6(b) 13 marks Discuss whether a government can achieve all its key macroeconomic aims at the same time.
What the mark scheme wants
  • State the aims: low unemployment, low inflation, growth and a balanced current account (and sometimes distribution and sustainability).
  • Use AD and AS to show what happens with spare capacity and near full employment. The Phillips curve trade-off, growth causing imports and inflation, and policy conflicts are examples.
  • Level 4 needs two conflicts analysed with a diagram or other analysis, and a conclusion on whether all aims can be met together (rarely, though supply-side growth and good luck may allow it for a time).
PlanTwo conflicts with diagrams. Role of spare capacity and time. Conclude 'rarely, and only temporarily'.
Practice (not a past paper question) 8 marks Explain why economic growth might lead to a deficit on the current account.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Growth raises incomes, which raises spending on imports; the effect depends on the marginal propensity to import or income elasticity of demand for imports.
  • If exports do not grow as fast as imports, X − M falls. Inflation from fast growth can also reduce competitiveness.
  • Evaluation: export-led or supply-side growth may improve the current account, and capital goods imports may raise future exports.
PlanHigher income, higher imports. Condition on exports and competitiveness. One reason the deficit might not appear.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Economic growth is a rise in real GDP, and a deficit on the current account means that the value of imports of goods and services plus net income and transfers exceeds exports. As real incomes rise, households and firms spend more, and part of the extra spending goes on imports. If the marginal propensity to import is high, as it is when the economy does not make many of the goods people want, imports could rise faster than exports. Fast growth may also raise inflation, so exports become less competitive, which adds to the deficit.

However, the deficit is not inevitable. If growth is export-led, exports rise with output and the current account may improve, and if the economy imports capital goods that raise productive capacity, exports could grow later. The effect therefore depends on the source of growth and the elasticities.

Practice (not a past paper question) 8 marks Explain how high inflation might affect a country's balance of payments.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Domestic prices rising faster than abroad reduce price competitiveness: exports fall and imports rise.
  • The size depends on price elasticities of demand for exports and imports (Marshall-Lerner).
  • Currency effect: the current account deficit and weaker demand may cause depreciation, which can partly restore competitiveness but raises import prices.
  • Evaluation: non-price factors, long-term contracts and time lags (J curve) mean the effect may be small or delayed.
PlanCompetitiveness chain. Elasticity condition. Exchange rate response. A limit.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

High inflation reduces the internal value of a country's money and can reduce the external value too. If prices in the country rise faster than in its trading partners, its exports become more expensive for foreign buyers and imports become cheaper for domestic consumers, so export volumes fall and import volumes rise and the current account moves towards deficit. The size of the effect depends on price elasticities: if demand for exports and imports is elastic, the volume changes are large.

The deficit also increases the supply of the currency relative to demand, so the exchange rate may depreciate, which restores some competitiveness but raises the price of imported goods and could add to inflation again. However, if exports are mainly differentiated goods where quality matters more than price, or if demand is inelastic in the short run, the current account may change little for a time, so the link is weaker than the simple theory suggests.

Top tips

  • Define both values of money. Internal: purchasing power at home, linked to inflation. External: the exchange rate and purchasing power abroad.
  • Show the chain. Inflation, then competitiveness, then demand for the currency, then the exchange rate, then import prices, then inflation again.
  • Add the condition. Elasticities of demand (Marshall-Lerner), spare capacity, or the exchange rate system.
  • Growth and BoP: say 'import suction'. Higher income raises imports, depending on the marginal propensity to import.
  • Growth and inflation: mention capacity. Demand-led growth is inflationary near the LRAS, supply-led growth is not.
  • Use both directions. The links run both ways, and policy can reverse them.
  • Name a policy and a cost. Raising interest rates fights inflation and may attract hot money, which could hurt exports.
