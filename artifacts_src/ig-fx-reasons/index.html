Reasons for Buying and Selling Foreign Currencies

Trade, speculation, government intervention, profits and dividends, remittances and investment: why currencies are bought and sold. Syllabus 6.3.2.

The big idea

People, firms and governments buy and sell foreign currencies because they need them to pay for things abroad, to invest, or to make money. The syllabus lists six reasons: trade in goods and services, speculation, government intervention in currency markets, payment of profit, interest and dividends, workers' remittances and investment in capital goods between countries. Each creates demand for or supply of a currency, which in a floating system sets its price.

The six reasons

ReasonDemand for the UK pound (foreigners buying £)Supply of the UK pound (UK residents selling £)
Trade in goods and servicesForeigners buy UK exports and need pounds to pay UK firms; foreign tourists in the UKUK firms and people buy imports and need foreign currency; UK tourists abroad
SpeculationSpeculators buy pounds expecting the pound to rise, to sell later at a profitSpeculators sell pounds expecting it to fall
Government interventionThe central bank buys pounds with its foreign currency reserves to raise or defend the rateThe central bank sells pounds (buys foreign currency) to lower the rate
Payment of profit, interest and dividendsUK firms and investors abroad send profits, interest and dividends home (converted into pounds)Foreign firms and investors in the UK send profits, interest and dividends abroad
Workers' remittancesUK workers abroad send money homeMigrants in the UK send money to their home countries
Investment in capital goods and firms between countriesForeign firms invest in UK factories and shares: they need poundsUK firms invest abroad: they need foreign currency

Demand for and supply of a currency

Demand for a country's currency comes from…

  • Foreigners buying its exports and services
  • Foreign tourists and students
  • Foreign investment into the country
  • Profits and remittances sent home from abroad
  • Speculators expecting it to rise
  • The central bank buying it

Supply of a country's currency comes from…

  • Residents buying imports
  • Residents travelling and studying abroad
  • Investment by residents and firms abroad
  • Profits and remittances sent abroad
  • Speculators expecting it to fall
  • The central bank selling it

The key idea: when you buy something from another country you supply your own currency and demand theirs. When foreigners buy from you they demand your currency.

Examples

  • A US importer buying British cars must sell dollars and buy pounds: it increases the demand for pounds.
  • A British family going on holiday to Spain sells pounds and buys euros: it increases the supply of pounds and the demand for euros.
  • A foreign investor buying shares in a UK company must buy pounds: demand for pounds rises.
  • A Filipino nurse working in London sends money home: she sells pounds and buys pesos.
  • A speculator who expects the Turkish lira to fall sells lira: the supply of lira rises and the rate may fall.

Tourism and the Thai baht

Millions of tourists must buy Thai baht to pay for hotels and meals in Thailand, supporting demand for the currency.

Exam use: Demand from tourism.

Remittances to the Philippines and Nepal

Money sent home by workers abroad is a large share of GDP in several countries, adding to the demand for their currencies.

Exam use: Workers' remittances.

Black Wednesday, 1992

Speculators sold large amounts of pounds expecting a fall, and the UK was forced out of the European exchange rate mechanism.

Exam use: Speculation moving a currency.

Central bank intervention

Switzerland and Japan have sold their own currency to stop it rising too fast and hurting exporters.

Exam use: Government intervention in currency markets.

Q1 Identify 2 marks Identify two reasons why people buy foreign currency.
ReminderImports, tourism abroad, investment abroad, remittances, speculation.
To pay for imports of goods and services. To invest abroad (or to go on holiday abroad, or speculate).
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain two reasons why foreigners may want to buy a country's currency.
ReminderExports, tourism, investment, remittances, speculation.
Foreigners buy the currency to pay for the country's exports. An importer in another country must exchange its own currency for the exporter's to pay, so demand for the exporter's currency rises. Foreign firms and investors buy it to invest in the country. To build factories or buy shares they need local currency, increasing demand.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse the causes of an increase in the demand for foreign currency. (Feb/Mar 2025, P22)
ReminderFrom a country's view: imports, investment abroad, tourism abroad, remittances out, profits out, speculation. Chain each.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Residents need foreign currency to pay for imports. If incomes rise, spending on imports rises, so more foreign currency is demanded. Firms and individuals investing abroad must buy foreign currency, so greater investment overseas increases demand for it. More citizens travelling abroad on holiday or to study need foreign currency. Foreign firms sending profits home and workers sending remittances abroad convert domestic currency to foreign currency. If speculators expect the foreign currency to rise, they also buy it.
Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain how speculation can change an exchange rate.
ReminderExpect a rise: buy; expect a fall: sell; self-fulfilling.
Speculators buy or sell a currency to make a profit from expected changes in its value. If they expect a currency to fall, they sell it, increasing its supply, which lowers its value. Others may follow, so the fall can be self-fulfilling. The central bank may have to intervene by buying the currency.

Top tips

  • Six reasons: trade, speculation, government intervention, profits and dividends, remittances, investment.
  • Foreigners buying from us demand our currency. Us buying from them supplies ours.
  • Give both demand and supply examples. Exports and imports, investment in and out.
  • Speculation can be self-fulfilling. Expectations move the rate.
  • In a floating system these flows set the rate. In a fixed system the central bank offsets them.
