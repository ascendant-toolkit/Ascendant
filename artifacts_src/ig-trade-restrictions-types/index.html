Types of Trade Restrictions

Tariffs, import quotas, subsidies and embargoes: what they are and how each affects a market. Syllabus 6.2.4.

The big idea

The syllabus lists four methods of protection (trade restrictions): tariffs, import quotas, subsidies and embargoes. All aim to protect domestic producers from foreign competition, but they work differently, and each has different effects on prices, quantities, the government and trading partners. Tariffs are tariff barriers; the others are non-tariff barriers.

The four methods

MethodWhat it isMain effects
TariffA tax on imports (or exports) that raises their priceImports fall, domestic output rises, consumers pay more, the government gains revenue
Import quotaA limit on the quantity of a good that can be importedImports fixed, supply falls, price rises; no tax revenue (the gain goes to importers)
SubsidyA payment by the government to domestic producers that lowers their costsDomestic producers can charge less and supply more, so imports fall; costs taxpayers
EmbargoA total ban on trade with a country or on a productImports (or exports) of that product stop; supply falls sharply and prices rise

Other non-tariff barriers include rules and regulations (quality, safety and labelling standards), complicated customs procedures (red tape), voluntary export restraints and exchange controls.

How each affects a market

  • Tariff: world price $40 plus a $10 tariff makes the domestic price $50. Quantity demanded falls, domestic supply rises, imports fall, and the government collects $10 on each unit imported.
  • Quota: limiting imports to a fixed number reduces supply, so the domestic price rises. The effect on the price can be the same as a tariff, but there is no tax revenue.
  • Subsidy: shifts the domestic supply curve to the right (costs fall), so domestic output rises and the market price falls, but the government pays for it.
  • Embargo: imports are zero, so the country relies on its own production, which usually means a higher price and less choice.

Why a government may subsidise

Subsidies can protect infant industries, help exporters compete abroad, reduce food prices for consumers, secure food supplies (a strategic aim), encourage production with external benefits (such as renewable energy) or support jobs in a declining industry. Costs: taxpayers pay, they can distort markets, and other countries may object that they are unfair.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

US steel tariffs

In 2018 the United States put tariffs on imported steel and aluminium, raising prices for US firms that use them and prompting retaliation from other countries.

Exam use: A tariff and its effects.

Quotas on imports

Some countries limit imports of sugar or textiles by quota. Quota holders can earn high profits because the quantity is restricted.

Exam use: A quota with no tax revenue.

Subsidies to farmers

The EU's Common Agricultural Policy and the US farm programmes pay subsidies to farmers, helping them compete with imports but costing taxpayers.

Exam use: Subsidies as protection.

Russia embargo

After 2022 many countries banned imports of Russian oil and gas and exports of certain technology, an embargo with major effects on prices.

Exam use: An embargo for political reasons.

Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain two non-tariff methods of protection. (Oct/Nov 2020, P21)
ReminderQuota, subsidy, embargo, regulations; each with a description.
An import quota is a limit on the quantity of imports that can enter a country, so supply from abroad is limited and domestic firms sell more. A subsidy is a grant to domestic firms, which lowers their costs so they can charge lower prices and compete better with imports.
Q4 Explain 4 marks Explain two reasons why a government may subsidise food production. (Oct/Nov 2023, P22)
ReminderLower food prices, food security, support farmers' incomes, correct market failure, reduce imports.
To reduce the price of food. A subsidy lowers farmers' costs so food can be sold more cheaply, helping low-income households afford basic necessities. To raise food self-sufficiency. More domestic production reduces dependence on imports, making supplies more secure and improving the current account.
Q5 Analyse 5 marks Analyse, using a demand and supply diagram, how a subsidy given to producers could affect the market for electric cars. (May/June 2020, P22)
ReminderDraw S shifting right to S₁, label P to P₁, Q to Q₁; explain lower price, higher quantity.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A subsidy lowers producers' costs of making electric cars, so the supply curve shifts to the right from S to S₁. At the original price firms now supply more, so the equilibrium price falls from P to P₁ and the quantity bought rises from Q to Q₁. Cheaper electric cars may raise sales and help reduce pollution. The government pays for the subsidy, which has an opportunity cost.
Q6 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how an import tariff affects a market for a good.
ReminderDraw: price rises, domestic supply up, imports down, revenue. Evaluate: consumers lose.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A tariff is a tax on imports, so the price of imported goods rises. Consumers buy less at the higher price, and domestic producers face less competition, so they supply more. Imports fall, and the government earns tariff revenue on the imports that remain. For example, a $10 tariff on a $40 import raises the price to $50. However, consumers pay more and have less choice, and other countries may retaliate.

Top tips

  • Four methods: tariffs, quotas, subsidies, embargoes.
  • Tariff = tax. Quota = limit. Subsidy = payment. Embargo = ban. Define each.
  • Draw the diagram. Tariff: price up. Subsidy: supply right. Label P, P₁, Q, Q₁.
  • Quota gives no revenue. Tariffs do.
  • Evaluate with consumers and retaliation. Higher prices, less choice, trade war risk.
