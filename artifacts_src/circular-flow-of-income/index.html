The Circular Flow of Income, Injections and Withdrawals

How money moves between households, firms, banks, the government and the rest of the world, what counts as an injection or a withdrawal, and why national income settles where J = W. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

The circular flow of income shows money moving between households and firms: households receive income from firms for the factors they supply and spend it on goods and services. Not all income is re-spent: withdrawals (leakages) are saving (S), taxes (T) and imports (M), while injections are investment (I), government spending (G) and exports (X). National income is in equilibrium when injections equal withdrawals (J = W). If J is greater than W, spending is above income and national income, output and employment rise; if W is greater than J, they fall.

1 · From two sectors to four

  • Two sectors (households and firms): households supply factors and receive income (Y); they spend it on output (C); firms pay out all they receive.
  • Financial sector: households may save (S, a withdrawal); firms borrow to invest (I, an injection).
  • Government: collects taxes (T, a withdrawal) and spends on goods, services and transfers (G, an injection).
  • Foreign sector: residents buy imports (M, a withdrawal) and foreigners buy exports (X, an injection).
Injections J = I + G + X  ·  Withdrawals W = S + T + M  ·  Equilibrium when J = W

Transfer payments (benefits) are not an injection of their own: they are negative taxes, so they reduce T. Spending by foreign tourists is an export; spending by residents abroad is an import.

3 · What happens when J and W are not equal

Injections greater than withdrawals

More is being spent than earned. Firms sell more, so they produce more and hire more workers. National income, output and employment rise, and withdrawals rise with income (for example, people save and pay more tax) until they match the injections again.

Withdrawals greater than injections

Spending falls short of income. Firms find stocks building up, cut output and jobs, and national income falls until withdrawals have fallen to equal injections.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A government infrastructure programme

When a government spends on roads and railways without raising taxes by the same amount, G is greater than T: a net injection. Construction firms hire workers, who spend their wages, and national income may rise.

Exam use: a rise in G as an injection. Say whether there is spare capacity and how far income might rise.

A tourism boom

Foreign visitors spending in hotels, restaurants and shops are buying a country's exports. A rise in tourist numbers increases X, an injection, and incomes in the local economy rise.

Exam use: services exports as an injection (a Paper 3 question noted that tourists visiting another country are not an injection for their own country).

A rise in household saving

If households become worried about the future and save more, saving (a withdrawal) rises. Unless investment rises as well, withdrawals exceed injections and national income falls.

Exam use: why a rise in saving can reduce national income in the short run.

A fall in interest rates

Lower interest rates reduce the reward for saving, so saving may fall, and make borrowing cheaper, so consumption and investment may rise. Withdrawals fall and injections rise.

Exam use: a Paper 4 question asked for the impact of a fall in interest rates on output and employment using a circular flow diagram.

Feb/March 2022 · 9708/42 · Q7(a) 12 marks Consider, with the help of a circular flow diagram, the potential impact of a fall in the rate of interest on the level of output and the level of employment.
What the mark scheme wants
  • An accurate, labelled three sector circular flow diagram with injections and withdrawals identified, the existing equilibrium and the full employment level of income, with the links between expenditure, output and employment explained.
  • Analysis of the effect of a fall in interest rates on at least one withdrawal (saving may fall, imports may rise) and one injection (investment, perhaps exports), each with an evaluative comment.
  • Evaluation: the effect also depends on the multiplier (a fall in the marginal propensity to save raises it), on how interest-elastic investment is, on business confidence, and on how much spare capacity exists. A conclusion is needed for the top level.
KANE

In a three sector circular flow, households and firms exchange factor payments and spending, with saving (S), taxes (T) as withdrawals and investment (I) and government spending (G) as injections. National income is in equilibrium where J = W, and output and employment are linked to the level of spending.

A fall in the rate of interest could reduce the reward for saving, so saving might fall and consumer spending rise, which reduces a withdrawal. It also makes borrowing cheaper, so firms may invest more, which raises an injection. With J greater than W, spending is above income, firms produce more and employ more workers, and national income rises towards the full employment level. The size of the effect may depend on how responsive investment is to the interest rate, and on business confidence: if firms are pessimistic, investment may rise only slightly.

The rise in income is likely to be magnified by the multiplier, because the extra spending becomes income for others, and a fall in the marginal propensity to save raises the multiplier. However, if the economy is already close to full employment, extra spending may raise prices more than output, and there may be time lags before lower rates affect spending. Overall, a fall in interest rates is likely to raise output and employment when there is spare capacity, but the size of the effect is uncertain.

What the mark scheme wants
  • Export-led growth as an outward-looking strategy that relies on openness and international trade. A labelled circular flow diagram showing all withdrawals and injections and the rise in exports (an injection) producing a bigger rise in income, output and employment through the multiplier, and a higher GDP per capita.
  • Evaluation: developing economies often export primary goods with income-inelastic demand and volatile supply (weather), so growth may be limited; higher income does not guarantee a better standard of living (distribution, leisure, long-term growth).

What the mark scheme wants

Top tips

  • Know the lists: injections are I, G and X; withdrawals are S, T and M. Equilibrium is J = W.
  • Say "net". A net injection means I > S, G > T or X > M overall, not that every injection is bigger than every withdrawal.
  • Label the diagram fully: every withdrawal and injection, the equilibrium, the full employment level and the direction of change.
  • Link spending to jobs: higher spending raises demand for output, firms produce more and employ more workers.
  • Mention the multiplier when income changes. The total change in income is bigger than the initial change in injections.
  • Evaluate with spare capacity. If the economy is near full employment, extra spending may raise prices rather than output.
  • Do not count transfer payments or spending by residents abroad as injections.
