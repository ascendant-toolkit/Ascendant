Causes of Inflation: Demand-Pull and Cost-Push

4.6.4: demand-pull and cost-push inflation shown with AD/AS diagrams, their causes, and the wage-price spiral. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Demand-pull inflation is caused by a rise in aggregate demand that is not matched by a rise in aggregate supply, so the AD curve shifts right and the price level rises; it is most likely when the economy is near full capacity. Cost-push inflation is caused by a rise in firms' costs (wages above productivity, raw materials, energy, import prices after a depreciation, indirect taxes), so the SRAS curve shifts left, raising the price level and reducing output. The two can feed each other in a wage-price spiral, where higher prices lead to wage demands, which raise costs and prices again. Identify the type by the curve that shifts: if output rises with prices, it is likely demand-pull; if output falls while prices rise, it is cost-push.

1 · Demand-pull inflation (4.6.4)

  • Cause: a rise in C, I, G or X, or a fall in M: lower interest rates, tax cuts, higher confidence, a boom in exports, rapid growth in the money supply, a depreciation that raises net exports.
  • Diagram: AD shifts right along SRAS; output and the price level both rise. The nearer to full capacity (or LRAS), the more the effect is on prices rather than output.
  • With spare capacity, a rise in AD is mainly growth; at full employment it is mainly inflation.

2 · Cost-push inflation

  • Cause: a rise in the costs of production: wage rises larger than productivity growth, higher raw material or energy prices, a depreciation that makes imported inputs dearer, higher indirect taxes, labour shortages, supply disruptions.
  • Diagram: SRAS shifts left; the price level rises and real output falls: stagflation (inflation with stagnant growth and rising unemployment).
  • Wage-price spiral: workers demand higher pay to match prices, raising costs, raising prices again.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

An oil price spike

A jump in world oil prices raises the cost of transport and production in an oil-importing country. SRAS shifts left, so prices rise while output and employment fall: cost-push inflation and stagflation.

Exam use: cost-push inflation from higher input prices.

A consumer boom

Low interest rates and rising house prices make households confident and they spend more. AD rises faster than capacity, so firms raise prices: demand-pull inflation.

Exam use: demand-pull inflation from rising consumption.

A weak currency

When the currency depreciates, imports cost more, raising the costs of firms that rely on imported inputs (cost-push), while exports become cheaper and demand rises (demand-pull). Both curves can shift.

Exam use: a depreciation causing both types of inflation.

Wage-price spiral

Prices rise 6%, so unions demand 6% pay rises. Firms pass the higher costs on in higher prices, workers demand more, and inflation becomes embedded.

Exam use: a cost-push spiral.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/21 · Q4(a) 8 marks With the help of an AD/AS diagram, explain cost-push inflation and demand-pull inflation in an economy and consider in what circumstances one may be more damaging than the other.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): an accurate diagram with correctly labelled axes of price level and real output (1), cost-push inflation shown by a leftward shift of AS (1) and demand-pull inflation shown by a rightward shift of AD (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): using the diagram to explain the possible damaging effects of cost-push inflation (up to 2) and of demand-pull inflation (up to 2), maximum 3.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a valid judgement on the comparison, including the factors that could make one cause more damaging than the other (1), and a conclusion (1).
PlanDiagram: AS left (cost-push), AD right (demand-pull). Effects: cost-push: price up, output down. Demand-pull: price up, output up until capacity. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

On an AD/AS diagram with the price level and real output on the axes, cost-push inflation is shown by a leftward shift of the SRAS curve, and demand-pull inflation by a rightward shift of the AD curve.

Cost-push inflation raises the price level while real output falls, which can mean falling employment as well as rising prices: stagflation. Demand-pull inflation raises the price level but also raises output and employment at first, though if the economy is at full capacity it only raises prices and may harm competitiveness and savers.

Cost-push inflation is probably more damaging when it comes with falling output and rising unemployment, since the economy gets worse in two ways. Demand-pull inflation can be more damaging if the economy is already at full capacity and the inflation becomes persistent, so it depends on the position of the economy.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/22 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain what is meant by inflation and, using diagrams, show how it can arise both as a result of a shift in the aggregate demand curve and a shift in the aggregate supply curve.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): inflation defined as a continuous or sustained rise (1) in the general or average level of prices (1).
  • Application (up to 3): a diagram showing inflation caused by a rightward shift of AD, with an explanation of why AD might increase (1), an accurately labelled diagram showing the rightward shift and rise in the general price level (1), and a clear explanation that inflation occurs due to excess aggregate demand (1).
  • Application (up to 3): a diagram showing inflation caused by a leftward shift of AS, with an explanation of why AS may fall, such as higher costs or taxes, and the resulting rise in the general price level. Micro (supply and demand) diagrams are not rewarded.
PlanInflation: sustained rise in general price level. AD diagram with a cause (e.g. lower interest rates). AS diagram with a cause (e.g. higher costs).
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general level of prices. On an AD/AS diagram, the price level is on the vertical axis and real output on the horizontal axis.

Lower interest rates make borrowing cheaper, so consumption and investment rise and AD shifts to the right. The price level rises along SRAS, and inflation results from this excess demand. If firms' costs rise, for example because oil prices increase, SRAS shifts left and the price level rises again, even though demand has not increased.

Based on Feb/March 2020 · 9708/22 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain, using aggregate demand and aggregate supply diagrams, the distinction between cost-push inflation and demand-pull inflation and explain one cause of each.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Cost-push inflation: an accurately labelled diagram showing a shift left of the aggregate supply curve (2) and up to 2 marks for an explanation of one cause, such as a decline in the exchange rate that raises the price of imported raw materials or a rise in wage rates above increases in productivity.
  • Demand-pull inflation: an accurately labelled diagram showing a shift right of the aggregate demand curve (2) and up to 2 marks for an explanation of one cause, such as increased exports or increased government spending.
PlanTwo diagrams. Cost-push: SRAS left, cause: depreciation or wages. Demand-pull: AD right, cause: exports or government spending.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Cost-push inflation is shown by a leftward shift of SRAS, which raises the price level and lowers real output. A depreciation of the currency is one cause: imported raw materials become dearer, raising firms' costs at every level of output.

Demand-pull inflation is shown by a rightward shift of AD, which raises the price level and output. Higher government spending is one cause: it adds to AD, and if the economy is near full capacity, firms respond mainly by raising prices.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/21 · Q1(d) 6 marks Analyse, with the help of AD/AS diagrams, how increasing wages can cause both demand-pull and cost-push inflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Demand-pull (up to 3): an analysis of demand-pull inflation from wage increases (1), such as greater consumer spending and confidence from higher incomes; an AD/AS diagram showing AD shifting right, labelled correctly (1) and the shift to the right with the rise in the price level (1).
  • Cost-push (up to 3): an analysis of cost-push inflation from wage increases not linked to higher productivity (1); an AD/AS diagram showing AS shifting left (up to 2): correct labelling (1) and the shift left showing the rise in the price level (1).
PlanHigher wages: more income and spending (AD right, demand-pull). Higher wages: higher costs (AS left, cost-push). Two diagrams.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Higher wages raise households' incomes, so consumption increases and the AD curve shifts to the right, which raises the price level: demand-pull inflation. Higher wages also raise firms' costs of production, especially if not matched by productivity, so the SRAS curve shifts to the left, which raises the price level again: cost-push inflation. Each is shown on its own AD/AS diagram with the price level and real output on the axes.

Top tips

  • Use AD/AS diagrams. Price level and real output, not a single market.
  • Demand-pull: AD right. Output and prices both rise.
  • Cost-push: SRAS left. Prices rise, output falls.
  • Give a cause and explain it. Name the cost or the spending change.
  • Mention capacity. Demand-pull is more inflationary near full capacity.
  • Mention the spiral. Wages and prices can chase each other.
  • Compare which is more damaging. Stagflation is often worse.
