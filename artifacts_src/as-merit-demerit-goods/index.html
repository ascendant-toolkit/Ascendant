Merit and Demerit Goods

1.6.3 to 1.6.4: merit goods, demerit goods, information failure, and policy responses (tax, subsidy, price controls, provision, regulation and information). Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

A merit good is a good that is under-consumed in a free market because consumers do not fully understand its benefits (information failure), so the benefit to the consumer is worth more than the consumer realises; examples are education, vaccinations and health check-ups. A demerit good is over-consumed because consumers underestimate the harm it does to themselves (and often others), such as tobacco, alcohol and sugary drinks. In both cases the market failure is that the free market output is not the socially best one, so resources are not allocated efficiently. Merit and demerit goods are still private goods (rival and excludable); the issue is how people value them, not whether they can be provided.

1 · Merit goods (1.6.3)

  • Consumers underestimate the benefit they get, so demand is too low (private benefit is understated) and the market under-provides and people under-consume them.
  • There are often positive externalities too (an educated workforce benefits everyone), so the social benefit is higher still.
  • They are private goods, not public goods: rival and excludable. They are often provided by the government (education, health care) but this is a policy choice, not part of the definition.
  • Cause: information failure (consumers do not know or fully understand the benefits), sometimes also a low income that stops them paying.

2 · Demerit goods (1.6.4)

  • Consumers underestimate the harm (or the cost of consumption), so demand is too high (private benefit is overstated) and the good is over-produced and over-consumed.
  • There may be negative externalities (passive smoking, drink-driving) which widen the gap between private and social cost.
  • Examples: cigarettes, alcohol, illegal drugs, sugary drinks, gambling. They are private goods: rival and excludable.
  • A product can be both a private good and a demerit good (a plastic bottle: rival and excludable, but over-consumed because of the harm to the environment that users do not weigh).

3 · What can be done?

PolicyMerit goodDemerit good
Indirect tax / subsidySubsidy lowers cost, raises output and consumptionIndirect tax raises cost, reduces output and consumption
Price controlsMaximum price below the equilibrium (to make it cheaper)Minimum price above the equilibrium (to make it dearer)
Direct provisionState schools, public hospitals, free vaccinationNot common
Regulation / banCompulsory schooling, compulsory vaccinationAge limits, advertising bans, banning a drug
Information / educationCampaigns about the benefitsHealth warnings, anti-smoking campaigns
  • Evaluate: tax and subsidy rely on knowing the size of the failure; demand for demerit goods is often inelastic (addiction), so a tax raises revenue but cuts consumption little; minimum prices can create black markets; bans are hard to enforce; information works slowly and may be ignored; direct provision costs taxpayers and has an opportunity cost.
  • The best choice is probably a combination of policies, depending on the good.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Vaccination

Many parents undervalue the full benefit of vaccination, both to the child and to others. Governments therefore provide it free, make it compulsory for school entry or run information campaigns. It is a private good that is under-consumed, so a merit good.

Exam use: information failure, positive externalities and direct provision.

Tobacco tax

Cigarettes carry heavy taxes and health warnings. Smokers underestimate the harm and demand is price inelastic, so the tax raises large revenue but reduces smoking by a smaller amount. Governments often add plain packaging and advertising bans.

Exam use: taxation of a demerit good, price inelasticity and the use of combined policies.

Sugar tax

A tax on sugar-sweetened drinks charged to producers raises their cost, so some cut the sugar content and prices rise. Consumption of the highest sugar drinks falls, which is one way a tax can improve the allocation of resources.

Exam use: an indirect tax shifting the supply curve to correct over-consumption.

Minimum alcohol prices

A minimum price per unit of alcohol aims to cut consumption of the cheapest, strongest drinks. If demand is inelastic, spending on alcohol rises and producers gain. It may also push some drinkers towards illegal or cross-border sales.

Exam use: minimum prices above the equilibrium and their unintended effects.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/21 · Q2(a) 8 marks Explain, using examples, why merit goods are classified as private goods rather than public goods.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): of private goods (1) and of public goods (1).
  • Application (up to 6): examples to explain why merit goods are private goods (up to 3) and why they are not public goods (up to 3).
PlanPrivate: rival and excludable. Public: non-rival and non-excludable. Apply both to education and health care.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A private good is rival, because when one person consumes it another cannot, and excludable, because those who do not pay can be kept out. A public good is non-rival and non-excludable.

Education and health care are merit goods. A hospital bed or a school place used by one person cannot be used by another, so they are rival, and a person who has not enrolled or registered can be excluded, so they are excludable. They are not public goods, such as street lighting or defence, which are available to everyone at once and cannot be withheld. Merit goods are often provided by the government, but this does not make them public.

Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/22 · Q2(a) 8 marks State what is meant by a demerit good and, with the help of a diagram, explain how a tax on producers can improve the allocation of resources in the market for sugar-sweetened drinks.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): a demerit good is one whose cost of consumption is underestimated, or is worse for the consumer than they realise, because of information failure (1), and is over-consumed (1).
  • Application (up to 3): a correctly labelled diagram showing a left shift of supply (1), a fall in quantity (1) and a rise in price (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): the tax increases the cost of production (1), which raises the price and reduces consumption (1), so the problem of over-consumption is reduced and the allocation of resources improves (1).
PlanDemerit: harm underestimated, over-consumed. Diagram: supply shifts left, price up, quantity down. Explain the improvement.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A demerit good, such as a sugary drink, is a good whose harm consumers underestimate because of information failure, so it is over-consumed in a free market.

A tax on producers raises their costs, so the supply curve shifts to the left. The equilibrium price rises and the quantity falls. Because consumption was too high, a smaller quantity is closer to the best level for society, so resources are used more efficiently and the market failure is reduced.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/23 · Q1(a) 4 marks Explain why a plastic bottle can be classified as both a private good and a demerit good.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Private good: excludability (1) and rivalry (1).
  • Demerit good: over-consumption (1) as a result of information failure (1).
PlanPrivate: rival and excludable. Demerit: over-consumed through information failure.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A plastic bottle is a private good because it is excludable, since a seller can refuse it to someone who will not pay, and rival, since one person using it stops another using it. It can be a demerit good because it is over-consumed: users do not fully understand the harm that plastic waste does to the environment, which is information failure, so too many are used.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Consider the problem of the provision of merit goods in a market economy and assess whether direct provision of such goods is the most effective way to deal with this problem.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the under-production and under-consumption of merit goods based on information failure (up to 4); a comparison of direct provision with at least one alternative, such as a subsidy or a public education campaign (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): evaluative comment and a reasoned conclusion on the most effective policy.
PlanProblem: information failure, under-consumption. Direct provision vs subsidy vs campaign. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Merit goods such as education are under-consumed in a free market because consumers do not fully understand the long-term benefit, so private demand is too low. Direct provision by the state, such as free schools, ensures everyone can use them, but costs taxpayers and has an opportunity cost. A subsidy lowers the price and encourages consumption, but depends on estimating the right level. An information campaign may raise understanding, but works slowly.

Direct provision is probably the most reliable way of reaching everyone, especially where income is low, but it is costly and may reduce quality. A mix of provision, subsidy and information is likely to be most effective, depending on the good and the size of the information failure.

Top tips

  • Say why: information failure. Merit goods: consumers do not see the full benefit. Demerit goods: they underestimate the harm.
  • Say which way the market fails. Merit: under-consumed. Demerit: over-consumed.
  • They are private goods. Rival and excludable, even when the state provides them.
  • Match the policy to the failure. Subsidy and maximum price for merit goods. Tax and minimum price for demerit goods.
  • Evaluate with elasticity. Inelastic demand means a tax cuts consumption only a little.
  • Mention unintended effects. Black markets, enforcement costs, opportunity cost of provision.
  • Use a diagram. A left shift in supply for a tax, a right shift for a subsidy.
