Monetary Policy: Tools and the Central Bank

5.3.1 to 5.3.2: the central bank, interest rates, the money supply, credit regulation and inflation targets, and how each changes borrowing and spending. Animated chains, labs, drills and past paper questions.

This part in five sentences

Monetary policy is the use of interest rates, the money supply and credit regulation to influence aggregate demand and meet macroeconomic objectives, and it is usually carried out by the central bank. The central bank sets the base (policy) interest rate, which feeds through to the rates that banks charge borrowers and pay savers, so changing it alters the cost of borrowing, the reward for saving, the exchange rate and expectations. It can also change the money supply (for example through quantitative easing) and the rules on bank lending (credit regulation) to make credit easier or harder to obtain. Many central banks have an inflation target and an independent role, which helps to make their policy credible. Each tool works through consumption, investment and net exports, and the effects depend on how people respond to interest rates and on the state of confidence.

1 · Monetary policy and the central bank

  • Who: the central bank (for example the Bank of England, the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank). It is often independent of the government, so decisions are not driven by election timing.
  • Objectives: usually price stability (an inflation target, often 2%), while supporting growth and employment, and keeping the financial system stable.
  • Other roles: bank to the government and to commercial banks, lender of last resort, and manager of the currency and foreign exchange reserves.
  • Compare with fiscal policy: fiscal policy uses government spending and taxation, decided by the government; monetary policy works through the price and quantity of money and credit, decided by the central bank. Changes in monetary policy are usually quicker to decide, but take time to work.

2 · Tool 1: Interest rates

  • The central bank sets the base rate, the rate at which it lends to commercial banks. Banks then adjust their own rates on mortgages, loans, credit cards and savings.
  • A cut in the rate: borrowing becomes cheaper, saving less rewarding, so households and firms spend and invest more. People with variable-rate mortgages have more disposable income. Asset prices (houses, shares) tend to rise, creating a wealth effect. The currency may weaken as hot money flows out, which raises net exports.
  • A rise in the rate: the opposite. Spending and investment fall, saving rises, and the currency may strengthen.
  • Real interest rate = nominal rate − inflation. It is the real cost of borrowing and the real reward for saving. A negative real rate means borrowers gain and savers lose in real terms.
  • Who gains and loses from higher rates: savers gain; borrowers (especially mortgage holders) and firms with debts lose.

ConsumptionCheaper credit, lower return on saving, more disposable income for borrowers, higher asset values

3 · Tool 2: The money supply

  • Money includes cash and bank deposits. Banks create money when they lend, so the amount of credit in the economy is closely linked to the money supply.
  • Open market operations: the central bank buys or sells government bonds. Buying bonds puts money into the banking system, raising the money supply; selling bonds removes money.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): large-scale purchases of bonds, often when interest rates are already close to zero, to raise the money supply, lower long-term interest rates and encourage lending and spending.
  • Reserve requirements: rules on how much banks must hold in reserve, which limit how much they can lend.
  • A larger money supply tends to lower interest rates and raise spending; a smaller one does the reverse. Too fast an expansion risks inflation.

4 · Tool 3: Credit regulation

  • Credit regulation is the rules governing how banks lend, which affect how easily households and firms can borrow.
  • Examples: capital requirements for banks, limits on loan-to-value (the deposit a buyer must put down), checks on borrowers' ability to pay, and limits on the growth of lending.
  • Loosening the rules (lower deposits, easier loans) is expansionary; tightening them is contractionary.
  • It can target a particular problem, such as a house-price bubble, without changing interest rates for everyone.

5 · Inflation targets and credibility

  • Many central banks aim for a published inflation target. If people believe the target, expectations of inflation stay low, which helps to avoid a wage-price spiral.
  • A target may be unhelpful if the bank is unwilling to take the steps needed, because raising rates to meet it risks a downturn.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A central bank sets the base rate

A central bank meets regularly to decide the base rate. If inflation is above its target, it may raise the rate; if the economy is weak, it may cut it. Commercial banks then change their mortgage and loan rates.

Exam use: interest rates as the main tool.

Mortgage holders and savers

When rates rise, people with variable-rate mortgages pay more each month, so they have less to spend, while savers earn more. The overall effect on spending depends on who has the larger response.

Exam use: the effects of interest rates on households.

Quantitative easing

With interest rates close to zero, a central bank creates money to buy government bonds, lowering longer-term interest rates and raising the money supply to encourage spending and lending.

Exam use: money supply as a tool of expansionary policy.

Easier credit rules

A central bank relaxes the deposit rules for mortgages. More households can borrow to buy homes, spending rises and house prices may rise. This is expansionary credit regulation.

Exam use: credit regulation.

Inflation targeting

A central bank announces a 2% inflation target. If people believe it, they expect low inflation, which helps to keep wage demands and price rises down.

Exam use: inflation targets and expectations.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/21 · Q1(b) 2 marks Explain what is meant by 'the real interest rate will be negative' in an economy where the nominal interest rate is 40% and the inflation rate is 50%.
What the mark scheme wants
  • A negative real interest rate is when the interest rate is lower than the inflation rate (1).
  • The real interest rate is calculated as the nominal rate minus inflation, so here it is 40 − 50 = −10% (1). The original question asked for the figure for Argentina in January 2022 or the method of calculation.
PlanReal rate = nominal − inflation. Negative when inflation exceeds the interest rate. Here −10%.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The real interest rate is the nominal interest rate minus inflation. It is negative when inflation is higher than the interest rate, so 40% − 50% gives −10%. This means borrowers gain in real terms because the value of their debt falls, and savers lose.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q1(e) 6 marks Assess whether a central bank setting an inflation target as part of its monetary policy is likely to be helpful for its economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 4): possibly helpful, because it helps to create and maintain stability and confidence at a time of significant price increases, informing consumers and firms that action will be taken to stop inflation becoming too high, and it can influence inflation expectations; if these stay stable, it may help to prevent a wage-price spiral (up to 3); possibly unhelpful, because the bank may not be willing to take the measures needed to keep inflation at the target, knowing there is a delicate balance between holding inflation close to a target and the risk of an economic downturn (up to 3).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): relevant evaluation, for example in relation to the particular target adopted, and a reserved mark for a conclusion on whether it is helpful.
PlanHelpful: confidence, expectations, no wage-price spiral. Unhelpful: bank may not act; target may risk a downturn. Judge: credibility decides.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

An inflation target could help by giving consumers and firms confidence that the central bank will act to stop prices rising too quickly, which keeps inflation expectations stable and may prevent a wage-price spiral. But the target may not help if the bank is reluctant to raise interest rates enough, because that could cause a slowdown and higher unemployment.

The target is likely to be helpful if the central bank is credible and independent, and if the target level is realistic. Its benefit depends on whether the bank is willing to meet it at the cost of lower growth.

Top tips

  • Name the three tools. Interest rates, money supply, credit regulation.
  • Explain the transmission. Rate change, borrowing and saving, C and I, AD.
  • Use real rates. Nominal minus inflation.
  • Say expansionary or contractionary. Cut or loosen versus raise or tighten.
  • Who gains and loses. Savers versus borrowers.
  • Mention the exchange rate. Interest rates change hot money and net exports.
  • Know the central bank's role. Independent, inflation target, credibility.
