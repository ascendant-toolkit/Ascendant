AO3 Evaluation: Long-Answer Technique

How the examiner moves a Discuss answer from Level 1 to Level 3: two developed sides and a conclusion that gives a reason.

The big idea

AO3 is the skill of weighing an argument, not just making it. For an 8-mark Discuss question that means three things: two sides, each developed with a chain of reasoning, and a conclusion that gives a reason. That is enough for the top level. The rest of this page is help with doing those three things well.

Where AO3 is worth marks

The numbers

AO3 is 10% of the whole IGCSE and 15% of Paper 2, which is roughly 12 of the 80 marks. Paper 1 (multiple choice) has no AO3 at all.

AO1 Knowledge and understanding is 43% and AO2 Analysis is 47%, so analysis does most of the work, but evaluation is what lifts a good answer to a top grade.

The questions

Part (d) of each Section B question is an 8-mark Discuss, marked by levels. Three of them are answered, so about 24 marks are marked this way. Section A also ends with longer Discuss parts.

In Section A the answer must use the information in the extract. In Section B you may use the stimulus paragraph and your own examples.

The levels table, decoded

The same table is used for every (d) question on every topic.

Where the marks moveFrom Level 1 to 2 you add a developed chain (A leads to B leads to C). From Level 2 to 3 you develop both sides and end with a conclusion that gives a reason. Length does not do either.

The answer in three parts

PartWhat it doesExample for "a minimum wage will reduce poverty"
Side 1: why it mightA developed reason, with a chain."A minimum wage raises the pay of the lowest paid workers, so their incomes may rise above the poverty line."
Side 2: why it might notA different developed reason."Firms' costs rise, so some may cut jobs, and workers who lose their jobs lose all their income."
ConclusionWhich side is stronger, and why."Overall a modest minimum wage is more likely to reduce poverty, because few jobs may be lost, than a high one in a weak economy."

Read the stem: five question types

Almost every 8-mark question fits one of these. The stem tells you what kind of judgement the examiner wants.

StemWhat it really asksWhat your conclusion can weigh
Will / would… (about half)A prediction.Say what is likely, then when it could differ: circumstances, size, time.
Benefit / harm / advantage…A cost-benefit judgement.Who gains and who loses; short run against long run.
Should…A decision.Opportunity cost, alternatives, and who is affected, then choose.
Can / could…An effectiveness test.How far it works: size, how responsive people are, other factors.
Always / all / only / mostA claim to test.Find the exceptions. One counter-example breaks "always".

Optional: eight ways to develop a point

If you are stuck for a second side, or for a reason in your conclusion, ask one of these questions. You do not need to use them all, or any at all if your reasoning is already developed.

1 · Size

How big is the change? A small tax, wage or interest rate move may do little; a large one may cause side effects.

2 · Time

Short run or long run? Some effects appear quickly and fade; others take years.

3 · Responsiveness

How much do buyers, sellers or workers react? This is elasticity and the multiplier in everyday words.

4 · Winners and losers

Who gains and who loses: consumers, producers, workers, taxpayers, the government.

5 · Opportunity cost

What is given up? The same money or resources could have been used elsewhere.

6 · Circumstances

Does the state of the economy, the market or confidence change the result? For example spare capacity, a recession, an addictive good.

7 · Alternatives

Is there another policy or choice that might work better or cost less?

8 · Conflict of aims

Does helping one objective hurt another: growth against inflation, jobs against the deficit, prices against profits?

Model 1 Discuss 8 marks Will stem: Discuss whether or not a cut in the rate of interest will increase investment.

Investment is spending by firms on capital goods, and the rate of interest is the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving.

A cut in the rate of interest could increase investment because borrowing becomes cheaper. Firms that borrow to buy machinery pay less interest, so more projects could now earn a profit after the cost of the loan, and firms may also find it less attractive to save their own funds. Lower rates could also raise consumer spending, giving firms more reason to expect higher sales and so more reason to expand.

A cut in the rate of interest may not increase investment if firms lack confidence. If firms expect demand to fall, for example in a recession, they might not borrow at any rate because extra capacity would stay unused. Banks may also be unwilling to lend even when the central bank cuts its rate, so the cut may not reach firms.

Overall, a cut in the rate of interest is more likely to increase investment when firms are confident about future sales and banks are willing to lend, and when the cut is large enough to change the cost of borrowing noticeably. In a weak economy with low confidence, it could have little effect.

Model 2 Discuss 8 marks Should stem: Discuss whether or not a government should impose a maximum price on food.

A maximum price is a legal limit on how high the price can be. To have any effect it must be set below the equilibrium price.

A government could impose a maximum price on food because it keeps food affordable for low-income households. Food such as rice or bread is a large share of poor families' spending, so a lower price could help them to eat enough and improve their health and ability to work. It could also protect consumers from sharp price rises after a poor harvest.

However, a maximum price below equilibrium could reduce the quantity supplied and increase the quantity demanded, creating a shortage. Farmers may produce less because their revenue is lower, and some food may be sold on a black market at higher prices, so the poor who were meant to benefit could still go without. The government may need to ration food or pay subsidies to farmers, which has an opportunity cost.

Overall, a maximum price is more defensible as a temporary measure just below the market price, and combined with support for farmers. For a long period, targeted measures such as food vouchers or subsidies for the poor could help the poorest without creating a shortage, so the government might choose those instead.

Model 3 Discuss 8 marks Always stem: Discuss whether or not MNCs always benefit their host countries.

A multinational company (MNC) is a firm that operates in more than one country.

MNCs often benefit host countries because they create jobs and raise incomes. A foreign electronics firm building a factory in a developing country could employ thousands of workers, whose spending could create further demand for local shops and suppliers, and the firm's tax payments could fund public services. MNCs may also bring new technology and skills, which could raise productivity and exports in the long run.

MNCs do not always benefit host countries. Profits may be sent back to the home country instead of being reinvested, and wages may be low and working conditions poor if rules are weak. Large MNCs could drive out local firms that cannot compete, reducing the number of locally owned firms, and the production may create pollution. If the MNC moves to another country when costs rise, the jobs could disappear.

Overall, MNCs are not guaranteed to benefit the host country. The gains in jobs, technology and tax revenue seem more likely where the government negotiates well, regulates pollution and working conditions, and where local suppliers can grow alongside the MNC. Where these are weak, the costs may outweigh the benefits.

Another way to build the paragraph

Point, Explain, Link

Every paragraph can follow three short steps. Using the same pattern each time keeps the answer clear when you are under pressure.

StepWhat to writeExample: a higher minimum wage
PointUse the wording of the question."A higher minimum wage could reduce poverty…"
ExplainThe chain, with economic terms."…because low-paid workers earn more, and their higher incomes may lift them above the poverty line."
LinkReturn to the question."So a minimum wage can help to reduce poverty for those who keep their jobs."

Described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Sugary drinks levies

The UK introduced a levy on sugary drinks in 2018. Instead of just raising prices, many manufacturers reduced the sugar in their drinks to avoid the levy.

Exam use: evaluating an indirect tax. Angle: responsiveness. The tax changed what producers made, not only what consumers bought, so the effect on consumption may differ from a simple demand-curve prediction.

Rent controls

Several cities have capped rents. Tenants who find a flat may gain, but landlords may supply fewer homes or let quality fall, and some rental homes may be sold instead.

Exam use: maximum price. Angle: winners and losers, time. Use it to show that a policy meant to help consumers may reduce supply over time.

Interest rate rises after 2021

Many central banks raised interest rates sharply to bring inflation down. Homeowners with variable mortgages faced higher payments, while savers earned more.

Exam use: monetary policy. Angle: winners and losers, conflict of aims. Use it to show that a policy aimed at prices may also slow spending and growth.

Samsung in Vietnam

Samsung assembles a large share of its phones in Vietnam, and its factories provide many jobs and a large share of the country's exports.

Exam use: MNCs. Angle: circumstances, time. Use it as a benefit (jobs, exports), then evaluate: dependence on one firm, profits sent abroad, and the risk that the firm moves if costs rise.

US tariffs on steel

In 2018 the US put tariffs on imported steel. Domestic steel producers benefited, but industries that use steel faced higher costs, and other countries introduced retaliatory tariffs.

Exam use: protection. Angle: winners and losers, alternatives. Use it to show that protecting jobs in one industry may cost jobs and raise prices elsewhere.

The UK minimum wage

The UK introduced a national minimum wage in 1999. Many studies found that it raised pay for low-paid workers with little loss of jobs, although critics argued that very high rates could cost jobs.

Exam use: labour market policy. Angle: size, circumstances. Use it to show that the effect on jobs may depend on how high the minimum wage is set.

Top tips

  • Read the stem first. Will, should, can, benefit or always: it tells you what your conclusion needs to decide.
  • Two sides, every time. "Whether or not" means a developed argument for and a different one against.
  • Develop each point. Use "because", "so" and "which could" to link every step.
  • Finish with a conclusion that gives a reason. "Overall it is more likely to… because…" beats "In conclusion there are two sides".
  • Hedge. Could, may, is likely to, tends to. Economic outcomes are uncertain, and the descriptor rewards saying so.
  • Do not reverse yourself. If side 2 is just the opposite of side 1, it earns little. Find a new argument.
  • Respect scope words. "Always", "all" and "only" are tested by finding exceptions.
  • Match length to marks. About 1.5 minutes a mark: roughly 12 minutes for 8 marks and 9 for 6 marks.
  • In Section A use the extract. The data and text are part of the answer.

Common mistakes

No conclusionTwo paragraphs and then stopping. Always finish with a sentence that picks a side and says why.
A list of pointsFive undeveloped points score less than two developed ones. Depth beats number.
Opinion without a reason"I think it is good" gives the examiner nothing to credit. Say why, and under what condition.
Long definitionsOne sentence is plenty. Time spent defining the policy is time not spent evaluating.
