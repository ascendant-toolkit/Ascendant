Causes and Consequences of Economic Growth

How growth can come from higher demand, more resources or better resources, and its advantages and disadvantages. Syllabus 4.5.3.

The big idea

Economic growth can be caused by an increase in total demand, an increase in the quantity of resources or an increase in the quality of resources. Growth has advantages (higher incomes, jobs, tax revenue) and disadvantages (pollution, inequality, inflation, a worse trade balance). Good answers separate the causes, then weigh the consequences for different groups.

Causes of growth

Demand-led growth

Works fastest, and when there is spare capacity output rises with little inflation. Near full capacity it mostly raises prices.

Supply-led growth

Slower, but raises the economy's capacity, so growth can last without inflation.

Advantages of growth

  • Higher incomes and living standards: more goods and services per person.
  • More jobs: firms need more workers, so unemployment may fall.
  • More tax revenue: the government can fund healthcare, education and infrastructure, or reduce a deficit.
  • Higher profits: firms may invest more.
  • Less poverty: more output makes it easier to help the poor.

Disadvantages of growth

  • Pollution and resource use: more output may mean more emissions, waste and depleted resources (environmental sustainability).
  • Inequality: the gains may go mainly to richer households and regions.
  • Inflation: fast demand growth near full capacity raises prices.
  • Balance of payments: higher incomes raise spending on imports, which may worsen the current account.
  • Opportunity cost: resources used to build capital now mean less consumption today; work and stress may rise.

Examples are described in general terms so they stay accurate. Check current details before quoting figures.

Growth fuelled by investment

Fast-growing economies such as South Korea and China invested a large share of output in capital goods and education, which raised capacity for decades.

Exam use: Growth from more and better resources, with a long time lag.

Growth and pollution

A past paper describes India's rise in output bringing more air pollution, while the government encourages growth through investment and trade.

Exam use: A disadvantage of growth: environmental cost.

Growth that bypasses some people

In several fast-growing economies the gains have been concentrated in cities and richer households, widening the gap with rural areas.

Exam use: Inequality as a cost of growth.

Oil discoveries

Discovery of oil or gas can raise an economy's output and exports, but may leave it dependent on one resource.

Exam use: More resources raising growth, with a risk if prices fall.

Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain two benefits of a higher economic growth rate. (Feb/Mar 2020, P22)
ReminderTwo separate benefits, each developed: incomes and living standards, jobs, tax revenue, profits.
Higher growth means higher output and incomes, so people can afford more goods and services and living standards could rise. It could also increase employment. Firms need more workers to produce more output, so unemployment is likely to fall and more households have an income.
Q2 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how higher government spending could increase economic growth. (Feb/Mar 2021, P22)
ReminderGovernment spending is part of AD. Chain: G up, AD right, multiplier, output rises. Longer run: capital and skills.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Government spending is a component of aggregate demand. If the government spends more on infrastructure or schools, firms and workers receive extra income and spend part of it, so AD could shift to the right with a multiplier effect. Real GDP rises, which is economic growth, and firms may hire more workers. Spending on infrastructure or education could also raise the quality and quantity of resources, raising productive capacity in the long run. However, if the economy is close to capacity, mostly prices might rise.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how an ageing population may affect a country's economic growth rate. (Feb/Mar 2025, P22)
ReminderPopulation ages: fewer workers, more retired. Two sides if possible: lower labour supply; more spending on health and pensions.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
An ageing population means a rising share of people are retired. The number of workers relative to the total population may fall, so the quantity of labour could fall and productive capacity could grow more slowly. Higher government spending on pensions and healthcare may need more tax or borrowing, which could reduce spending on other things that raise growth. Older workers may have experience, and demand for healthcare and leisure may rise, which could create growth in some industries. Overall the effect on growth is likely to be negative unless the country raises productivity or retirement ages.
Q4 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how economic growth conflicts with balance of payments stability. (May/June 2021, P23)
ReminderGrowth raises incomes, which raises imports; a current account deficit may result. Mention exports if you can.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Economic growth is a rise in real GDP, while balance of payments stability means the current account is not in persistent deficit. As incomes rise, households and firms spend more, including on imports. If imports rise faster than exports, the current account deficit could widen. Firms may also import more machinery and raw materials to expand output. The conflict may be smaller if growth comes from higher exports.
Q5 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not governments should aim for a high rate of economic growth. (May/June 2022, P21)
ReminderAdvantages: incomes, jobs, tax revenue. Disadvantages: pollution, inequality, inflation, imports. End with 'it depends on…' what kind of growth and how the gains are used.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Economic growth is a rise in real GDP over time. A high growth rate could raise incomes and living standards, because there is more to spend on goods and services. It could also reduce unemployment as firms hire more workers, and raise tax revenue so that the government can pay for health, education and infrastructure. These gains are larger when growth is shared across the population. On the other hand, high growth could cause more pollution and use up resources, which harms future generations, and it may widen the gap between rich and poor. If growth is driven by demand near full capacity it could cause inflation, and higher incomes could raise imports and worsen the current account. Overall, governments are likely to benefit from aiming for growth, but they should consider the kind of growth: sustainable growth from better resources with policies to limit pollution and share the gains is more likely to benefit the economy than fast growth with high costs.

Top tips

  • Three causes: demand, quantity of resources, quality of resources.
  • Link each cause to a diagram. AD shifts right, or AS shifts right.
  • Give both sides on 'should aim for growth'. Incomes, jobs, tax revenue against pollution, inequality, inflation, imports.
  • Say who gains. Consumers, workers, firms, government: and who may lose.
  • Evaluate with 'it depends'. On the cause of growth, the state of the economy, and how gains are shared.
