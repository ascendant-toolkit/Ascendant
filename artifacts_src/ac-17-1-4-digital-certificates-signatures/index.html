Digital Certificates and Signatures

Coursebook: Chapter 17 · Security, section 17.1 Encryption, encryption protocols and digital certificates

What a digital certificate is and how it is acquired, how a digital signature is produced and checked with a message digest, and why a certificate is needed; animations, a sign-and-verify lab and the real Paper 3 questions from 2022 to 2025.

This page in five sentences

A digital certificate is an electronic document, issued by a trusted Certificate Authority (CA), that proves the identity of a website, individual or organisation and contains their public key. To get one, you ask a CA, send your details and public key, the CA verifies your identity and then issues a certificate containing your public key. A digital signature shows that a message really came from its sender and has not been changed: the sender hashes the message to make a message digest and encrypts the digest with their private key. The receiver uses the sender's public key, which the digital certificate supplies and vouches for, to decrypt the signature, hashes the message they received, and compares the two digests: if they match, the message is genuine and unaltered.

1 · Digital certificates

A digital certificate is an electronic (online) document used to authenticate or prove the identity of a website, individual or organisation. It is typically issued by a CA. Items commonly found inside it:

  • the name of the certificate holder (subject);
  • the holder's public key;
  • a serial number and a version number;
  • the start and expiry dates (validity);
  • the certificate issuer (the CA) and the CA's digital signature.

2 · How a certificate is acquired

  1. The organisation generates a public and private key pair (for example with a key-generation tool, on its own computer).
  2. It requests a digital certificate from a Certificate Authority.
  3. It gathers the information the CA needs, including information to prove its identity and its public key, and sends it to the CA (protected: signed with its own private key and encrypted with the CA's public key).
  4. The CA verifies the organisation's identity.
  5. If the checks are passed, the CA generates and issues the certificate, which includes the organisation's public key (signed with the CA's private key, so others can trust it).

3 · Digital signatures

A digital signature validates the authenticity of an electronic message and shows it has not been altered. It uses a hashing algorithm, which turns a message of any length into a short fixed-size message digest; the same message always gives the same digest, and changing even one character gives a different one.

Producing the signature (sender)

  1. The message is hashed with the agreed hashing algorithm...
  2. ...to produce a message digest.
  3. The digest is encrypted with the sender's private key. This is the digital signature. The message and signature are then sent (and may themselves be encrypted with the receiver's public key to keep the message private).

Checking the signature (receiver)

  1. If the message and signature were encrypted for the receiver, they are decrypted with the receiver's private key.
  2. The digital signature is decrypted with the sender's public key to recover the message digest that was sent.
  3. The message received is hashed with the same hashing algorithm to make a digest of the message as received.
  4. The two digests are compared. If they are the same, the message has not been altered (and came from the sender). If they differ, the message has been altered.

4 · Why a certificate is needed for a digital signature

  • The certificate provides the sender's public key, which is needed to check the signature.
  • It validates that the public key belongs to the person or organisation it claims to, and so validates the private key used to create the signature.
  • It makes a digital signature virtually impossible to spoof, and gives evidence of who signed and that the document was not altered.
  • It gives non-repudiation: the sender cannot deny sending it.

5 · Common ways to lose marks

  • Saying the signature is a scanned picture of a handwritten signature. It is an encrypted message digest.
  • Encrypting the whole message with the private key instead of the digest, or hashing with the public key.
  • Leaving out the comparison of the two digests, or saying what it shows.
  • Writing that the receiver uses their own private key to check the signature. The signature is checked with the sender's public key.
  • Describing a certificate as a key. It is a document that contains a public key and proves identity.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

The padlock on a website

Click it and you can see the site's certificate: who it was issued to, who issued it (the CA) and when it expires. Your browser checks it before trusting the site.

Exam use: a digital certificate proves the identity of a website.

Signed software

Apps and updates carry a digital signature. Your device checks it with the publisher's public key and refuses software that has been tampered with.

Exam use: the digest of the received file must match.

Signing a PDF contract

A digital signature on a contract shows who signed it and that nothing has been changed since. If one word is edited, verification fails.

Exam use: authenticity and integrity.

An ID card

A certificate is like a passport: a trusted authority checks who you are and issues a document that others accept without having to check you themselves.

Exam use: the CA verifies identity and issues the certificate.

A wax seal on a letter

The seal shows the sender (only they have the stamp) and that the letter has not been opened. A digital signature does the same for a message.

Exam use: non-repudiation and integrity.

Top tips

  • Certificate = document with a public key. Issued by a CA after checking the identity.
  • Sign the digest. Hash the message, encrypt the digest with the sender's private key.
  • Check with the public key. Decrypt the signature, hash the message, compare.
  • Two digests compared. Same: unaltered. Different: altered.
  • Why a certificate? It provides and validates the public key; non-repudiation.
  • Order matters. Hash, digest, encrypt; then decrypt, hash, compare.
