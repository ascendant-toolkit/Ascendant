Differing Objectives and Policies of Firms

Profit is not the only thing firms chase. Revenue, sales, growth and a quiet life all show up in real businesses, and so do the pricing tactics that go with them: price discrimination, limit pricing and predatory pricing. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions, with mark scheme points.

The chapter in four sentences

Textbook firms maximise profit where MR = MC, but real firms often pursue other aims: revenue, sales volume, growth, satisficing or social goals. One big reason is the principal-agent problem: managers (agents) run the firm but shareholders (principals) own it, and their interests may differ. A firm's pricing policy follows from its objective and its market power: price discrimination to capture more surplus, limit pricing to deter entry, predatory pricing to drive rivals out. Each policy affects consumers differently in the short run and the long run.

3 · Why objectives differ: the principal-agent problem

Divorce of ownership and control

In a large company the shareholders (owners) are not the managers (who make the decisions). This is the divorce of ownership and control. It does not have to cause trouble, but it can.

The problem

The principal (shareholders) wants high profit. The agent (manager) may want pay, status, a large team or a quiet life. Managers know more about the firm than shareholders do (asymmetric information), so shareholders cannot easily check.

What agents may do

  • Chase revenue or growth because pay and status depend on size
  • Satisfice: make "enough" profit and relax
  • Spend on perks, offices and prestige projects
  • Take decisions with a short-term view to hit bonus targets

How firms reduce the problem

  • Pay linked to profit or share price (bonuses, share options)
  • Managers owning shares
  • Audits, reports and independent directors (monitoring)
  • Contracts with clear targets, and threat of takeover or dismissal
CarefulThe problem does not definitely follow from the divorce of ownership and control (a 2023 multiple choice question asked this). It is a risk whenever one person acts for another and the interests differ.

4 · Pricing policies

Price discrimination

Selling the same product at different prices to different groups, for reasons not related to cost.

Needed: (1) the firm can set its own price, (2) it can separate the markets, (3) different price elasticities of demand, (4) no resale between markets

How it works (third degree)

Charge a higher price where demand is inelastic (business travellers, adults) and a lower price where demand is elastic (students, early bookers). Set MR = MC in each market. Profit may be higher than with one price.

Forms

  • First degree: each buyer pays the most they will pay (rare)
  • Second degree: price depends on quantity (bulk, tariffs)
  • Third degree: different prices for different groups or times

Possible benefits

  • Higher profit, which may fund R&D
  • Some consumers (low price market) can now afford the product
  • Output may rise, and a loss-making service may survive
  • Can help exporters (low prices abroad)

Possible costs

  • Consumer surplus is transferred to the producer
  • Higher price for those with inelastic demand
  • Seen as unfair, may need monitoring and separation costs
  • Possible misallocation of resources

Limit pricing

An incumbent sets a price below the profit-maximising price, close to average cost, so that a new entrant sees only normal profit and stays out. It works because the incumbent has lower costs (economies of scale). In the short run consumers may gain from the lower price. In the long run competition is reduced and the firm may return to higher prices, or may invest and keep prices low (dynamic efficiency).

Predatory pricing

Setting a price below average cost (even making a loss) to force an existing rival out of the market, then raising the price. It is anti-competitive and may be illegal. A firm needs deep pockets to do it.

Limit versus predatoryLimit pricing aims to stop new entrants and keeps the price near normal profit. Predatory pricing aims to push out firms already in the market and uses a price below cost.

Other policies

  • Cost-plus (mark-up) pricing: add a fixed percentage to unit cost. Simple, but ignores demand.
  • Price leadership and collusion in oligopoly (see syllabus 7.6).
  • Non-price competition: advertising, quality, branding, customer service.

5 · Which objective is "best"?

It depends on the market. In perfect competition firms have to chase profit or leave. In monopoly or oligopoly there is more room to pursue other goals. A contestable market (low barriers to entry) pushes even a monopolist towards low costs and prices. Information also matters: firms rarely know their exact MR and MC.

7 · Multiple choice

Examples are described in general terms and are for illustration. Check current facts before quoting figures in an exam answer.

Cinemas, trains and airlines

Students, children and pensioners often pay less than adults for the same seat. Airlines charge more for last-minute bookings and less for early ones. Business travellers tend to have more inelastic demand and often cannot choose dates, so the firm may set a higher price for them.

Exam use: a ready example of third degree price discrimination. State the conditions (set price, separate markets, different PED, no resale) and link to MR = MC in each market.

Online retailers that put growth first

Some online firms have, for years, put their money into expansion and low prices rather than profit. They may be pursuing market share or growth. Shareholders accepted this because they expected larger profits later.

Exam use: an example of a firm that does not maximise short-run profit. Evaluate: it could be long-run profit maximisation in disguise.

A bank with aggressive sales targets

In 2016 a large US bank was found to have opened accounts for customers who had not asked for them, under pressure from sales targets. Staff pay and promotion depended on sales numbers, not on customer benefit or long-term profit.

Exam use: a principal-agent example. Agents responded to the incentives they were given. Shareholders (principals) bore the cost in fines and reputation.

Employee-owned and trust-owned firms

The John Lewis Partnership is owned in trust for its employees, who share the profit as a bonus. Some founders have moved ownership to a trust so that profits fund a social or environmental purpose. This reduces the divorce of ownership and control, and the aim is no longer only shareholder profit.

Exam use: a non-profit-maximising objective, and a way to reduce the principal-agent problem.

Predatory pricing claims

Claims of predatory pricing are common in airlines, buses and online retail. They are hard to prove: competition authorities usually need to see prices below cost and an intention to push a rival out, with a chance to raise prices afterwards.

Exam use: shows why the policy is anti-competitive, and why a regulator may find it hard to act.

A dominant firm keeping prices low

A firm with lower costs than any newcomer can set a price that gives rivals little profit. Customers may enjoy lower prices for a time. If rivals do stay out and the firm later raises its price, consumers could lose.

Exam use: limit pricing: short-run gain, possible long-run loss. Add dynamic efficiency as an evaluation point.

Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/41 · Q3 20 marks Evaluate the consequences for the price and output of a firm if it changes its objective from profit maximisation to sales maximisation as a response to the principal-agent problem.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge and analysis (14): define profit and sales maximisation and the principal-agent problem. Profit maximisation at MR = MC, with its price and output. Sales maximisation at AR = AC, with its price and output. Compare the two. Diagrams are expected.
  • Evaluation (6): profit maximisation gives a higher price and lower output than sales maximisation; welfare benefit to consumers as producer surplus is transferred to them; possibly greater equality; but resources may be misdirected to sales maximising firms, which could reduce allocative efficiency.
Diagram to drawOne diagram: AR, MR, MC, AC. Mark Q1 where MR = MC with price P1, and Q2 where AR = AC with price P2. Show Q2 > Q1 and P2 < P1. Shade the supernormal profit lost.
KANE

The principal-agent problem arises when the managers of a firm (agents) act in their own interest rather than in the interest of the shareholders (principals), who own the firm but cannot easily monitor what the managers do. Profit maximisation occurs where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, and sales maximisation occurs where average revenue equals average cost, so that the firm sells as many units as possible while still earning normal profit.

If a firm changes its objective from profit maximisation to sales maximisation as a response to the principal-agent problem, its price is likely to fall and its output to rise. Managers may prefer a larger firm because their pay, status and the size of their team could depend on sales rather than profit. On a diagram the profit-maximising firm produces Q1 where MR = MC and charges P1 on the demand curve. Moving to AR = AC increases output to Q2 and lowers the price to P2, because the firm accepts only normal profit and the supernormal profit disappears. This holds for a firm with market power and downward sloping demand. In perfect competition the firm is a price taker, so a change of objective might not alter price at all.

The change is likely to benefit consumers because they pay a lower price and can buy more. Producer surplus is transferred to consumers, so consumer surplus rises and the distribution of income may become more equal. However, the gain depends on the size of the firm's costs. If the sales-maximising firm becomes less efficient (X-inefficiency) because shareholders are no longer pressing for cost control, the benefits could be smaller.

There could also be costs. Beyond the profit-maximising output the marginal cost of each extra unit is greater than the marginal revenue it earns, so resources may be misdirected into the sales-maximising firm, which could reduce allocative efficiency. Lower profit may also mean less money for research and development. Overall, the change is likely to bring a lower price and a higher output, which could benefit consumers, but the net effect on society depends on the firm's costs, the market structure and whether shareholders respond, for example by changing the managers' pay.

Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/42 · Q4(b) 13 marks Discuss whether consumers or producers benefit more from the practice of price discrimination.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Definition and explanation of price discrimination, and when it can occur.
  • The impact on both consumers and producers, then a judgement on who benefits more. Conclusion needed for 12 or 13.
LevelWhat earns it
L4 (9–13)Full explanation of when it can occur, discussion of the impact on both sides, conclusion.
L3 (7–8)Limited explanation of when it can occur, limited analysis of the impact.
L2 (5–6)Explains price discrimination only.
L1 (1–4)Basic facts, irrelevance, errors.
KANE

Price discrimination is the practice of charging different prices to different groups of consumers for the same product, where the difference is not due to differences in cost. It requires the firm to have some market power so it can set its own price, to be able to separate the markets so that the product cannot be resold, and for the price elasticity of demand to differ between the markets.

Producers are likely to benefit more from price discrimination because it allows them to capture consumer surplus. An airline could charge business travellers, whose demand is inelastic, a high price and students, whose demand is elastic, a low price. Setting MR = MC in each market is likely to give a higher total profit than a single price. The extra profit may be used for research and development or to keep a service running that would otherwise close. However, the gain depends on the cost of separating the markets and on the risk of resale, which could remove the benefit.

Some consumers may also benefit. Students in the elastic market pay a lower price than under a single price and may be able to buy a product they could not afford before, so total output could rise. Consumers in the inelastic market pay more and lose consumer surplus, so the effect on consumers as a whole is mixed and could be negative if the firm has strong market power.

Overall, producers are likely to benefit more because they gain revenue from both markets and capture more of the consumer surplus, while consumers are split between winners and losers. The balance could change if regulation limits the practice or if competition forces the firm to pass on some of its gains.

C · Limit pricing and predatory pricing

Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/43 · Q3(b) 13 marks Limit pricing may sometimes be used by a monopoly. It creates a barrier to entry which benefits consumers in the short run but increases costs to the consumer in the long run. Discuss this statement.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Monopoly, its profit-maximising output (MR = MC), and barriers to entry.
  • How limit pricing works: a price lower than the profit-maximising price but still covering costs, below what a new entrant could profit at. Low costs come from dynamic efficiency and economies of scale. Use a diagram.
  • Short run: consumers benefit from the lower price. Long run: with the threat of entry removed, the monopoly could return to higher prices.
  • The other side: the monopoly could use its profits for R&D, which could keep costs and prices low. Conclusion required.
KANE

Limit pricing is when an existing firm sets a price below its profit-maximising price, close to its average cost, so that new firms see only normal profit and are discouraged from entering the market. It could work as a barrier to entry because a monopoly with economies of scale has lower average costs than a new, smaller firm, so a price set above the monopoly's costs but below the entrant's costs makes entry unprofitable.

Limit pricing could benefit consumers in the short run. The monopoly moves away from MR = MC and charges a lower price, so consumers pay less and consumer surplus is higher than under the profit-maximising price. This depends on how far below the monopoly price the limit price is set, because a limit price only slightly below the monopoly price might give little benefit.

In the long run limit pricing could increase costs to the consumer. If the threat of entry is removed, the monopoly keeps its market share and could revert to its profit-maximising price, so consumers might pay more for a longer period than if a new rival had entered and increased competition. Alternatively, the monopoly could use its extra profit for research and development and gain dynamic efficiency, which could lower costs and keep prices low.

Overall, consumers are likely to gain in the short run, but whether they lose in the long run depends on whether the monopoly passes on its cost advantage and on how contestable the market is. If regulators or potential entrants are able to keep up pressure, the long-run cost to consumers could be small.

Top tips

  • Know the three rules: profit MR = MC, revenue MR = 0, sales AR = AC. Say the rule, then read off price and output.
  • Order the outcomes: price is highest at profit maximisation and lowest at sales maximisation, output is the other way round.
  • Distinguish revenue from volume. Revenue is price times quantity. Volume is the number of units.
  • Define the principal-agent problem in one sentence: the agent (manager) acts for the principal (shareholder) but may pursue their own interests, helped by asymmetric information.
  • For price discrimination, state all the conditions. Set price, separate markets, different PED, no resale. Then say MR = MC in each market.
  • Draw two side-by-side diagrams for price discrimination, the same MC in both, a higher price in the inelastic market.
  • Limit pricing keeps out new firms. Predatory pricing pushes out existing ones. Predatory prices are below cost.
  • Evaluate with time and market structure: short run versus long run, contestable markets, regulation, dynamic efficiency, and "it depends on the cost saving".
