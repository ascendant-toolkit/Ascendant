Inflation, Deflation and the Consumer Price Index

4.6.1 to 4.6.3: inflation, deflation and disinflation, measuring price changes with the CPI and its difficulties, and nominal and real values. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, deflation is a sustained fall in the general price level (a negative inflation rate), and disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation, where prices still rise but more slowly. Inflation is measured by the consumer price index (CPI): a typical basket of goods and services is priced each period, each item is weighted by the share of household spending on it, and the weighted average change in prices is the inflation rate. The CPI has difficulties: the basket and weights become out of date, it cannot capture quality changes, new products or substitution, and households differ. Nominal (money) values are measured at current prices, while real values are adjusted for inflation, so rising prices reduce the real value and purchasing power of money.

  • Price stability means avoiding prolonged inflation and deflation, usually a low and stable inflation rate such as 2%.
  • With inflation the value of money falls (the same money buys less); with deflation it rises, but falling prices can cause consumers to delay spending.

2 · Measuring inflation with the CPI (4.6.2)

  • Steps: (1) survey households to find what they buy and how much they spend on each category (the weights); (2) record the prices of the basket in a base year (index = 100); (3) record prices each period; (4) calculate the weighted average price change; (5) inflation rate = % change in the CPI.
  • Weights reflect the importance of each item: a 10% rise in the price of food (30% of spending) has a bigger effect than a 10% rise in clothing (10%).
  • CPI index: (cost of basket this year ÷ cost of basket in base year) × 100. Inflation = (CPI now − CPI before) ÷ CPI before × 100.
  • Possible difficulties: the basket becomes out of date as buying patterns change; quality improvements and new products are hard to include; consumers substitute cheaper goods; households differ (poor households spend more on food and fuel); the informal economy and regional price differences are missed.

3 · Nominal and real values (4.6.3)

  • Nominal (money) values are measured at current prices. Real values are adjusted for inflation, so they show purchasing power.
  • Real value of money: if prices double, the same money buys half as much: its real value halves.
  • Real income ≈ nominal income growth − inflation. A 3% pay rise with 5% inflation is a 2% fall in real income.
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation.
Exam tipDo not confuse a lower inflation rate (disinflation) with falling prices (deflation). For CPI evaluation, give a developed reason: weights or basket becoming out of date, quality change or substitution.

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

The weight of food

In a low-income country households spend a large share of income on food, so food has a large weight in the CPI. A rise in food prices therefore pushes the CPI up a lot, and the inflation rate may overstate the experience of richer households.

Exam use: CPI weights and why households differ.

New technology and quality

A new phone costs the same as last year's but is much better. The CPI records the same price, so it misses the quality improvement and may overstate the true rise in the cost of living.

Exam use: a difficulty in measuring inflation.

A pay rise that is not one

Pay rises 3% in a year when inflation is 5%. The worker has more money but can buy less, so real income has fallen by about 2%.

Exam use: nominal versus real income.

Japan's falling prices

For years prices in Japan stagnated or fell. Consumers delayed purchases in the hope of lower prices, which held back spending and growth: a reason deflation is a policy concern.

Exam use: deflation discouraging consumption.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/23 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain how inflation is measured and why a rising rate of inflation may be of concern to consumers and producers.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): inflation is a sustained increase in the (general) price level (1) and is normally measured by the CPI or an equivalent index (1).
  • Application (up to 3): problems for consumers, such as loss of purchasing power for those on fixed incomes and erosion of the real value of savings (1 per explained problem).
  • Application (up to 3): problems for producers, such as menu costs and difficulty making long-term decisions such as investment (1 per explained problem). At least two problems for each should be explained; one problem well explained can gain 2 marks.
PlanInflation: sustained rise in the price level, measured by the CPI. Consumers: fixed incomes, savings. Producers: menu costs, uncertainty for investment.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. It is normally measured by the annual percentage change in a consumer prices index, which prices a weighted basket of goods and services bought by a typical household.

A rising rate of inflation reduces the purchasing power of money, so consumers on fixed incomes can buy less, and the real value of savings is eroded if interest rates are below inflation. For producers, rising prices bring menu costs, because prices must be changed, and uncertainty about future costs and prices, which makes long-term investment decisions harder.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q1(b) 2 marks Explain one reason why using the Consumer Price Index (CPI) to measure the inflation rate in an economy may not produce an accurate result.
What the mark scheme wants
  • The CPI is based on the average basket of products bought by all households (1), but as buying patterns change the basket used may no longer be typical (1).
  • OR the weights and products are usually updated every two or three years to reflect changes in buying patterns (1), but this may not be frequent enough if there is rapid change in the products bought or the proportion of income spent on them (1).
PlanBasket or weights out of date as buying patterns change.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The CPI uses a fixed basket of goods meant to represent what a typical household buys. As buying patterns change, for example with new products, the basket becomes out of date and may no longer be typical of all households, so the measured inflation rate may be inaccurate.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/21 · Q1(b) 2 marks Explain why price changes in the food and the non-food sectors of an economy are treated differently when calculating the CPI.
What the mark scheme wants
  • A basic recognition that price changes in the food and non-food sectors may be different, possibly with appropriate examples (1).
  • A clear recognition of the importance of weights in the construction of a CPI, with reference to weightings (1): products are given larger weights to reflect the greater proportion of income spent on them.
PlanPrice changes differ by sector. Weights reflect the share of spending, so each sector counts differently.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Food prices and non-food prices may change by different amounts in the same year. The CPI gives each a weight according to the share of household spending on it, so a rise in the price of food, which takes a large share of spending, has a bigger effect on the index than a rise in the price of something that takes a small share.

What the mark scheme wants
  • The real value is the quantity of products that can be bought for a certain amount of money (1).
  • If prices double, the quantity that can be bought is halved (1).

The real value of money is the quantity of goods and services a given amount can buy. If inflation causes prices to double, the same amount of money buys only half as much, so its real value has halved.

Based on Feb/March 2021 · 9708/22 · Q1(c) 2 marks Explain why deflation, as experienced by Japan, discourages consumption spending.
What the mark scheme wants
  • A clear understanding that deflation refers to a fall in the general price level (1).
  • An explanation of why a fall in the general price level discourages consumption (1): consumers expect prices to fall further, so they delay purchases, and the uncertainty encourages saving.
PlanDeflation: falling price level. Consumers delay purchases expecting lower prices.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Deflation is a fall in the general price level. When prices are expected to keep falling, consumers delay spending because the same goods will be cheaper in the future, and uncertainty makes them save more, so consumption spending falls.

Top tips

  • Know the three terms. Inflation: price level up. Disinflation: inflation rate down. Deflation: price level down.
  • Explain the CPI steps. Basket, weights, base year, index.
  • Weights matter. Bigger spending share, bigger effect.
  • Give a developed CPI difficulty. Out-of-date basket, quality, substitution.
  • Real = nominal adjusted for inflation. Real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation.
  • Real value of money. What it can buy.
  • Use the formula. (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
