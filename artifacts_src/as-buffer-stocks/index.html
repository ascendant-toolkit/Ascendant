Buffer Stock Schemes

3.2.5: how buffer stock schemes use a price floor and ceiling to stabilise agricultural prices, with the costs, risks and limits. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

A buffer stock scheme aims to stabilise the price of a product, usually an agricultural good, whose supply varies with the weather. A government agency sets a minimum (floor) price and a maximum (ceiling) price: when a good harvest pushes the price down to the floor, it buys the surplus into storage; when a poor harvest pushes the price up to the ceiling, it sells from its stock. This smooths prices and farmers' incomes and protects consumers from price spikes, but it needs money to buy and store the crop, a stock big enough to last, and a correctly judged price band. If the band is set too high, the agency buys too much and costs rise; if poor harvests run on, the stock can run out and the scheme collapses.

1 · How the scheme works (3.2.5)

  • Why prices fluctuate: the supply of crops is price inelastic in the short run and affected by weather, while demand for food is also inelastic, so small shifts in supply cause big price swings.
  • Good harvest: supply shifts right, price would fall below the floor, so the agency buys up the excess at the minimum price.
  • Poor harvest: supply shifts left, price would rise above the ceiling, so the agency sells from the stock at the maximum price.
  • In an average year, little action is needed. Over time the agency should buy and sell about the same amount, so the stock stays close to a safe level.

3 · Why are agricultural prices so unstable?

  • Supply is unpredictable: weather, disease and pests change the size of the harvest from year to year, shifting the supply curve.
  • Supply is inelastic in the short run: farmers cannot change output once the crop is planted, so they cannot respond to price within the season.
  • Demand is inelastic: food is a necessity, so a fall in price does not raise quantity demanded by much.
  • Result: with both curves steep, a small shift in supply leads to a large change in price. Example: if the quantity supplied falls by 10% and the price elasticity of demand is −0.5, the price must rise by about 20% to restore equilibrium.
  • Farmers' incomes can fall in a good harvest: if demand is inelastic, price falls by a larger percentage than quantity rises, so total revenue falls.
  • Planning problem: farmers decide how much to plant on last year's price, so output may overshoot and undershoot, and unstable income discourages investment in the long run.

4 · Worked example

Demand is P = 90 − Q. The agency sets a floor price of $40 and a ceiling of $60. In a normal year supply is P = 10 + Q (equilibrium $50, quantity 40).

YearWithout the schemeWith the scheme
Good harvest, supply P = Q − 30 (shifts right)Supply P = Q − 30 gives 90 − Q = Q − 30, so Q = 60 and the price falls to $30At the $40 floor, consumers buy 50 but producers supply 70, so the agency buys 20 units
Poor harvest, supply P = 50 + Q (shifts left)Supply P = 50 + Q gives 90 − Q = 50 + Q, so Q = 20 and the price rises to $70At the $60 ceiling, consumers want 30 but producers supply only 10, so the agency sells 20 units from stock
Cost of buying 20 units at $40None$800, to be recovered when the stock is sold at higher prices
  • The scheme works if the agency buys when prices are low and sells when they are high, so the stock cycles around a safe level. If the floor is set above the long-run equilibrium, the agency keeps buying and the stock grows without limit.

5 · Why schemes fail, and alternatives

  • Running out of stock: several years of poor harvests exhaust the stock, and the agency can no longer hold the price down.
  • Running out of money: several good harvests require the agency to keep buying, and it may not be able to finance it. For example, the International Tin Council's scheme ended in the mid-1980s when it ran short of funds.
  • Wrong price band: a floor set too high is just a minimum price with growing surpluses; too low and farmers get no protection.
  • Storage costs and spoilage for perishable goods; opportunity cost of the money tied up.
  • Alternatives: subsidies or income support for farmers, crop insurance, futures contracts and diversification into other crops or products, and improved storage and information.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Grain reserves

Some countries hold national reserves of rice or wheat. In a good year the government buys grain from farmers at a guaranteed price; in a shortage it releases stock at a set price to keep bread and rice affordable.

Exam use: buying at the floor and selling at the ceiling.

Coffee exporters

Countries that depend on coffee exports can see their incomes swing with world prices. A stock scheme can smooth them, but past international schemes failed when prices stayed above the band and stocks grew too large to finance.

Exam use: cost and the risk of setting the band too high.

Poor harvest years

After a drought the agency sells stock, so prices rise less than they otherwise would. If droughts last several years the reserve runs out, and prices then jump to the free market level.

Exam use: stock running out.

Food price spikes

Rising food prices hit low-income households hardest because they spend a large share of income on food. A release of stored food can hold prices down at a critical time.

Exam use: the case for buffer stocks and the equity argument.

Coffee and cocoa price swings

Countries that rely on one crop see export income and farmers' earnings swing with world prices. A stabilisation scheme could smooth these swings, but needs finance and a correct price band.

Exam use: price instability for a single-crop economy.

A run of poor harvests

A government agency sells grain from its stock to stop prices rising above the ceiling. After several bad years the stock runs out, the ceiling cannot be defended and prices jump.

Exam use: why buffer stocks can fail.

Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A buffer stock agency sets a minimum and a maximum price. In a good harvest supply shifts right, so it buys the surplus to hold the price at the floor, and in a poor harvest supply shifts left and it sells from the stock to hold the price at the ceiling. Stable prices protect farmers' incomes, encourage investment in the long run and keep food affordable for poor households. But buying and storing is costly, crops may spoil, the agency must judge the right price band, and a series of poor harvests could exhaust the stock, while a floor set too high would build up surpluses.

The benefits are likely to outweigh the costs when harvests fluctuate around a stable average and the band is well chosen. They may not if the floor is above the long-run equilibrium, if the stock is too small, or if storage is very expensive, so the benefits do not always outweigh the disadvantages.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/23 · Q3(b) 12 marks Assess the effectiveness of government intervention through buffer stock schemes in agricultural markets.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): how the scheme works and why agricultural prices fluctuate (inelastic supply and demand, weather); how it stabilises prices and incomes (up to 4); the limits on effectiveness, such as the cost, finite stocks, errors in setting the band, perishability, and the effect on producers' incentives (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on how effective the schemes are, with reasons and a conclusion.
PlanHow it works. Effective in stabilising price and income. Limits: cost, stock, wrong band. Judge: effective for short-term fluctuations, less for long-run trends.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Agricultural prices swing because supply depends on the weather and both supply and demand are inelastic. A buffer stock buys when the price falls to the floor and sells when it reaches the ceiling, which reduces swings and gives farmers stable incomes, so they invest more and supply is more reliable. However, running the scheme costs money for purchases, storage and administration, the stock is finite, and an incorrect price band either wastes money or fails to stabilise.

Buffer stocks are likely to be effective at smoothing short-run fluctuations, but not at dealing with a long-term fall in demand or prices, since the stock would grow without limit. Overall, effectiveness depends on correctly forecasting the long-run price and on the scheme having enough funds.

A buffer stock scheme buys coffee beans when the price falls to a set minimum and sells from the stock when it reaches a set maximum, which should narrow price swings. It would probably help in the short run, if swings come from harvest variation. Top tips

  • Draw floor and ceiling. Show buying in a good harvest and selling in a poor one.
  • Explain why prices swing. Weather shifts supply; supply and demand are inelastic.
  • Name the aim. Stabilise prices and farmers' incomes.
  • List costs. Buying, storage, spoilage, administration.
  • Mention running out. A run of bad harvests empties the stock.
  • Band must be right. A floor above the long-run price builds surpluses.
  • Compare with alternatives. Subsidies, futures markets, diversification.
