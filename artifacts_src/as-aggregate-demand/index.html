Aggregate Demand

4.3.1 to 4.3.5: aggregate demand and its components, the shape of the AD curve, and the causes of shifts in AD. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Aggregate demand (AD) is the total planned spending on an economy's goods and services at each price level in a given period, and equals C + I + G + (X − M): consumption, investment, government spending and net exports. The AD curve slopes downward because a lower price level raises real wealth, lowers interest rates and makes exports more competitive and imports less so. A change in the price level causes a movement along the AD curve; a change in any component at every price level (interest rates, income taxes, confidence, the exchange rate, government spending, foreign income) shifts the whole curve right or left. Detailed knowledge of the components is not required, only what they are and what shifts them.

1 · The components of AD (4.3.1 to 4.3.3)

ComponentMeaningDeterminants (examples)
C ConsumptionHousehold spending on goods and servicesDisposable income, income taxes, interest rates, wealth, consumer confidence, credit
I InvestmentFirms' spending on capital goods (and stock building)Interest rates, business confidence and expected profits, technology, corporation tax
G Government spendingSpending on goods, services and infrastructure (not transfer payments)Government policy, budget position, political aims
X − M Net exportsExport spending less import spendingExchange rate, foreign incomes, price competitiveness, protectionism
  • Closed economy: AD = C + I + G (no trade). Open economy: add X − M.
  • Transfer payments are not part of G because they are not spending on output; they affect AD indirectly through disposable income.

2 · Shape of the AD curve and shifts (4.3.4 to 4.3.5)

  • Why AD slopes down: (1) wealth effect: a lower price level raises the real value of savings, so people spend more; (2) interest rate effect: lower prices reduce demand for money and interest rates, raising spending; (3) international trade effect: lower domestic prices make exports cheaper and imports dearer, so net exports rise.
  • Movement along AD: caused only by a change in the price level.
  • Shift right (increase): lower interest rates, lower income tax, higher confidence, higher government spending, depreciation of the currency, growth in trading partners' incomes, easier credit.
  • Shift left (decrease): higher interest rates, higher taxes, falling confidence, cuts in government spending, appreciation, a recession abroad, tighter credit.
  • An increase in AD raises real output and the price level; how much of each depends on the shape of the AS curve.

5 · The effect of a change in AD on output and prices

  • The effect depends on the shape of the AS curve: if there is spare capacity (the flat part), a rise in AD mainly raises real output and employment; near full capacity (the steep part), it mainly raises the price level.
  • Benefits of higher AD: economic growth, falling unemployment, higher incomes and tax revenue.
  • Problems: inflationary pressure, a worsening current account (more imports), a larger budget deficit if driven by government spending, and possible overheating.
  • Lower AD reduces inflation but may cause recession and unemployment.

6 · Common mistakes

Do not write thisA change in the price level shifts the AD curve (it causes a movement along it). Do not include transfer payments in G. Do not say that AD slopes downward for the same reason as a micro demand curve: it slopes down because of the wealth, interest rate and international trade effects.
Exam tipName the component that changes and say which way the AD curve shifts. Do not draw a change in the price level as a shift. For essays, say what then happens to real output and prices using the AS curve.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Lower interest rates

A central bank cuts interest rates. Borrowing becomes cheaper, so households buy more on credit (C) and firms invest more (I). Aggregate demand shifts right, raising real output and, as the economy nears capacity, prices.

Exam use: interest rates affecting C and I.

A weaker currency

If the exchange rate falls, exports become cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home, so net exports (X − M) rise and AD shifts right, other things equal.

Exam use: the exchange rate affecting net exports.

A fall in consumer confidence

Households fear job losses, so they cut spending and save more. Consumption falls, AD shifts left, and output and employment tend to fall.

Exam use: confidence as a determinant of consumption.

Government road building

State spending on infrastructure is part of G. It raises AD directly, and also supports the economy's capacity if it improves transport, so it may also shift AS in the long run.

Exam use: G as a component of AD.

A cut in interest rates

A central bank cuts interest rates. Borrowing becomes cheaper, so households spend more on credit and firms invest more, and a weaker currency helps exports. AD shifts right.

Exam use: interest rates changing C, I and net exports.

A fall in consumer confidence

News of job losses makes households pessimistic. They cut back on big purchases and save more, so consumption falls and AD shifts left, even though prices and incomes have not yet changed.

Exam use: confidence as a determinant of AD.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/21 · Q4(a) 8 marks Explain three of the components of aggregate demand and consider the extent to which they may be increased without leading to inflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): a clear and accurate explanation of three components of AD: consumer spending, investment, government spending and net exports (1 each). Simply mentioning a component does not earn a mark.
  • Analysis (up to 3): one cause of inflation is where AD rises more than AS (1), but it considers why this may or may not occur, for example the economy is operating below capacity or some of the increase in AD also increases AS. If both scenarios are considered, full marks may be awarded; one-sided answers get a maximum of 1.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): consideration of both sides (1), leading to a valid conclusion (1).
PlanThree components explained. Inflation if AD rises faster than AS. No inflation if spare capacity or AS also rises. Judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Consumption is household spending on goods and services, such as cars and holidays. Investment is firms' spending on capital goods, such as machinery. Government spending includes spending on public services and infrastructure, such as schools and roads.

If these components increase and the economy has spare capacity, firms can raise output without raising prices, so little inflation results. If AD rises faster than AS, as when the economy is near full capacity, the extra demand pulls up prices.

Whether inflation follows depends on how much spare capacity there is and whether the rise in AD, for example from investment, also raises AS. Investment is the most likely to be increased without inflation, because it adds to capacity.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/23 · Q4(a) 8 marks With the help of a diagram, explain how increases in aggregate demand affect the level of real output and the price level in an economy and consider when such increases may become a problem.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): the components of AD, C + G + I + X − M (1); an accurately labelled AD/AS diagram with the initial equilibrium (1); a rightward shift of AD and the subsequent changes in the price level and real GDP (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): an explanation of why AD might increase (1); why the increase may not cause problems such as inflation, for example because of the shape of the AS curve or because AS may increase in line with AD if the cause is investment (1); why it may cause problems, for example the economy is at full capacity and AS cannot increase (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a recognition that increases in AD may cause an inflationary rise in the price level beyond the full-employment level of output (1), with a justified conclusion (1).
PlanDiagram: AD shifts right, output and price level rise. No problem with spare capacity. Problem at full capacity: inflation only.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Aggregate demand is C + I + G + (X − M). On an AD/AS diagram, the initial equilibrium is where AD meets short-run AS. An increase in AD shifts the curve right, so real output and the price level both rise.

AD may increase because of lower interest rates, which raise consumption and investment. If the economy has spare capacity, output rises a lot and prices little, which is beneficial. But at or near full capacity AS cannot increase, so extra AD mainly raises prices.

The increases become a problem when output cannot rise further, as then the rise in AD is inflationary beyond the full-employment level of output. The result depends on the position of the economy and the shape of the AS curve.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/22 · Q5(a) 8 marks With the help of a diagram, explain one reason for an increase in aggregate demand and one reason for an increase in aggregate supply and consider whether an increase in aggregate demand will always lead to inflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): AD is the total demand for an economy's goods and services at a given price level, or C + I + G + (X − M) (1); AS is the total output producers are willing and able to supply at a given price level (1); an accurate diagram showing AD and AS curves (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): a valid cause of an increase in AD (1); a valid cause of an increase in AS (1); both increases shown by rightward shifts, with the resulting impact on the price level on an accurate diagram (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): whether an increase in AD always leads to inflation depends on the increase in AS and the shape of or position on the AS curve.
PlanDefine AD and AS. One cause each, shown as right shifts. AD rise leads to inflation only if AS does not rise or the economy is near capacity.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Aggregate demand is total spending on a country's output at each price level, C + I + G + (X − M). Aggregate supply is the total output firms are willing and able to produce at each price level.

AD might increase because of lower interest rates, which raise consumption and investment, shifting AD to the right. AS might increase because of better technology, which lowers costs, shifting SRAS to the right. Together they raise real output, and the price level may rise only a little, or fall.

An increase in AD does not always lead to inflation. If there is spare capacity, or AS also increases, output rises with little change in the price level, but if the economy is near full capacity, inflation is more likely.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/23 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess whether increases in aggregate demand are the best way of reducing unemployment in a high-income country.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): what is meant by unemployment and its main types in a high-income economy; increases in AD arise from fiscal and monetary policy; policies such as lower taxes, higher government spending and lower interest rates may reduce cyclical unemployment but may not reduce structural unemployment; supply-side policy may reduce unemployment by influencing AS, through training, education and deregulation, but is a long-term approach, may be costly and does not address falls in AD behind cyclical unemployment.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): considers alternative policies and balances them; a judgement on whether AD is the best way, with a conclusion.
PlanAD: cuts cyclical unemployment, quick, but not structural; inflation, deficit. Supply-side: structural, slow, costly. Judge by type of unemployment.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Increasing AD through lower interest rates, tax cuts or more government spending raises output, so firms need more workers and cyclical unemployment falls, and this can work quickly. But if unemployment is structural, because workers' skills do not match vacancies, more spending creates little employment and may only raise prices. Higher AD can also worsen the budget deficit and the current account. Supply-side policy, such as training and education, tackles structural unemployment but takes years, costs money and does not help where the problem is weak demand.

Increasing AD is the best way when unemployment is mainly cyclical and there is spare capacity, but not when it is structural. A high-income country is likely to have both types, so a mix of demand-side and supply-side policies is likely to be most effective.

Based on May/June 2021 · 9708/23 · Q3(b) 12 marks Discuss whether an increase in aggregate demand would be of overall benefit for an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the benefits of an increase in AD to the economy (up to 4), such as economic growth and higher employment, considering firms, government and consumers or workers; the possible problems (up to 4), such as inflation and a current account deficit.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): clearly compares the benefits and the problems resulting from an increase in AD, with one mark reserved for a reasoned conclusion. No KU marks for merely explaining terms in the question.
PlanBenefits: growth, jobs, profits, tax revenue. Problems: inflation, current account deficit, overheating. Judge: depends on spare capacity.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A rise in AD raises real output, which creates jobs for workers, higher profits for firms and more tax revenue for the government. But if the economy is near full capacity the extra demand mainly raises prices, causing inflation, and higher incomes raise spending on imports, worsening the current account.

The overall benefit is likely to be greatest when there is spare capacity and unemployment is high, and smallest when the economy is already near full capacity. So an increase in AD is beneficial overall, but not always, and its benefits depend on how much capacity is available.

Top tips

  • AD = C + I + G + (X − M). Know each component and an example of what shifts it.
  • Price level moves along; everything else shifts. A fall in prices is a movement, not a shift.
  • Explain the downward slope. Wealth, interest rate and international trade effects.
  • Name the component. Interest rates affect C and I; the exchange rate affects X − M.
  • Say what happens next. Output and price level, depending on AS.
  • Transfer payments are not G. They affect AD through income.
  • Judge with capacity. Spare capacity means growth; full capacity means inflation.
