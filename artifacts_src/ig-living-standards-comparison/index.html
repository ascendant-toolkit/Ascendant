IGCSE Economics · 0455 · 5.1.2 Comparing living standards and income distribution

Comparing Living Standards and Income Distribution

Why living standards differ between countries and why income is unequally distributed within them. Syllabus 5.1.2.

The big idea

Living standards and income differ between countries and also within them. Between countries, differences come from productivity, capital, education, health, technology, institutions, trade, conflict and natural resources. Within a country, income is unequally distributed between rich and poor, urban and rural, regions, and by gender, because of differences in skills, jobs, wealth, taxes and benefits. An average such as GDP per head hides these differences.

Why living standards differ between countries

FactorHow it raises (or lowers) living standards
Productivity and technologyMore output per worker means higher incomes; low productivity keeps incomes low
Capital and investmentMore machinery, roads and power raise output; poor countries often lack capital and save little
Education and healthSkilled, healthy workers earn more; poor health and low schooling hold back growth
Institutions and stabilityStrong property rights, low corruption and political stability attract investment; conflict destroys capital
Natural resources and geographyOil, minerals and fertile land can raise income; landlocked countries, deserts and disaster-prone areas face costs
Trade and globalisationOpen trade and investment can raise growth; dependence on a few exports can leave countries vulnerable
Population growthFast population growth can spread resources thinly
Government policiesSpending on schools, health and infrastructure and sensible economic policy raise living standards

Why income is unequally distributed within a country

  • Wages and skills: highly educated workers earn more than unskilled workers.
  • Unemployment and low pay: some groups face unemployment or low-paid, insecure jobs.
  • Ownership of wealth: people who own property, shares or businesses earn rents, profits and dividends.
  • Location: cities usually have higher wages and better services than rural areas; some regions are left behind.
  • Discrimination and gender: unequal pay or access to education and jobs.
  • Taxes and benefits: progressive taxes and generous benefits reduce inequality; the opposite increases it.
  • Informal work: many poorer workers have no pension or protection.

Measuring income distribution

Households can be ranked from poorest to richest and split into five equal groups (quintiles). In an equal country each group has about 20% of income. In an unequal country the richest 20% may have 50% or more and the poorest 20% only a few per cent.

Cities and rural areas

City dwellers often have higher wages, more jobs and better schools and hospitals, but higher housing costs, congestion, pollution and slums. Rural dwellers may have lower incomes and poorer services, but lower living costs and cleaner air.

Low-income farmers

Low productivity (little capital, small plots), low and unstable prices, weather risks and seasonal work give low and uncertain incomes; poor roads, schools and healthcare lower living standards further.

Norway and Niger

Norway's real GDP per head is more than 100 times that of Niger. Differences in productivity, capital, education, health, stability and institutions all help to explain the gap.

Exam use: Reasons for differences between countries.

Cities and slums

In many fast-growing countries, city incomes are far above rural incomes, but a large share of city dwellers live in informal settlements with poor sanitation.

Exam use: Both sides of urban living standards.

Oil in Guyana

A past paper asks whether the discovery of oil is likely to raise living standards in Guyana. The answer depends on who gains and how the revenue is used.

Exam use: Natural resources and the distribution of income.

Reducing inequality through taxes and benefits

Countries such as Denmark and Sweden have high taxes and generous benefits and have a smaller gap between rich and poor than many countries with low taxes.

Q1 Explain 4 marks Explain two reasons for differences in living standards between countries. (Oct/Nov 2022, P23)
ReminderTwo different reasons, each with a link: productivity, education and health, capital, resources, stability.
One reason is differences in productivity and technology. Countries with more capital and better technology produce more per worker, so incomes and the goods people can afford are higher. Another reason is education and health. Healthy, skilled workers earn more and countries with better schooling and healthcare can grow faster, raising living standards.
Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain two factors that could influence living standards, apart from unemployment rates. (Oct/Nov 2024, P21)
ReminderIncome, education, health, cost of living, working hours, environment.
Income or wage levels are one factor. Higher wages allow people to afford more goods and services, so living standards are higher. Another factor is the cost of living. If prices are high relative to incomes, purchasing power is lower and people can afford less.
Q3 Explain 4 marks Explain two reasons why income may be unequally distributed within a country.
ReminderSkills and wages; ownership of wealth; location; tax and benefits; discrimination.
Differences in skills and education lead to differences in wages. Highly qualified workers can earn much more than unskilled workers, so income is unequal. Ownership of wealth is also unequal. People who own property, shares or businesses earn rent, dividends and profits on top of wages, so they can earn much more than those who rely on wages alone.
Q4 Analyse 6 marks Analyse why low-income farmers are likely to have low living standards. (Feb/Mar 2023, P22)
ReminderChain: low productivity, low and unstable income, little spending on necessities, poor access to services.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Low-income farmers often farm small plots with little capital and old methods, so yields and productivity are low. Crop prices may be low and unstable and the weather brings risks, so incomes are low and uncertain. With low income they can afford less food, healthcare and schooling, which lowers their health, education and ability to raise productivity. Poor roads and markets may increase costs and cut them off from services, keeping living standards low.
Q5 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how growth in a country's tertiary sector can increase its living standards. (Oct/Nov 2024, P22)
ReminderServices create jobs and income; tax revenue; better services; link to GDP per head.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
The tertiary sector provides services such as banking, tourism, health and education. Growth creates jobs and raises incomes, so spending power and GDP per head could rise. Higher output and profits raise tax revenue, which the government can spend on schools and hospitals. The services themselves, such as better healthcare and education, directly improve living standards. However, the gains may be concentrated in cities or among skilled workers.
Q6 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether or not people living in cities have a higher living standard than those living in rural areas. (Oct/Nov 2023, P22)
ReminderCities: wages, jobs, services; costs: housing, congestion, slums, pollution. Rural: lower income but lower costs, cleaner air. End with 'it depends on income, costs and quality of life'.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Living standards describe the goods, services and quality of life people can obtain. People in cities often have higher wages and more job opportunities, and better access to schools, hospitals, transport and entertainment, which could raise living standards. This is likely to be more important in poor countries where rural areas lack services. However, cities have higher housing and living costs, congestion and pollution, and low-paid workers may live in slums. Rural areas may have lower incomes but also lower costs, cleaner air and stronger communities. Overall, average living standards are likely to be higher in cities, but it depends on the person's income and housing costs, and on non-income factors such as pollution and leisure.
Q7 Discuss 6 marks Discuss whether or not a cut in income tax rates will increase living standards. (May/June 2022, P22)
ReminderFor: higher disposable income. Against: lower tax revenue and public services; benefits high earners; inequality; saving.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
A cut in income tax rates raises people's disposable income. They can afford more goods and services, so living standards could rise, and the incentive to work may increase, raising output. However, lower tax revenue may mean less government spending on healthcare, schools and benefits, which could reduce living standards, particularly for low-income households. The gains may also go mainly to higher earners, and people may save the extra income. Overall, a tax cut could raise living standards for those who gain, but the effect on the whole population depends on how the government pays for it and who benefits most.

Top tips

  • Split between and within countries. Different factors apply to each.
  • Give a link for each factor. 'Education raises skills, so wages and living standards are higher.'
  • Averages hide distribution. Use quintile or rich/poor ideas.
  • Name the group affected. Farmers, city workers, women, low-skilled workers.
  • Evaluate with costs as well as benefits. City living costs, pollution, who gains from a tax cut.
