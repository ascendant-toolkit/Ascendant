Indirect Taxes: Impact and Incidence

3.2.1: specific and ad valorem indirect taxes, their effect on price and quantity, incidence and elasticity, and tax revenue. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

An indirect tax is a tax on spending, collected by firms but paid partly or wholly by consumers; a specific tax is a fixed sum per unit, while an ad valorem tax is a percentage of the price. A tax raises firms' costs, so the supply curve shifts to the left (up) by the amount of the tax, which raises the price and reduces the quantity traded. The incidence of the tax is who actually bears it: consumers bear the rise in the price they pay, and producers bear the rest of the tax out of the price they keep, with the split depending on the price elasticity of demand and supply. The government gains tax revenue (tax per unit times the quantity sold after the tax), but there is a loss of consumer and producer surplus and some quantity is no longer traded.

1 · Impact of a specific tax (3.2.1)

  • The vertical gap between the old supply curve and the new one is the tax per unit.
  • The price consumers pay rises (but by less than the tax); the price producers keep (price paid minus tax) falls; quantity falls.
  • Tax revenue = tax per unit × quantity sold after the tax.
  • Specific vs ad valorem: a specific tax shifts supply parallel; an ad valorem tax makes the new curve pivot, getting further from the old curve at higher prices.

2 · Incidence and elasticity

CaseWho bears more of the tax
Demand inelastic (steep), supply elasticConsumers: price rises by almost the whole tax, quantity falls little
Demand elastic (flat), supply inelasticProducers: they must cut the price they receive, or lose sales
Demand perfectly inelasticConsumers bear all of it
Demand perfectly elastic (or supply perfectly inelastic)Producers bear all of it
  • Consumer share of the tax = PES ÷ (PES + PED) in size terms; the more inelastic your side, the more of the tax you bear.
  • Why governments use them: raise revenue; discourage demerit goods (tobacco, alcohol, sugary drinks); make users pay for external costs.
  • Limitations: if demand is inelastic the tax may not reduce consumption much; they can be regressive (take a bigger share of low incomes); high taxes may cause black markets or avoidance; the correct tax size is hard to estimate; producers' jobs may be hit.

3 · Types of indirect tax

Specific taxAd valorem tax
MeaningA fixed amount per unit (for example $2 per litre)A percentage of the price (for example 20% VAT)
Effect on supply curveParallel shift up by the taxPivots: the gap widens as the price rises
Tax per unit as price risesStays the sameRises
ExamplesFuel duty, tobacco duty per packet, alcohol duty per unitVAT or sales tax
  • Direct and indirect taxes: a direct tax (income tax) is paid by the person it is levied on; an indirect tax is levied on spending and collected by firms, but the burden may be passed on to consumers.
  • Why the supply curve shifts: the tax is an extra cost of production for every unit, so at each price firms are willing to supply less.

4 · Worked example

Demand is P = 90 − Q and supply is P = 10 + Q, so equilibrium is a price of $50 and quantity of 40. The government imposes a specific tax of $10 per unit. The new supply curve is P = 20 + Q.

ItemWorkingResult
New equilibrium90 − Q = 20 + QQ = 35, price paid by consumers = $55
Price received by producers55 − 10$45
Consumers' burden per unit55 − 50$5
Producers' burden per unit50 − 45$5
Tax revenue$10 × 35$350 (consumers $175, producers $175)
Welfare loss (deadweight loss)½ × $10 × (40 − 35)$25
  • Here the tax is shared equally because the demand and supply curves have the same steepness. If demand were steeper (more inelastic), consumers would bear more than $5.
  • Use the quantity after the tax (35) to calculate revenue, not the old quantity.

5 · Who gains and who loses?

GroupEffect of an indirect tax
ConsumersPay a higher price, buy less, lose consumer surplus
ProducersReceive a lower price net of tax, sell less, lose producer surplus; workers may lose jobs if output falls a lot
GovernmentGains tax revenue that can fund services or reduce other taxes
SocietyMay benefit if the tax reduces harmful consumption; loses the transactions that no longer take place (welfare loss)

6 · Why use indirect taxes, and how effective are they?

  • To raise revenue: most effective on goods with inelastic demand (fuel, tobacco), since sales fall little and revenue is high.
  • To reduce consumption of demerit goods: works only if demand is relatively elastic. If demand is inelastic or the good is addictive, consumption falls little.
  • To alter relative prices and encourage substitutes, for example taxing high-sugar drinks so producers cut the sugar content.
  • Disadvantages: regressive (take a larger share of low incomes), may cause smuggling or black markets, raise firms' costs, hurt producers if demand is elastic, and the right level is hard to set.
  • Compared with other policies such as bans, subsidies for substitutes, information campaigns or minimum prices, a tax uses the market and raises revenue but does not guarantee the target.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Fuel duty

A fixed amount of tax is added to each litre of petrol. Demand for fuel is inelastic in the short run, so most of the tax is passed on to drivers and consumption falls only a little. The government collects large revenue.

Exam use: a specific tax and the incidence on consumers.

A sugar tax

A tax on sugary drinks aims to cut over-consumption of a demerit good. Producers may absorb some of it by changing recipes or cutting margins, and prices of the highest-sugar drinks rise.

Exam use: using an indirect tax to correct information failure, and incidence shared with producers.

Value added tax

A percentage added to the price of most goods. It is an ad valorem tax, so the amount of tax rises with the price. It raises a lot of revenue but takes a larger share of low incomes, so it can be regressive.

Exam use: ad valorem tax and its fairness.

Tax on a luxury good

A tax on yachts is paid mostly by sellers if buyers can easily switch to other luxuries (elastic demand). Fewer boats are sold, so producers lose volume as well as price.

Exam use: elastic demand and a greater producer share.

A tax on sugary drinks

A government taxes soft drinks according to their sugar content. Producers may cut the sugar content to avoid the tax, and consumers who keep buying pay more. Revenue can fund health programmes, but if demand is inelastic consumption may fall only a little.

Exam use: an indirect tax on a demerit good, elasticity and the incidence.

Fuel duty

Fuel has few substitutes in the short run, so demand is inelastic. A tax raises a large amount of revenue and consumers bear most of it, but the tax takes a larger share of income from low-income drivers.

Exam use: inelastic demand, revenue and regressivity.

Based on Feb/March 2024 · 9708/22 · Q2(a) 8 marks With the help of a demand and supply diagram, explain how the introduction of an indirect tax affects equilibrium in a market and consider the extent to which the incidence of the tax will fall on the consumer.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): an accurate definition of an indirect tax (1); an accurate diagram with labelled axes and equilibrium price and quantity (1); understanding that indirect taxes are used to discourage consumption of demerit goods (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): the diagram shows an indirect tax shifting the supply curve to the left (1), raising the price to the consumer (1) and reducing the quantity demanded (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): the incidence on the consumer depends on the price elasticity of demand: the more inelastic demand, the more of the tax is passed to the consumer (1), and a justified conclusion (1).
PlanDefine the tax. Diagram: supply left, price up, quantity down. Evaluate: incidence depends on PED.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

An indirect tax is a tax on spending, such as VAT or a duty on cigarettes, collected by the seller but ultimately paid by consumers or producers. On a diagram, the original equilibrium is where demand meets supply at P1 and Q1. Governments often use indirect taxes to discourage consumption of demerit goods.

The tax raises costs by a fixed amount for each unit, so the supply curve shifts to the left. The equilibrium price paid by consumers rises to P2 and the quantity falls to Q2.

How much of the tax falls on consumers depends on the price elasticity of demand. If demand is inelastic, consumers have few substitutes, so the price rises by nearly the whole tax and consumers bear most of it. If demand is elastic, producers absorb more of it. So the incidence is likely to fall mainly on consumers for goods such as cigarettes, but not for goods with close substitutes.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/23 · Q2(b) 12 marks Discuss whether the incidence of an indirect tax on a product always affects its consumers more than its producers.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): an explanation of the incidence of an indirect tax and how it affects consumers in terms of the price paid and the quantity they buy (up to 4); how it affects producers in terms of the price they receive, revenue and sales (up to 4). The incidence depends on the price elasticity of demand and supply: the more inelastic PED, the greater the burden on consumers.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): judgement on whether consumers always bear more, supported by consideration of elasticity values, and a conclusion (1).
PlanIncidence depends on elasticity. Inelastic demand: consumers. Elastic demand or inelastic supply: producers. Judge: not always.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The incidence of an indirect tax is the way the burden is divided between consumers, through a higher price, and producers, through a lower price kept after the tax. The tax shifts supply to the left and raises the price, but by less than the tax. If demand is inelastic, such as for fuel, the price rises by nearly the whole tax and quantity falls little, so consumers bear most of it. If demand is elastic, or supply inelastic, consumers can switch away, so the price rises little and producers absorb more, and lose sales.

So consumers do not always bear more of the tax. It depends on the relative elasticities of demand and supply and on how quickly firms can adjust. The tax is more likely to fall on consumers for necessities and addictive goods, and on producers where there are close substitutes.

What the mark scheme wants

  • A specific tax is a fixed amount per unit, such as a duty on fuel or alcohol (1).
PlanPercentage of price versus fixed amount per unit.

An ad valorem tax is a percentage of the price, so the tax rises with the price, as VAT does. A specific tax is a fixed sum on each unit sold, such as a duty of a set amount per litre of fuel.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/22 · Q2(b) 12 marks If the cost of a subsidy is to be paid from tax revenues, discuss whether the revenue should be raised through direct taxes or indirect taxes.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): how direct taxes (such as income tax) work, their effect on incentives and income distribution, and the problems of collecting them (up to 4); how indirect taxes work, their effect on prices and on different income groups, and their problems (up to 4). Indicative content may include progressive and regressive effects, ease of collection and effect on consumption.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): judgement on which tax is more suitable, such as using equity, efficiency and the purpose of the subsidy, with a conclusion.
PlanDirect tax: progressive, equity, disincentive. Indirect tax: easy to collect, regressive. Judge: a mix, depending on aims.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Direct taxes, such as income tax, can be progressive, taking a larger share from high earners, which is fairer, and they may be a stable source of revenue. But they may reduce the incentive to work and can be avoided. Indirect taxes are easy to collect and can be set to discourage demerit goods, and spending can be hard to hide. But they are regressive, taking a larger share of low incomes, and raise prices, which could reduce spending.

A mix is probably best. If the subsidy helps low-income households, as with vaccinations, funding it mainly from progressive direct taxes seems fairer, though indirect taxes might be preferred if the aim is to raise revenue quickly with fewer disincentives to work.

Top tips

  • Draw the tax as a vertical gap. Between the old and new supply curves.
  • Label three things. Price paid by consumers, price received by producers, tax revenue.
  • Say the price rises by less than the tax. Unless demand is perfectly inelastic.
  • Use elasticity for incidence. More inelastic demand means a bigger consumer share.
  • Revenue = tax × quantity after. Use the new quantity, not the old one.
  • Match tax to aim. Revenue: inelastic demand works. Cutting use: needs elastic demand.
  • Mention fairness. Indirect taxes can be regressive.
