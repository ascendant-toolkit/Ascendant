Floating-point Numbers: Normalisation, Range and Errors

Coursebook: Chapter 13 · Data representation, section 13.3 Floating-point numbers, representation and manipulation

Why and how numbers are normalised, how the allocation of bits affects range and precision, overflow, underflow and rounding errors; animations, labs and the real 9618 theory questions from 2021 to 2026.

This page in five sentences

A floating-point number is normalised when its mantissa starts 01 (positive) or 10 (negative), so there are no wasted leading bits; this gives the most precision for the bits available, a single representation for each number, and the widest range. To normalise, shift the mantissa left until the first two bits differ and subtract the number of shifts from the exponent. With a fixed total number of bits, more bits for the mantissa give more precision and fewer for the exponent give a smaller range, and the reverse. A result too large to store causes overflow, one too small (closer to zero than the smallest number) causes underflow. Many numbers, such as 0.1, cannot be stored exactly in binary, so the stored value is an approximation and rounding errors appear.

1 · Normalisation

In two's complement the first bit is the sign. A mantissa is normalised if its first two bits are different:

MantissaNormalised?Reason
0111010000Yesstarts 01: positive, 0.1...
1000110000Yesstarts 10: negative
0001111000Nostarts 00: the leading zeros are wasted
1110000000Nostarts 11: the leading ones are wasted

A normalised positive mantissa has a value from 0.5 up to just below 1, and a normalised negative mantissa from −1 up to just below −0.5.

How to normalise

  1. Shift the mantissa left until its first two bits are different, adding zeros on the right.
  2. Each shift left makes the number twice as big, so subtract 1 from the exponent for every shift (to keep the same value).
  3. Write the new mantissa and exponent. Check: the value is unchanged.
StepMantissaExponent
Given (starts 00, not normalised)0000000111100111 (−25)
Shift left 6 places: starts 010111000000100111 − 6 = 100001 (−31)
Why normalise?(1) To store the maximum range of numbers in the minimum number of bits. (2) It minimises the number of leading zeros or ones, so the most significant bits are kept: the number has the greatest possible precision. (3) It avoids a number having multiple representations.

2 · Range and precision: how the bits are shared

The total number of bits is fixed (for example 16). Every bit given to the mantissa is taken from the exponent.

ChangeEffect on precision (accuracy)Effect on range
More bits for the mantissa, fewer for the exponentIncreases: more significant digits can be storedDecreases: the exponent is smaller, so the largest and smallest numbers are less extreme
Fewer bits for the mantissa, more for the exponentDecreasesIncreases: larger and smaller numbers can be stored

The range and the accuracy trade off: when the range increases the accuracy decreases, and the other way round. For a mantissa of m bits and an exponent of e bits (normalised, two's complement), with E = 2e−1:

  • Largest positive: mantissa 0111...1 and the largest exponent: (1 − 2−(m−1)) × 2E−1
  • Smallest positive: mantissa 0100...0 (0.5) and the most negative exponent: 0.5 × 2−E = 2−E−1
  • Largest negative number (most negative): mantissa 1000...0 (−1) with the largest exponent.
MantissaExponentLargest positive numberSmallest positive number
8 bits8 bits(1 − 2−7) × 21272−129
10 bits6 bits(1 − 2−9) × 2312−33
11 bits5 bits(1 − 2−10) × 215 = 327362−17
12 bits4 bits(1 − 2−11) × 27 = 127.93752−9

For 12 bits and 4 bits the largest positive number is 127.9375 (exponent 7). Giving 10 bits to the mantissa and 6 to the exponent increases the range (up to 231) but reduces the precision of each number.

3 · Overflow and underflow

OverflowUnderflow
What happensThe result of a calculation is too large to be stored: the exponent needed is bigger than the system can holdThe result is too small: closer to zero than the smallest number the system can represent
ExampleMultiplying two very large numbers; adding 91.5 and 123.25 in a system whose largest value is 127.9375 (the answer, 214.75, needs an exponent of 8)Dividing a very small number by a large one repeatedly
ConsequenceThe most significant bits are lost, so the answer is wrong, or the program stops with an errorThe result is stored as zero (or loses all accuracy), so later calculations are wrong

4 · Approximations and rounding errors

A binary fraction can only be built from halves, quarters, eighths and so on. A decimal fraction such as 0.1 or 0.2 is a recurring binary fraction (0.000110011...), and a finite mantissa has to cut it off, so the stored number is only the nearest value it can hold. The difference is a rounding error.

NumberStored in 12 + 4 bitsError
0.10.0999755859−0.0000244...
65.265.1875−0.0125
0.250.25none: 1/4 is a power of two
print(0.1 + 0.2)              # 0.30000000000000004print(0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3)       # False print(abs((0.1 + 0.2) - 0.3) < 1e-9)   # True: compare with a tolerance, not ==
  • Rounding errors build up when many calculations are done on approximate numbers.
  • More mantissa bits give a closer approximation but never remove the problem.
  • Programs compare real numbers using a small tolerance, and store money as a whole number of pence.

5 · Common ways to lose marks

  • Calling a mantissa normalised because it starts with 0. It must start 01 or 10.
  • Changing the exponent in the wrong direction when normalising: each shift left reduces the exponent by 1.
  • Saying more mantissa bits give a bigger range. They give more precision; more exponent bits give the bigger range.
  • Mixing up overflow (too large) and underflow (too small, near zero).
  • Explaining an approximation without saying why: the fractional part cannot be written exactly as a sum of powers of ½ in the available bits, so bits are lost or rounded.
  • Not giving the consequence when asked: loss of precision, a wrong answer, or the value stored as zero.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Spreadsheet totals that are off by a tiny amount

A sum of prices can show 0.30000000000000004 instead of 0.3. The computer holds 0.1 and 0.2 as approximations, so adding them gives an answer that is very slightly wrong.

Exam use: rounding errors come from binary approximations.

A game physics engine

If an object's speed is multiplied by 0.99 many times it may shrink until it is too small to store and becomes zero. That is underflow, and the object stops.

Exam use: underflow: a result closer to zero than the smallest number.

A scientific simulation

A model of a galaxy multiplies huge masses and distances. If the result is bigger than the largest number the format can hold, the program reports an overflow error or gives a wrong answer.

Exam use: overflow: a result too large to store.

Choosing 32-bit or 64-bit floats

A 64-bit float has more bits for both parts, giving far more precision and range than a 32-bit float, but uses twice the memory.

Exam use: the allocation of bits sets the precision and the range.

Storing money

Banks store pounds and pence as a whole number of pence, because 0.10 cannot be held exactly as a binary fraction and tiny errors would add up over millions of transactions.

Exam use: avoid floating point where exact values matter.

Top tips

  • Normalised means 01 or 10. If the first two bits match, shift left and lower the exponent for each shift.
  • Mantissa bits: precision. Exponent bits: range. A fixed total means gaining one costs the other.
  • Give the cause and the consequence. Overflow: too large, bits lost. Underflow: too small, stored as zero. Approximation: lost bits, rounding error.
  • Say why 0.1 is an approximation. It is not a sum of powers of ½, so it recurs in binary and has to be cut off.
  • Use numbers from the question. 'The mantissa needs 13 bits but only 10 are available, so the last 3 are lost.'
  • Quote the limit. In 12 + 4 bits the largest number is 127.9375, so 214.75 overflows.
