Equity and Redistribution of Income and Wealth

Fair is not the same as equal. How inequality is measured (Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient), why it arises, and how taxes, benefits and supply-side policies redistribute income, with the trade-offs governments face. Animated diagrams built from numbers, labs, drills and every past paper question we could find.

The chapter in four sentences

Equality means everyone gets the same; equity means a fair distribution, which is a value judgement. Inequality can be measured with a Lorenz curve and a Gini coefficient (0 is perfect equality, 1 is perfect inequality), for both income (a flow) and wealth (a stock). Governments redistribute through progressive taxes, transfer payments and supply-side policies such as education, but there are trade-offs with incentives, efficiency and the cost to the budget.

1 · Equity, equality, income and wealth

Equality

An equal distribution of income, wealth or opportunity. It is a positive idea that can be measured.

Equity

Fairness or justice, for example that people in the same situation are treated the same, or that those with greater ability to pay contribute more. It is normative: people disagree on what is fair.

Income

A flow over a period: wages, profit, rent, interest, dividends and benefits.

Wealth

A stock of assets at a point in time: property, savings, shares, pension funds. It is usually more unequally distributed than income.

Causes of inequality include differences in skills and education, ownership of assets and inheritance, wage differences between jobs, unemployment, discrimination, regional differences, and tax and benefit systems. Differences in opportunity (for example, access to education) can matter as much as differences in outcomes.

2 · Measuring inequality

The Lorenz curve plots the cumulative percentage of income against the cumulative percentage of the population, ranked from poorest to richest. The diagonal is the line of perfect equality. The further the curve bows away from it, the greater the inequality.

Gini coefficient = area A ÷ (area A + area B)  ·  0 = perfect equality  ·  1 = perfect inequality

A is the area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve. B is the area under the Lorenz curve. A bigger bow means a higher Gini. Lorenz curves can be drawn before and after tax and benefits to show how far policy redistributes.

Strengths

  • Shows the whole distribution, not just an average
  • Lets countries and years be compared
  • Can show the effect of taxes and benefits

Limitations

  • Different Lorenz curves can give the same Gini
  • Does not show who is poor, or wealth (usually uses income)
  • A large informal economy and hidden income are missed
  • Does not show differences in opportunity or living costs
MeasureMeaning
Absolute povertyIncome too low to buy the basics needed to survive, often shown by a poverty line (such as an income per day)
Relative povertyIncome low compared with others in the same society (for example below 60% of median income)

3 · Taxes and the tax system

  • Direct taxes (on income, wealth, profits) are usually more progressive. Indirect taxes (on spending) are often regressive.
  • A tax-free allowance makes an income tax more progressive.
  • Wealth, capital gains and inheritance taxes target wealth inequality.

4 · Benefits and policies to redistribute

Fiscal policy

  • Progressive income tax, higher taxes on wealth and inheritance
  • Transfer payments: unemployment, disability and child benefits, pensions (money paid with no good or service produced in return)
  • Means-tested benefits (targeted at low incomes) or universal benefits (paid to everyone)
  • Spending on health, education and housing

Supply-side and labour market policies

  • Education, training and apprenticeships, to raise skills and earnings
  • Improving infrastructure in poorer regions, to create jobs
  • A national minimum wage (see syllabus 8.3)
  • Equal opportunity and anti-discrimination laws, a role for trade unions

5 · Trade-offs and evaluation

  • Incentives: very high taxes could reduce the incentive to work, save and take risks, which could lower growth and, in time, tax revenue. High benefits could reduce the incentive to look for work.
  • The poverty trap: when means-tested benefits are withdrawn as earnings rise, and tax is paid too, a worker may gain little or nothing from earning more (a very high effective marginal tax rate).
  • Equity versus efficiency: redistribution is most appropriate when equality is valued more highly than efficiency. Others argue that rich people have a lower marginal utility of income, which justifies redistribution, though comparing utility between people is hard.
  • Cost and fiscal limits: benefits and services must be paid for, and tax avoidance and capital flight limit what a tax can raise.
  • Time lags: education and training take many years to change the distribution.
  • Growth may reduce poverty without reducing inequality. The Kuznets curve suggests inequality may first rise then fall as a country develops, though evidence is mixed.

Comparing countries' Gini coefficients

Countries in northern Europe tend to have low Gini coefficients (around 0.25 to 0.3), partly because of progressive taxes and generous benefits. Some countries in Latin America and southern Africa have much higher ones (above 0.5). The Gini can fall when policy changes, as in several Latin American countries in the 2008 to 2018 period.

Exam use: the May/June 2024 data response used Gini coefficients, literacy ratios and poverty rates. Say what the data show, then warn that a correlation is not a cause.

Income tax bands and allowances

Most countries tax income in bands with higher rates on higher slices, and exempt a first slice of income. A worker moving into a higher band pays the higher rate only on the part above it, so the average rate rises more slowly than the marginal rate.

Exam use: a worked example of a progressive tax. Distinguish marginal from average rates.

Sales taxes and fuel duty

A 10% sales tax takes the same rate from every purchase, but a poorer household spends nearly all its income while a richer one saves more. So the tax takes a larger share of the poorer household's income: it is regressive in effect.

Exam use: indirect taxes are likely to be regressive. A Paper 3 question asked which tax changes increase inequality.

Means-tested and universal benefits

A means-tested benefit goes only to those below an income level, which targets help but can create a poverty trap. A universal benefit, such as a state pension or child benefit paid to all, is simpler and avoids stigma but costs more.

Exam use: compare equity, cost and incentives. A 2020 Paper 3 question asked which combination redistributes most.

A free school meal or a scholarship

Education and nutrition raise the future earning power of children from low-income families, which is a supply-side route to equality of opportunity. The effect takes years to appear and depends on the quality of schooling.

Exam use: the supply-side example asked for in the May/June 2024 8-mark question. Link to long-term earnings and to the time lag.

An inheritance tax

An inheritance tax charges the wealth passed on at death, often with an exempt amount. It aims to limit the build-up of wealth across generations, but wealthy families may find ways to avoid it, and it may weaken the incentive to save.

Exam use: a tax on wealth. Evaluate with avoidance and incentives.

May/June 2024 · 9708/41 and 43 · Q1(a) and (b) 3 + 3 marks (a) Describe what is meant by the Gini coefficient and its possible range of values. (b) Distinguish between absolute poverty and relative poverty.
What the mark scheme wants
  • (a) A measure of income inequality (or, less often, wealth) in a population (1). It ranges from 0 to 1, or 0% to 100% (1), with 0 perfect equality and 1 perfect inequality (1).
  • (b) Absolute poverty: not enough income to buy what is needed to sustain life, shown by a poverty line such as an income per day (World Bank definitions accepted). Relative poverty: income low compared with others in the same society.
KANE

The Gini coefficient is a measure of the inequality of income (or sometimes wealth) among a population. It has a value between 0 and 1, where 0 means perfect equality, with everyone receiving the same, and 1 means perfect inequality, with one person receiving everything.

Absolute poverty is where a person's income is too low to buy the goods needed to survive, often defined by an income below a poverty line, while relative poverty is where income is low compared with the incomes of others in the same society.

The table does not support the conclusion. Brazil has the highest Gini coefficient (0.53) but also the best female to male literacy ratio (1.01), while Pakistan has the lowest Gini (0.30) and the worst ratio (0.67). The countries with higher Gini coefficients, such as Brazil at 4.2% and Mauritius at 10.3%, also have lower percentages of the population below the poverty line than Egypt at 32.5% with a Gini of 0.31. So the data show no clear link, and with only five countries, and no information on relative poverty, it is not possible to say that inequality causes either poor literacy ratios or greater poverty.

May/June 2024 · 9708/41 and 43 · Q1(d) 8 marks Use one example of a supply-side policy and one example of a fiscal policy to assess how a government might be able to achieve greater equality in the distribution of income.
What the mark scheme wants
  • For each policy: a correct example (1), development (2), and a link to equality (1).
  • Fiscal: for example progressive income tax or transfer payments. Supply-side: for example education and training, or infrastructure in poorer regions.
KANE

A fiscal policy example is a more progressive income tax. Higher tax rates on high incomes, with a tax-free allowance for low incomes, mean that richer households pay a larger share of their income, and the extra revenue can be paid to poorer households as transfer payments or spent on public services. This would reduce the gap between the highest and lowest disposable incomes and move the Lorenz curve closer to the line of equality. However, very high rates may reduce the incentive to work and encourage tax avoidance, so the effect could be smaller than expected.

A supply-side policy example is increased spending on education and training, particularly for girls and for poorer regions. Better skills raise the productivity and earnings of those who would otherwise be low paid, which narrows the distribution of earnings and raises growth. The article suggests that unequal access to education between females and males is an obstacle to a more equal distribution of income. The effect is likely to take many years to appear and depends on the quality of the schools and whether jobs are available.

Overall, a government might use both: fiscal policy can reduce inequality quickly, while the supply-side policy may be more lasting but slow.

Practice · 12 marks · Explain what is meant by equity and consider whether a progressive income tax is likely to promote it.
PlanDefine equity (fairness, a value judgement, different from equality). Define progressive tax with a numerical example. Link to ability to pay and the Lorenz curve. Evaluate: incentives, avoidance, cost, whether people agree it is fair. Conclude.
KANE

Equity means fairness, which is a value judgement, so it is different from equality, which means that everyone receives the same income or wealth. A progressive income tax is one in which the average tax rate rises as income rises, so that higher income earners pay a larger share of their income in tax.

A progressive income tax is likely to promote equity if fairness is judged by ability to pay. For example, with a tax-free allowance of $10,000, a rate of 20% on income from $10,000 to $40,000 and 40% above that, a worker earning $30,000 pays $4,000, an average rate of about 13%, and one earning $100,000 pays $30,000, an average rate of 30%. The richer worker therefore contributes a larger share, and the revenue could fund benefits and public services that help low-income households. This is likely to reduce the Gini coefficient and move the Lorenz curve towards the line of equality.

However, people disagree about what is fair. Some would argue it is fairer for everyone to pay the same share of their income, or that people should keep what they earn. High marginal rates could also reduce the incentive to work, save and take risks, and encourage tax avoidance, which could lower revenue and growth. Overall, a progressive tax is likely to promote equity as many people understand it, but how far depends on the rates, on avoidance, and on how the revenue is spent.

Top tips

  • Define equality and equity separately. Equality: the same share or amount. Equity: fairness, a value judgement.
  • Gini: give the range and meaning. 0 is perfect equality, 1 perfect inequality. It is usually about income, so it can miss wealth.
  • Draw the Lorenz curve. Label the axes, the line of equality, areas A and B, and show the curve before and after redistribution.
  • Absolute versus relative poverty: survival (a poverty line) versus compared with others in the same society.
  • Describe tax types by the average rate. Progressive rises, proportional constant, regressive falls with income. Use a tiny numerical example.
  • Use the data carefully. Quote figures from the table, then say what cannot be concluded (correlation is not causation, small sample).
  • For policies, name a fiscal example and a supply-side example, then link each to the distribution of income, with development.
  • Evaluate with incentives, the poverty trap, avoidance, cost and time lags. Say which of equity or efficiency the government values more.
