AS Level Economics · 9708 · Exam technique

AO2 Analysis: How Each Level Is Reached

Exactly what separates Level 1, 2 and 3 analysis: length, depth of reasoning, use of terms, application to context and balance, with examples at every level for the 8 mark, 12 mark and data response questions.

This part in five sentences

AO2 is analysis: using economic concepts, theories and information to show causes, effects and relationships, and explaining the impact of changes in economic variables, in written, numerical and diagrammatic form. In the 12 mark question analysis is marked together with knowledge (AO1) in Table A out of 8, in three levels: Level 1 (1 to 2) is largely descriptive, Level 2 (3 to 5) is generally accurate but limited in development, detail or application, and Level 3 (6 to 8) is developed, detailed, accurate and clearly applied. In the 8 mark part analysis is worth 3 marks and in data response parts it is worth 3 of 4 marks or 4 of 6 marks, each as separate points. What moves an answer up a level is not length for its own sake but how many logical steps are linked, how precisely the economics is used, how clearly it is applied to the context in the question, and (in 12 mark questions) whether both sides or an alternative are analysed. This page shows exactly what each level looks like, with examples at every level.

The marks within a level depend on how fully the descriptors are met: a response at the bottom of a level shows the descriptors only in part; one at the top meets them all and is close to the next level.

3 · What changes from level to level: length, depth, context and balance

Caps written into real mark schemes. Examiners are told, for example: "a one-sided response can only reach Level 2"; for a question on a trade-dependent economy, "answers which do not refer to the heavy dependence on international trade cannot gain more than Level 2"; for a question set in a developing economy, "if the analysis is not in the context of a developing economy, then Level 2 maximum"; and "at least one negative effect in addition to positive effects should be considered to gain lower Level 3". These are the rules that decide the level, so read the question for the who and where as well as the what.

4 · One question, three answers: a 12 mark analysis ladder

Question (Table A part, out of 8): Assess whether fixing a minimum price is likely to be the best policy to reduce the consumption of a demerit good. The mark scheme expects: minimum pricing raises the price and reduces consumption; problems such as inelastic demand, persistent information failure and informal markets; and alternatives such as indirect taxes, education and information, with advantages and disadvantages. (Evaluation is marked separately: see the AO3 review.)

Level 1 answer: 1 to 2 marks (about 45 words)

A minimum price is a price set by the government. Alcohol is a demerit good. A minimum price makes alcohol cost more so people drink less. A tax can also be used to make people drink less.

Why Level 1: relevant and correct, but it is assertion. There is no mechanism (no price above equilibrium, no change in quantity demanded), no mention of elasticity or any limit, no diagram, and the alternative is named but not explained. The reader learns that the student knows the terms, not that they can analyse.

Level 2 answer: 3 to 5 marks (about 120 words)

A minimum price is a legal price floor set above the market price. For alcohol, it raises the price of cheap drinks, so the quantity demanded falls and consumption falls. This should reduce the health problems caused by drinking. However, if people are addicted, demand may be inelastic, so they keep buying and consumption may fall only a little. Sellers also gain more revenue from the higher price. A tax could be used instead. A tax would also raise the price, and the government would get the money.

Why Level 2: the mechanism is stated correctly and one limit (inelastic demand) is given, so the analysis is accurate with some development. But each point stops after one or two steps, there is no diagram, the alternative is undeveloped, and nothing is applied beyond "alcohol". Roughly 4 marks: the top of Level 2 would add a second developed point.

Level 3 answer: 6 to 8 marks (about 300 words)

A minimum price is a legal limit below which a price cannot fall. To be effective for a demerit good such as alcohol it must be set above the equilibrium price. On my diagram the floor is set at Pmin, above P*. At this price, quantity demanded contracts along the demand curve from Q* to Qd while quantity supplied extends to Qs, so consumption falls by Q* − Qd. Because the policy can be aimed at cheap, strong drinks, it targets the products linked to the most harm, which may reduce hospital and other health costs.

How far consumption falls depends on the price elasticity of demand. Alcohol is habit-forming, so demand is likely to be price inelastic: a 20% rise in price might reduce quantity demanded by only 5%, so the fall in consumption is small and heavy drinkers spend more, leaving less for other goods. The higher price also creates excess supply (Qs − Qd), and the extra revenue goes to retailers, not the government. Informal markets may develop, and information failure, the root cause of over-consumption, remains.

An indirect tax would also shift supply left and raise the price, but the revenue goes to the government, which can fund health services, and it applies to all alcohol, not only the cheapest. Education and information tackle the cause by raising awareness of the harm, shifting demand left, but the effect is slow and uncertain. A minimum price is simple and quick to introduce, but its effect is limited when demand is inelastic.

Why Level 3: three developed chains (minimum price, elasticity limit, alternatives), the diagram is used and explained in words, terms are precise (equilibrium, contraction, excess supply, elasticity, indirect tax), the policy is compared with alternatives, and the paragraphs are organised around the question. This would earn 7 or 8; it would drop to 6 if the alternatives were mentioned but not developed.

A Level 3 quality answer that is one-sided is still cappedIf the same student had written three excellent paragraphs only about why minimum prices work, the mark scheme limit for a one-sided response (Level 2) would apply, so the top mark would be 5 however well each point was explained. Balance is part of the level, not an extra.

5 · The 8 mark part: how the 3 analysis marks are awarded

Question: Explain the difference between cost-push and demand-pull inflation and consider which is more likely to occur if there is a depreciation in the exchange rate of a country with few natural resources. The mark scheme gives 3 marks for analysis: a depreciation lowers the price of exports and raises the price of imports; this is analysed for demand-pull (net exports and AD, with the price elasticity of demand for exports and imports) and for cost-push (importance of imports; a country with few natural resources must import them). A maximum of 2 marks if only one type of inflation is considered.

AO2 marksExample answerWhy that many
0 to 1"A depreciation makes imports dearer so prices rise."Correct direction, but it is assertion: no link to AD or costs, no type of inflation, no reference to the context.
2"Cheaper exports and dearer imports raise net exports, so AD shifts right and the price level rises: demand-pull."A complete chain for one type only, so the cap of 2 applies.
3The chain above, and "a country with few natural resources must import raw materials and fuel, so a depreciation raises firms' costs, shifting SRAS left: cost-push."Both types analysed, with the context (few natural resources) used to explain why the cost-push link is strong.

6 · Data response: 2, 4 and 6 marks

2 marks: "Explain one reason why using the CPI to measure inflation may not give an accurate result." The mark scheme gives 1 mark for the reason and 1 for the development: the CPI is based on a basket for the average household (1), but buying patterns change so the basket may no longer be typical (1). A one-line answer ("it is not accurate") earns 0.

6 marks: "Use AD/AS analysis to explain how recent interest rate rises might help reduce the annual rate of inflation." The mark scheme rewards an explanation for demand-pull (up to 4) and for cost-push (up to 4), up to 6 in total, with 2 marks available for evaluative comment that the rise might exacerbate inflation.

MarksExampleWhy
2"Higher interest rates make borrowing dearer, so spending falls and inflation falls."A correct but short chain, with no AD/AS, for one type of inflation only.
4"A rise in interest rates makes borrowing dearer and saving more attractive, so consumption and investment fall and AD shifts left. The price level falls, so demand-pull inflation is reduced. The central bank's higher rate may also raise the exchange rate, cheapening imported inputs and so shifting SRAS right."Two chains (demand-pull, cost-push), with AD and SRAS named at the key steps.
6The answer above, plus: "However, higher borrowing costs raise firms' costs, which could shift SRAS left and worsen cost-push inflation, so the rise may not help."The two analysis chains and a developed evaluative comment, as the mark scheme allows.

7 · The building blocks of a Level 3 paragraph

PartJobExample (minimum price)
PointOne sentence that answers the question using its wording"A minimum price could reduce consumption of alcohol, but only if demand responds."
MechanismThe economic reason, with the concept and the diagram named"The floor is above equilibrium, so quantity demanded contracts from Q* to Qd."
ApplyUse the named good, country or data"Cheap, strong drinks are mainly bought by heavier drinkers, so the policy targets the most harmful consumption."
Consequence chainTwo or three linked steps"Lower consumption means fewer alcohol-related illnesses, so health spending falls."
LinkTie the result back to the question (no judgement yet)"So price alone can reduce consumption of this demerit good."

Linking words that show a chain: because, so, which means, as a result, therefore, this leads to, causing, which in turn. The "so what" test: after each sentence ask "so what happens next?" and write the next step until you reach the effect the question asks about.

8 · Generic or applied?

Generic (could be about anything)Applied (needs this question)
"The tax makes the good more expensive so people buy less.""A 20% tax on cigarettes raises the price, but because smokers are addicted, demand is price inelastic, so quantity falls by less than 20% and total spending on cigarettes rises."
"The depreciation helps exports.""After the depreciation, the country's coffee is cheaper abroad, so export volumes may rise, but if demand for coffee is inelastic, export revenue may fall."
"Unemployment is bad for the economy.""As unemployment rises from 5% to 8%, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises, so the budget deficit widens."

Swap test: could this sentence appear unchanged in an answer about a different market? If yes, add the name of the good, the group affected, the figure from the extract or the feature of the economy that makes the point true here.

10 · Common ways to lose analysis marks

  • Description instead of analysis: defining the policy at length and never saying what it does.
  • Jumping steps: "so unemployment falls" with no reason AD or output changes.
  • Direction errors: shifting the wrong curve or confusing a movement along a curve with a shift.
  • Unused diagrams: drawing a diagram but never referring to it, so it earns little.
  • Answering a different command: explaining when the question says assess, or listing when it says explain a chain.
  • Missing the scope: ignoring "developing economy", "open economy", "always" or "only".
Exam tipAim for two developed chains per side in a 12 mark answer, not five thin points. Use the question's own noun in each topic sentence, refer to your diagram in the text, and finish each paragraph by linking back to the question.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A cap that decides the level

In one series, the mark scheme for a question on terms of trade in an economy heavily dependent on trade said that answers not referring to that dependence could not exceed Level 2, and answers considering only a rise or only a fall could not exceed Level 2.

Exam use: reading the question for the context and covering both sides.

Balance in a one-sided answer

A question on whether free trade is always positive carried the note that a one-sided response cannot gain any evaluation marks, and the Level 2 descriptor says answers 'may be one-sided'. Both sides, each explained, are needed for Level 3.

Exam use: why one-sided analysis stops at Level 2.

Diagrams that earn marks only if used

Mark schemes say 'an accurate diagram should not be expected but would enhance the quality of the analysis' for some questions, and require the diagram to be explained for the top level.

Exam use: drawing, labelling and referring to a diagram.

Point marks in an 8 mark part

For a question on the effect of an interest rate fall, the analysis marks were: explain how lower rates affect saving and borrowing (1), the effect on consumption, investment or net exports (1) and the effect on growth (1).

Exam use: one mark per developed step in the chain.

Key terms alone are not credited

The generic marking principles say examiners do not credit an answer simply for using a key term unless there is evidence it is understood and not used wrongly.

Exam use: explaining, not just naming.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/22 · Q5(a) 8 marks Explain the difference between cost-push and demand-pull inflation and consider which is more likely to occur if there is a depreciation in the exchange rate of a country with few natural resources.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): cost-push inflation is caused by rises in the costs of production (1); demand-pull inflation is caused by increases in AD not matched by increases in AS (1); a clear explanation of the differences (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): a depreciation lowers the price of exports for trading partners and raises the price of imports. Demand-pull: the effect on X - M and AD, with reference to the PED of exports and imports (maximum 2 if only this is considered). Cost-push: the importance of imports, and that a country with few natural resources probably must import its inputs (maximum 2 if only this is considered).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): considers that the country has few natural resources (1) and reaches a justified conclusion on which type of inflation is more likely (1).
PlanDefine both types. Depreciation: AD up (demand-pull) and import costs up (cost-push). Few resources: imported inputs, so cost-push is likely.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Cost-push inflation is caused by a rise in the costs of production, which shifts SRAS to the left. Demand-pull inflation is caused by a rise in aggregate demand that is not matched by a rise in aggregate supply.

A depreciation raises the price of imports and lowers the price of exports for foreigners. Cheaper exports and dearer imports may raise net exports, increasing AD and creating demand-pull pressure, depending on the elasticities of demand. At the same time a country with few natural resources probably has to import raw materials and fuel, so its costs of production rise, which shifts SRAS left and creates cost-push inflation.

Cost-push inflation is probably the more likely outcome, because the country relies on imported inputs, so the rise in costs is likely to be large and immediate. Demand-pull pressure may be smaller if demand for exports is inelastic or if there is spare capacity.

Based on Feb/March 2024 · 9708/22 · Q5(a) 8 marks With the help of an aggregate demand and aggregate supply diagram, explain how a decrease in interest rates could cause economic growth and consider whether economic growth will always result in inflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): a clear understanding of economic growth (1), inflation (1) and interest rates (1); an accurate diagram with labelled axes and equilibrium price level and real GDP (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): using the diagram to explain how a decrease in interest rates affects saving and borrowing (1), which causes an increase in consumption, investment or net exports (1), and so an increase in economic growth or real GDP (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a rise in inflation assumes that the economy is operating at full capacity; growth may be focused on capital intensive industries or its source may be long term (1), and a justified conclusion (1).
PlanDefine growth, inflation, interest rates. Diagram: lower rates raise C and I, AD right, output up. Inflation only if near full capacity.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Economic growth is an increase in real GDP, inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, and the interest rate is the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving. On an AD/AS diagram the equilibrium is where AD meets SRAS at the price level P1 and real GDP Y1.

A lower interest rate reduces the reward for saving and the cost of borrowing, so households borrow and spend more and firms invest more. Consumption and investment are components of AD, so the AD curve shifts to the right and real GDP rises to Y2, which is economic growth.

Growth will not always result in inflation. If the economy has spare capacity, output rises with little change in prices, and if the growth comes from investment that raises capacity, AS also rises. Inflation is more likely when the economy is near full capacity.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/22 · Q3(b) 12 marks Assess whether fixing a minimum price is likely to be the best policy to reduce the consumption of a demerit good.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): use of minimum pricing to raise the price of a demerit good and reduce consumption; problems of minimum pricing, such as inelastic demand, the persistence of information failure and the emergence of informal markets; and alternative policies such as indirect taxes, education and the provision of information, with their advantages and disadvantages, including ease of introduction, time and cost and reception by consumers.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether a minimum price is likely to be the best policy, with a conclusion.
PlanMinimum price: raises price, cuts consumption if demand elastic; inelastic demand, informal markets. Alternatives: tax, education, information. Judge: a combination may be best.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A minimum price raises the price of cheap, strong products, which should reduce quantity demanded, particularly if demand is elastic. But if demand is inelastic, because the good is addictive, consumption may fall little, and the information failure that causes over-consumption remains. Informal markets may develop, and the extra revenue goes to sellers rather than the government. An indirect tax would raise revenue for the state, education and information tackle the cause of the problem but work slowly.

A minimum price is unlikely to be the best policy on its own. It may work for goods with elastic demand, but a combination of price measures and information is likely to be more effective at reducing consumption.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess whether a rise or a fall in the terms of trade will benefit the macroeconomic performance of an open economy that is heavily dependent on international trade.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): better answers examine the effects on the wider economy: employment, price stability, economic growth and the current account, with reference to the dependency on international trade. For a rise: it may lower inflation if raw material import prices are lower; depending on price elasticity of demand it may lead to growth if income from exports increases and boost employment; it may lead to lower growth if it causes a current account deficit. For a fall: exports may be boosted and growth may rise; it may reduce a current account deficit; it may be a sign of a weak economy; if imports are price inelastic it may cause import cost-push inflation if the economy depends on imports. Answers that do not refer to heavy dependence on international trade cannot gain more than Level 2; answers that refer to only a rise or only a fall cannot gain more than Level 2; at least one negative effect in addition to the positives should be considered to reach lower Level 3.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether a rise or a fall benefits the economy, with a conclusion.
PlanRise: lower import costs, higher real income; but dearer exports, current account. Fall: exports up, CA better; but lower real income, import inflation. Link to trade dependence. Judge by elasticities and cause.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

In an open economy that depends heavily on trade, a rise in the terms of trade lowers the price of imported raw materials relative to exports, which eases cost-push inflation and raises real income. If demand for exports is inelastic, export revenue also rises, boosting growth and employment. But if demand is elastic, dearer exports may sell less, and a current account deficit may reduce growth. A fall boosts exports and may narrow a deficit, helping output and jobs in exporting industries, but a heavily trade-dependent economy would face dearer imports, so inflation could rise if import demand is inelastic, and real incomes fall.

Neither is clearly better. A rise benefits an economy that wants lower inflation and higher real incomes, while a fall helps one that needs to boost exports. The result depends on the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports and on the reason for the change, so I would judge that the benefit depends mainly on whether demand for exports is elastic.

Based on Feb/March 2025 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess whether the consequences of free trade are always positive for an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): understanding of free trade; how free trade may benefit an economy, including increased economic growth, a reduction in unemployment, improved public finances and an improved balance of payments; an explanation of the drawbacks, including reduced domestic growth, increases in structural unemployment, over-reliance on other countries for goods not produced domestically and the possibility of a current account deficit. Level 3 considers both positives and negatives and is balanced; Level 2 may be one-sided.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): consideration that fully assesses the consequences and reaches a reasoned conclusion on whether free trade is always positive, which may refer to factor endowments, how open an economy is and its stage of development. A one-sided response cannot gain any marks for evaluation.
PlanBenefits: growth, jobs, revenue, BoP. Drawbacks: structural unemployment, dependence, current account deficit. Judge by factor endowments, openness, stage of development.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Free trade lets an economy specialise where it has a comparative advantage, so output and growth may rise, exporting industries create jobs, higher incomes raise tax revenue, and the balance of payments can improve if exports grow. But industries that cannot compete may close, causing structural unemployment, the economy may become dependent on other countries for goods it no longer makes, and cheaper imports may create a current account deficit.

The consequences are not always positive. Free trade is more likely to benefit an economy with strong factor endowments and a flexible labour market, and one that is already competitive, while a developing economy with young industries may suffer from competition. How open the economy already is, and how quickly it opens up, also matter.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q1(b) 2 marks Explain one reason why using the Consumer Price Index (CPI) to measure the inflation rate in an economy may not produce an accurate result.
What the mark scheme wants
  • The CPI is based on the average basket of products bought by all households (1), but as buying patterns change the basket used may no longer be typical (1).
  • OR the weights and products are usually updated every two or three years to reflect changes in buying patterns (1), but this may not be frequent enough if there is rapid change in the products bought or the proportion of income spent on them (1).
PlanBasket or weights out of date as buying patterns change.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The CPI uses a fixed basket of goods meant to represent what a typical household buys. As buying patterns change, for example with new products, the basket becomes out of date and may no longer be typical of all households, so the measured inflation rate may be inaccurate.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/22 · Q1(d) 6 marks Use aggregate demand and aggregate supply analysis to explain how recent interest rate rises might help a country to reduce its annual rate of inflation and achieve its target.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis: an explanation of how the interest rate rise might help to reduce inflation caused by demand-pull factors (up to 4 marks) and by cost-push factors (up to 4 marks); 2 marks are allowed for evaluative comment that the rise might exacerbate inflation. 6 marks maximum.
PlanDemand-pull: rate rise, C and I fall, AD left, price level falls. Cost-push: higher exchange rate, cheaper imports, SRAS right. Evaluate: higher rates raise firms' costs.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Higher interest rates make borrowing dearer and saving more attractive, so consumption and investment fall and AD shifts left, which reduces the price level and so demand-pull inflation. Higher rates may also raise the exchange rate, making imported raw materials cheaper, which lowers firms' costs, shifting SRAS right and reducing cost-push inflation. However, higher interest rates raise firms' borrowing costs, which could shift SRAS left and worsen cost-push inflation, so the policy might exacerbate inflation.

B · Multiple choice questions (Paper 1)

Choose an answer, then read the reason. Reworded from the paper and question number shown.

Top tips

  • Link at least three steps. Cause, mechanism, effect: one step is Level 1.
  • Name the concept at each step. Elasticity, AD, SRAS, excess supply.
  • Use and explain the diagram. A drawn diagram that is not referred to earns little.
  • Apply to the context. The named good, country or figures.
  • Cover both sides or an alternative. One-sided analysis is capped at Level 2.
  • Two developed chains beat five thin points. Depth, not a list.
  • End each paragraph back at the question. Use its key noun and verb.
