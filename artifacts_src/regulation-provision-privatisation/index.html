Regulation, Public Provision, Privatisation and Nudges

The legal and ownership tools: regulation and competition policy, price caps on natural monopolies, state provision, nationalisation and privatisation, and information and nudges. Animated diagrams built from numbers, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

When prices and taxes are not enough, governments use laws (regulation and competition policy), ownership and provision (nationalisation, privatisation, public goods and merit goods supplied by the state) and information and nudges. A natural monopoly such as a water network cannot easily be made competitive, so the choice is between public ownership and a regulated private firm, often with a price cap. A cap at marginal cost is allocatively efficient but may leave the firm making a loss, while a cap at average cost keeps normal profit. Each approach has costs, so the evaluation compares them.

1 · Regulation and competition policy

Regulation

Rules backed by law: bans, standards, licences, price caps and quality targets. They are quick and certain, but cost money to enforce, give no reward for doing better than the standard and may be influenced by the firms being regulated (regulatory capture).

Competition policy

Laws that review mergers, ban cartels and stop firms abusing a dominant position. The aim is to keep prices low and choice high. Regulators may lack information on costs, and breaking up firms can lose economies of scale.

2 · A price cap on a natural monopoly

A water network has a fixed cost of $800 and a marginal cost of $20 a unit, so average cost falls as output rises. Demand is P = 100 − Q. Left alone, the monopolist sets MR = MC: Q = 40, P = $60 and a supernormal profit of $800.

  • Cap at marginal cost ($20): allocative efficiency, output 80, but the firm loses $800 and needs a subsidy or may leave.
  • Cap at average cost ($31.70): output 68, price just covers cost, so the firm earns only normal profit and consumers get far more than under monopoly.

The Watch and Explore tabs show this. Regulators rarely know the true cost curves, which is why price cap regulation is only as good as its information.

Natural monopoliesIndustries such as rail track, water pipes and electricity grids have very high fixed costs, so one network is cheapest. Competition on the network is not realistic, so the choice is between public ownership and regulated private ownership.

3 · State provision, nationalisation and privatisation

In favourAgainst
State provision / nationalisationPublic goods and merit goods are supplied whatever the profit; control of natural monopolies; decisions may reflect social benefitsRisk of X-inefficiency (costs higher than necessary), losses paid by taxpayers, political interference, may crowd out private supply
PrivatisationCompetition and shareholder scrutiny may raise efficiency; access to private funds; government stops funding losses and receives sale proceedsA state monopoly may become a private monopoly; unprofitable but valuable services may be cut; regulation is still needed

Evaluation turns on whether competition can develop. Privatising a market that can be competitive is likely to improve efficiency; privatising a natural monopoly leaves the monopoly problem, so strong regulation matters.

4 · Information and nudges

People often choose badly because they lack information, put off decisions or follow the default. Governments can respond with information (health warnings, labelling, campaigns) and nudges: changes to how a choice is presented that influence behaviour without banning anything or changing prices. Opt-out organ donation and placing fruit at eye level are nudges; a tax or a ban is not.

  • For nudges: cheap, keep freedom of choice, can work where taxes would be unpopular.
  • Against: the effect may be small or uncertain, may not reach the people who need it, and someone must decide what is the "right" choice.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Privatised utilities and regulators

Water and energy networks in several countries were sold to private firms and regulators were set up to cap prices and set investment targets. The network itself remains a natural monopoly.

Exam use: the 2024 privatisation question. A state monopoly may simply become a private monopoly, so regulation matters.

Opt-out organ donation

Some countries changed from asking people to opt in to assuming consent unless they opt out. The choice is still free, but the default changes behaviour.

Exam use: an example of a nudge. A Paper 3 question asked which action is not a nudge: a tax is not a nudge.

Competition authorities and mergers

Competition authorities in many countries review large mergers and can block them or require firms to sell parts of the business. The aim is to stop a merger that would give the new firm power over prices.

Exam use: competition policy. Evaluate: authorities may lack information, and a merger can bring lower costs as well as higher power.

State-funded health care and education

Many governments provide or fund health care and schooling because they are merit goods with external benefits, and because access based on ability to pay may be thought unfair.

Exam use: state provision. Evaluate: efficiency, cost to taxpayers, quality and whether private providers could be better or worse.

A · 20 mark essays

Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/42 · Q3 20 marks Privatisation is often required by the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank before they are prepared to offer support to countries requiring loans, grants, debt relief and debt cancellation programs. Evaluate the view that privatisation will always improve the allocation of resources in a country.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (14): privatisation as the sale of state-owned corporations; productive, allocative and X-inefficiency; a monopoly model compared with a more competitive structure (monopolistic competition or oligopoly) with correct diagrams; the benefits: greater competition, scrutiny by shareholders, wider access to funds; the government no longer funds losses and can use the receipts to repay debt or fund health and education.
  • Evaluation (6): test against productive, allocative and dynamic efficiency; privatisation may break up a natural monopoly (railways, water pipes), a state monopoly may be replaced by a private monopoly with high barriers to entry, and privatisation often needs an industry regulator.
KANE

Privatisation is the sale of state-owned corporations to the private sector, so that goods and services are provided by private firms. Allocative efficiency occurs where price equals marginal cost and productive efficiency occurs at the minimum of average cost, while X-inefficiency means costs are higher than necessary because of weak pressure to control them.

Privatisation could improve the allocation of resources by increasing competition and efficiency. A state monopoly may suffer from X-inefficiency because it has no competitors and may be protected from losses by the taxpayer. If privatisation leads to several competing firms, so that the market moves from monopoly towards oligopoly or monopolistic competition, the demand curve for each firm becomes more elastic, the price falls closer to marginal cost and average costs are likely to fall as firms try to win customers. This moves the market towards allocative and productive efficiency. Private shareholders also scrutinise managers and can remove them if profits fall, and private firms can borrow without competing with other demands on government spending.

Privatisation may also benefit the government. The sale raises funds that could repay debt or be spent on education and health, and the taxpayer no longer pays for the losses of a loss-making industry, which could reduce the budget deficit. These gains depend on the price the government receives and on whether the sale is rushed.

However, privatisation may not always improve the allocation of resources. Industries such as railways and water pipes are natural monopolies with very high fixed costs, so competition may be unrealistic and a state monopoly could simply be replaced with a private monopoly, which may restrict output and raise price to earn supernormal profit. A regulator would then be needed to cap prices, and regulators may lack information. Private firms might also neglect unprofitable but socially important services, such as rural routes. Overall, privatisation is likely to improve resource allocation where competition can develop and the regulation is strong, but it is unlikely to do so in every case, especially for natural monopolies, so the view that it will always improve allocation is too strong.

B · A 13 mark question

Feb/March 2020 · 9708/42 · Q4(b) 13 marks Consider the view that in the absence of government regulation there are no limits on the ability of a monopolist to charge whatever price it wishes.
What the mark scheme wants
  • The usual aim of a monopoly is maximum profit, but even then the price is limited by costs and demand. Market control and price discrimination could make price higher than in perfect competition.
  • Not all monopolies are free of potential competition: a contestable market limits the price.
  • Level 4 (9 to 13): a sound discussion with a reasoned conclusion.
KANE

A monopolist is the sole supplier in a market and is usually assumed to aim for profit maximisation, setting output where MR equals MC and charging the price shown on the demand curve at that output. Government regulation might include price caps, competition law and rules on mergers.

Without regulation a monopolist has considerable power over price, but it is not unlimited. The price is restricted by the demand curve: if the monopolist raises the price too much, quantity demanded falls, and above the profit-maximising price its profit falls. Costs also set a floor, because it must cover average cost to stay in business. With barriers to entry, market control and perhaps price discrimination, the price could well be higher than under perfect competition, and the firm may earn supernormal profit in the long run.

However, not every monopoly is protected by high barriers. If the market is contestable, the threat of new entrants could force the firm to charge a lower price, for example by limit pricing. Substitutes, even if imperfect, also limit price, and in some markets consumers can respond by buying less or moving to other products. Overall, a monopolist is likely to be able to charge more than a competitive firm, but without regulation the limits come from demand, costs and the threat of entry, so the claim that there are no limits is too strong.

Top tips

  • Name the type of policy. Regulation, ownership and provision, or information and nudges. Then link it to the failure it fits.
  • For privatisation, split the industry: can competition develop, or is it a natural monopoly? Mention regulators.
  • For a price cap, compare the two levels. At marginal cost is efficient but loss-making; at average cost keeps normal profit.
  • Know what a nudge is. It changes the way choices are presented (defaults, placement) and leaves people free. A tax or a ban is not a nudge.
  • Use the merit good link for state provision: external benefits, under-consumption, equity, and the risk of X-inefficiency.
  • Test strong words: "all", "always", "no limits". Weigh both sides and conclude.
