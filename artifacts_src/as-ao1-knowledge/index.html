AO1 Knowledge and Understanding: How the Marks Are Earned

What earns AO1 marks in Paper 1 and Paper 2: definitions, formulae, diagrams and calculations, how the three marks in an 8 mark part are awarded, and what Level 1, 2 and 3 knowledge looks like in a 12 mark answer.

This part in five sentences

AO1 is knowledge and understanding: showing knowledge of syllabus content (facts, formulae and definitions), demonstrating understanding with appropriate explanations and examples, and applying knowledge and understanding to economic information in written, numerical and diagrammatic form. In the 8 mark part of Paper 2 it is worth 3 marks (for papers up to 2022, 2 marks), in the 12 mark part it is combined with analysis in Table A out of 8, in the short data response parts it earns the marks for definitions, comparisons and calculations, and in Paper 1 it is tested in every question. Marks are earned for precise definitions, correct formulae, accurately drawn and labelled diagrams, correct calculations and explanations that show a concept is understood, and the generic marking principles say examiners do not credit a key term simply for being used unless there is evidence that it is understood. This page shows what earns each AO1 mark and what separates a Level 1, 2 and 3 knowledge base in a 12 mark answer.

2 · What earns the three AO1 marks in an 8 mark part

The mark scheme for the 8 mark part lists the AO1 points. They are almost always a definition, a formula or distinction, and a diagram or a developed meaning. Real examples:

QuestionThe three AO1 marks (from the mark scheme)

The pattern: every item in the question ("with the help of a formula", "explain the difference between", "with a diagram") tells you what AO1 marks are available. If you leave one out, you lose a mark that no amount of analysis will replace.

TermNot enough (no credit)PartialFull credit

Opportunity cost"The cost of something.""What you give up.""The value of the next best alternative that is given up when a choice is made."
Merit good"A good that is good for you.""A good that is under-consumed.""A good that is more beneficial to consumers than they realise, so it is under-consumed and under-provided by the free market, such as education."

What full credit needs: the key idea (what it measures or is), the qualifier (the direction, the units or the condition) and, where the question allows, a short example or the formula. Repeating the term in the definition ("elastic demand is demand that is elastic") earns nothing.

4 · AO1 in Table A: Levels 1, 2 and 3 of knowledge

In the 12 mark question the knowledge and understanding in Table A is judged on how many relevant concepts, how accurately and how well explained. Take the question Assess whether the consequences of free trade are always positive for an economy.

Level 1 knowledge: 1 to 2 marks (about 40 words)

Free trade is when countries trade. It means goods are cheaper. Some firms might lose out.

Why Level 1: a small number of relevant points, each only named; the definition is imprecise (trade, not trade without barriers); no concept is explained and nothing supports the points.

Level 2 knowledge: 3 to 5 marks (about 100 words)

Free trade is trade between countries without barriers such as tariffs. It lets countries specialise in what they are good at, so output rises and consumers get cheaper goods and more choice. Free trade can lead to unemployment in industries that cannot compete. Some countries may depend on a few exports.

Why Level 2: several relevant concepts (specialisation, choice, unemployment, dependence), generally accurate, but they are over-generalised ("what they are good at" is not comparative advantage; no explanation of why unemployment is structural; no example).

Level 3 knowledge: 6 to 8 marks (about 220 words, with the analysis)

Free trade is trade between countries without barriers such as tariffs and quotas. It allows countries to specialise in goods in which they have a comparative advantage, meaning a lower opportunity cost, so that total output rises and each country can consume beyond its production possibility curve. Consumers gain a wider choice and lower prices, firms gain economies of scale from larger markets, and competition raises efficiency. The costs include structural unemployment when industries decline because workers cannot easily change occupation, over-specialisation and dependence on a few commodities whose prices fall, as with countries that rely on one crop, and a possible current account deficit if imports rise faster than exports. Whether free trade is positive depends on factor endowments, how open the economy already is, and its stage of development...

Why Level 3: detailed, accurate knowledge of the concepts the question needs (comparative advantage, opportunity cost, structural unemployment, current account), each with an explanation and an example. The knowledge is relevant to this question, not a dump of everything known about trade.

7 · Calculations: how the marks go

  • The mark scheme shows the steps, the mark for each step and the mark for the answer. Write the formula, substitute the figures, then give the answer with the unit.
  • Unless the question says working is needed, a correct final answer earns full marks even without working; but if the answer is wrong, working earns the method marks.
  • The own figure rule: if you use an incorrect figure from earlier in the question, you can still earn the later marks for a correct method.
  • Check percentage or percentage point, the sign (a deficit is negative) and whether to round as instructed.

8 · Common ways to lose AO1 marks

  • Confusing terms: deficit and debt, GDP and GNI, flow and stock, movement along and shift of a curve, budget deficit and current account deficit, real and nominal.
  • Imprecise definitions: "how much demand changes" for elasticity, with no mention of percentages or price.
  • Leaving out what the question asked for: no formula when it says "with the help of a formula", no diagram when it says so.
  • Unlabelled or wrong diagrams: missing axes, a demand curve that slopes the wrong way, a shift in the wrong direction.
  • Knowledge dumping: writing everything known about a topic, which does not earn Level 3 unless it answers the question.
  • Using a key term without showing understanding.
Exam tipUnderline every "with the help of", "define" and "explain the difference" in the question: each is an AO1 mark you can bank. Learn the definitions word for word, and write the formula first in any calculation.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

An explicit comparison

A 2 mark data part asked students to compare changes in two series. The mark scheme said marks were for an explicit comparison, not for describing each in turn: 'the inflation rate rose then fell while the interest rate rose continuously'.

Exam use: comparing, not describing twice.

Real GDP in two marks

Asked what is meant by real GDP, the answer needs GDP and the adjustment for inflation (or prices). 'GDP in real terms' is not enough.

Exam use: a precise definition with the qualifier.

The formula mark

In several 8 mark parts a mark is given for the correct formula, such as percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percentage change in price. Writing 'quantity over price' loses it.

Exam use: learning formulae exactly.

Distinguish questions

A 'distinguish between a budget deficit and a current account deficit' question gave one mark for each side: government spending exceeds revenue, and payments to the rest of the world exceed receipts.

Exam use: one mark per term, so define both.

A calculation with steps

An extract question on GNI and NNI awards a mark for adding net primary income and a mark for subtracting depreciation; a student who writes the steps can earn marks even if the final number is wrong.

Exam use: showing working for method marks.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/24 · Q2(a) 8 marks With the help of a formula, explain the meaning of price elasticity of demand and consider the significance to a firm of having price elastic or price inelastic demand for its product.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): it measures the responsiveness of changes in demand to changes in price (1); the correct formula (1); the meaning of price elastic and inelastic in terms of the percentage or proportionate response (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): the impact of rising or falling prices on the demand for a firm's products when PED is inelastic (1) and when it is elastic (1), with both rising and falling prices considered.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a valid judgement on the importance of the distinction between elastic and inelastic demand for the effect of price changes on sales, with a mark for assessing what a firm should do to influence demand or spending.
PlanFormula and meaning. Elastic: price cut raises revenue. Inelastic: price rise raises revenue. Judge: the firm should use elasticity, but other factors matter.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Price elasticity of demand measures how responsive the quantity demanded is to a price change. It equals the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. Demand is elastic if the quantity changes by a greater percentage than price, and inelastic if by a smaller percentage.

If demand is elastic, a fall in price may raise total revenue because the rise in quantity outweighs the lower price, while a rise in price may reduce revenue. If demand is inelastic, a rise in price may raise revenue because quantity falls by less than price rises, while a price cut would reduce revenue.

Knowing whether demand is elastic or inelastic is likely to be very useful in setting prices, as it suggests whether to raise or cut prices to increase revenue. But the estimate may be inaccurate and other factors, such as competitors' responses, could change the outcome.

Based on Feb/March 2024 · 9708/22 · Q3(a) 8 marks With the help of a formula, explain the meaning of income elasticity of demand and consider the extent to which a rise in income will increase the consumption of all goods and services.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): an accurate definition of YED (1), an accurate formula (1), and the difference between normal and inferior goods (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): normal goods have a positive value (1); uses a diagram (an upward-sloping income demand curve) or the formula to show why a rise in income increases consumption of such goods (1), justified with an example (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): if the good is inferior, demand falls when income rises (1) as consumers switch to normal or luxury goods; the second mark is for a justified conclusion.
PlanDefine and formula. Normal: positive, demand rises. Inferior: negative, demand falls. Conclude: not all goods rise.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Income elasticity of demand measures how responsive demand is to a change in income. It equals the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in income. Normal goods have a positive YED and inferior goods a negative one.

For a normal good such as foreign holidays, a rise in income raises demand, because consumers can afford more of them, so a positive YED shows that consumption increases as incomes rise.

Not all goods will rise. For an inferior good such as a basic own-brand product, a rise in income may lead consumers to switch to better goods, so consumption falls. Overall, a rise in income increases the consumption of normal goods but not of all goods and services.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/21 · Q3(a) 8 marks With the help of a formula, explain the meaning of cross elasticity of demand and consider which determinants are most important in establishing the size and sign of its coefficient.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): what cross elasticity of demand measures (1), the formula (1), and the distinction between positive and negative XED (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): why XED can be positive, for substitutes (1); why it can be negative, for complements (1); and the determinants of its magnitude, where the closer the relationship between two products the greater the size of XED (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a valid judgement on the relative importance of the determinants of the size and sign of XED (1) and a conclusion (1).
PlanFormula and meaning. Positive: substitutes. Negative: complements. Size: closeness of the relationship. Judge: closeness of relationship matters most for size; the nature of the link for sign.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Cross elasticity of demand measures how responsive the demand for one good is to a change in the price of another. It equals the percentage change in quantity demanded of good X divided by the percentage change in the price of good Y. It is positive for substitutes and negative for complements.

XED is positive when goods are substitutes, because a higher price for Y makes consumers switch to X, and negative for complements, because a higher price for Y lowers demand for the good used with it. The closer the relationship, whether substitutes or complements, the larger the size of the coefficient.

The nature of the relationship, whether the goods are used together or in place of each other, is likely to determine the sign, while how close the substitutes are, and how the market is defined, may be most important for the size. A narrowly defined market is likely to give a larger coefficient.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/23 · Q5(a) 8 marks Explain two possible causes of a change in the terms of trade in an economy and consider which of these causes is likely to be more important for low-income countries.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): what is meant by the terms of trade, the ratio of the export price index to the import price index (1); that it measures the number of exports that have to be sold to buy a given number of imports (1); that the terms of trade improve when the ratio increases and deteriorate when it falls (1).
  • Analysis (up to 3): two main causes of changes, for example a rise in export prices and a fall in import prices, often the result of changes in demand for exports and imports (1); low-income countries are more likely to export primary goods and import manufactured goods, and the demand for primary goods may be lower than for manufactures when incomes rise, so import prices may fall and the terms of trade deteriorate (1); but commodity prices such as oil may have inelastic demand and prices may rise, improving the terms of trade (1).
  • Evaluation (up to 2): considers the significance of changes in the terms of trade for low-income countries that produce foodstuffs compared with those that export minerals in high demand, and a justified conclusion.
PlanToT formula. Two causes: export price changes (world demand), import price changes (oil). Low-income: primary goods, low income elasticity. Judge which matters more.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The terms of trade are the index of export prices divided by the index of import prices, times 100. It shows how many exports must be sold to buy a given amount of imports. A rise in the index is an improvement and a fall is a deterioration.

One cause is a change in world demand for the country's exports, which changes export prices. Another is a change in the price of imports, such as oil. Low-income countries typically export primary goods and import manufactures. As world incomes rise, demand for primary goods grows slowly while demand for manufactures grows faster, so export prices may fall relative to import prices and the terms of trade may deteriorate.

For a low-income country exporting food, the export-price cause is probably more important because demand for these goods is slow-growing and prices volatile. A country exporting a mineral in high demand could see its terms of trade improve, so the importance depends on what it exports.

What the mark scheme wants

An ad valorem tax is a percentage of the price, so the tax rises with the price, as VAT does. A specific tax is a fixed sum on each unit sold, such as a duty of a set amount per litre of fuel.

What the mark scheme wants

  • Real GDP is GDP corrected for or taking account of the effects of inflation or price changes (1). Real GDP is nominal GDP less the rate of inflation (2). Maximum 2 marks.

GDP is the total value of goods and services produced in an economy in a year. Real GDP is GDP adjusted for inflation, so it shows the change in output rather than in prices.

Based on Oct/Nov 2024 · 9708/21 · Q1(b) 2 marks Explain what is meant by 'the real interest rate will be negative' in an economy where the nominal interest rate is 40% and the inflation rate is 50%.
What the mark scheme wants
  • A negative real interest rate is when the interest rate is lower than the inflation rate (1).
  • The real interest rate is calculated as the nominal rate minus inflation, so here it is 40 − 50 = −10% (1). The original question asked for the figure for Argentina in January 2022 or the method of calculation.
PlanReal rate = nominal − inflation. Negative when inflation exceeds the interest rate. Here −10%.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

The real interest rate is the nominal interest rate minus inflation. It is negative when inflation is higher than the interest rate, so 40% − 50% gives −10%. This means borrowers gain in real terms because the value of their debt falls, and savers lose.

Based on May/June 2020 · 9708/22 · Q1(c)(i) 2 marks Distinguish between a budget deficit and a current account deficit.
What the mark scheme wants
  • A budget deficit means that government expenditure exceeds government revenue (1).
  • A current account deficit means that exports, or inflows, are less than imports, or outflows, of current account items such as goods and services (1).
PlanBudget deficit: government spending above revenue. Current account deficit: payments abroad above receipts.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A budget deficit is where the government spends more than it receives in revenue in a year. A current account deficit is where a country's payments to the rest of the world for goods, services and income are greater than the receipts it gets from abroad.

Top tips

  • Learn definitions word for word. Key idea, qualifier, example.
  • Write the formula first. In words or symbols, exactly.
  • Do what 'with the help of' asks. Each phrase is a mark.
  • Label every diagram. Axes, curves, equilibrium, shift.
  • Show working. Method marks and the own figure rule.
  • Explain, don't just name. A key term alone earns nothing.
  • Match knowledge to the question. Relevant concepts, not everything you know.
