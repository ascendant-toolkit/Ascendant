Arguments for and Against Protectionism

6.2.3: the case for protecting infant, declining and strategic industries, the case against, and how to judge between them. Animated chains, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Governments use protectionism for several reasons. The main arguments for are to protect infant (sunrise) industries while they grow to a size where they can compete, to give declining (sunset) industries time to adjust and for workers to find new jobs, to protect strategic industries (such as defence or food) that matter for national security, to prevent dumping (selling below cost in another country), to reduce a current account deficit and to raise government revenue through tariffs. The main arguments against (and for free trade) are that protection reduces specialisation and so output, misallocates resources, raises prices and limits consumer choice, may protect inefficient firms, invites retaliation and lowers living standards. Whether the case for protection is stronger depends on the industry, the country, the size of the effects and how long the protection lasts.

1 · Arguments for protectionism

ArgumentThe ideaThe weakness
Infant industryA new industry cannot yet get economies of scale, so temporary protection lets it growIt may never become efficient, and the protection can be hard to remove
Declining industrySlows the decline so that workers and capital can move to other uses graduallyDelays necessary change and keeps resources in a failing industry
Strategic industryKeeps control of industries vital for security, such as defence, energy or food"Strategic" can be stretched to cover almost any industry
Anti-dumpingPrevents foreign firms selling below cost to drive out domestic rivalsHard to prove dumping; consumers lose cheap goods
Current account deficitCutting imports can reduce a deficitRetaliation and inelastic demand for imports can cancel the effect
Government revenueTariffs raise tax income, especially where other taxes are hard to collectThe revenue falls if the tariff is high enough to cut imports sharply

2 · Arguments against protectionism

  • Lower specialisation and output: the gains from comparative advantage are reduced, so world output and growth are lower.
  • Misallocation of resources: resources stay in less efficient domestic industries.
  • Higher prices and less choice for consumers, which can lower living standards.
  • Reduced competition: protected firms may have less incentive to cut costs or innovate (X-inefficiency).
  • Retaliation and trade wars: exporters in the protecting country may lose markets.
  • Higher costs for firms that use imported inputs.
Exam tipFor "assess" questions, explain both sides in context (a developing economy, a particular industry), then judge: the case for protection is strongest for temporary support of a specific industry, and weaker as a long-term, wide policy. Use "may" and "could".

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A new car maker

A country with no car industry protects a start-up manufacturer with a tariff while it builds scale. If its costs fall, it may compete without help; if they do not, consumers may be paying more for years.

Exam use: the infant industry argument and its risk.

Farming and food security

Many governments support domestic farming to protect food supply in a crisis. Critics say the support raises prices and protects less efficient farms.

Exam use: the strategic industry argument.

Cheap steel imports

Domestic producers claim foreign steel is sold below cost. An anti-dumping duty may protect jobs but raises costs for car and construction firms.

Exam use: dumping and its trade-offs.

A shrinking coal industry

A government slows the closure of mines to give workers time to retrain. Critics say it delays change and costs taxpayers.

Exam use: the declining industry argument.

Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/21 · Q5(b) 12 marks Assess whether the arguments for protectionism in the context of international trade are always stronger than the arguments against.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): explanation of the arguments for protectionism, such as infant and sunrise industries, declining and sunset industries, strategic industries, preventing dumping, reducing a current account deficit and raising revenue (up to 4); the arguments against, such as greater specialisation and output from free trade, more efficient resource allocation, wider consumer choice and higher living standards (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): considers the issues with protectionism and the relative strength of the arguments, and concludes which approach could be better for particular economic situations.
PlanFor: infant, declining, strategic, dumping, deficit, revenue. Against: lost specialisation, misallocation, prices, choice. Judge: depends on the situation.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Protectionism may be justified to protect an infant industry that needs time to reach economies of scale, to slow the decline of a sunset industry so that workers can retrain, to protect strategic industries and to stop dumping. But protection reduces the gains from specialisation, keeps resources in less efficient firms, raises prices and reduces consumer choice, so living standards could fall.

The arguments for protection are not always stronger. They are most convincing for temporary help to a specific industry or for security, and least convincing as a broad long-term policy that could invite retaliation. Which side is stronger may depend on the industry, the length of protection and the country's circumstances.

Based on May/June 2024 · 9708/22 · Q5(b) 12 marks Assess whether free trade is always better than a policy of protectionism for a developing economy that wishes to trade internationally.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the advantages and disadvantages of free trade and of protectionism, both in the context of a developing economy. Advantages of free trade may include efficient allocation from specialisation, use of different factor endowments, competitive pressure on costs and prices, and economies of scale and choice; disadvantages may include over-exploitation of scarce resources, closure of domestic industries and unemployment, and dumping or unfair competition. Maximum Level 2 if not in the context of a developing economy.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether free trade is always better, with a conclusion.
PlanFree trade: specialisation, competition, scale. Risks: industries close, dumping, resource use. Protection: infant industries. Judge: depends on the economy and time.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

For a developing economy, free trade may allow it to use its factor endowments, such as cheap labour or natural resources, and to gain from specialisation. Competition from abroad could push domestic firms to lower costs and consumers may get lower prices and more choice. But young industries may be unable to compete and could close, raising unemployment, and dumping by foreign firms may be a risk. Protection could give infant industries time to grow, but could raise prices, reduce competition and lead to inefficient firms.

Free trade is not always better. It may be more beneficial for developing economies with a clear comparative advantage, while temporary and targeted protection could help young industries. The best policy may be a mix that changes as the economy develops.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess whether the potential benefits of free trade always outweigh the arguments for protectionism.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the potential benefits of free trade, such as an increase in world output, more efficient allocation through specialisation, lower costs, wider choice, economic growth and improved living standards (up to 4); the arguments for protectionism, such as the infant industry argument, declining or sunset industries, strategic industries, a current account deficit and anti-dumping (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): balanced consideration of alternatives, explaining which is stronger in particular circumstances, with a conclusion.
PlanFree trade: output, efficiency, cost, choice, growth. Protection: infant, sunset, strategic, deficit, dumping. Judge: benefits may usually outweigh, but not always.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Free trade could raise world output because countries specialise where they have a comparative advantage. Resources are allocated more efficiently, costs may fall and consumers may enjoy a wider choice and higher living standards. But infant industries may need temporary protection to grow, sunset industries may need time to adjust, strategic industries may matter for security and a deficit could be reduced by cutting imports. Anti-dumping measures may stop unfair competition.

The benefits of free trade may often outweigh the case for protection, especially over a long time, but not always. Protection can be justified when it is temporary, targeted and unlikely to provoke retaliation, such as support for a genuinely strategic industry.

Top tips

  • Give both sides. Arguments for and against, in context.
  • Name the arguments. Infant, sunset, strategic, dumping, deficit, revenue.
  • Explain, don't list. One or two developed points, not a long list.
  • Use the context. A developing economy or a named industry.
  • Evaluate. Temporary versus permanent, targeted versus broad.
  • Mention retaliation. Exporters may lose markets.
  • Conclude. Which side is stronger, and when.
