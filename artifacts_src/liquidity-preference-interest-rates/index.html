Liquidity Preference and Interest Rate Determination

9.4.7 to 9.4.8: the motives for holding money, the Keynesian money market and the liquidity trap, and the loanable funds theory of the interest rate. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

In Keynes's liquidity preference theory, people demand money for three motives: transactions, precautionary (together called active balances, which depend mainly on income) and speculative (idle balances, which depend on the interest rate, because the interest rate is the reward for giving up liquidity). The interest rate is set where the demand for money (the liquidity preference curve) meets the money supply. At a very low rate, people prefer to hold money rather than bonds, so the demand curve becomes flat: a liquidity trap, where more money does not lower rates further. The loanable funds theory instead says the interest rate is the price that balances saving (the supply of loanable funds) with borrowing for investment (the demand).

The speculative motive and the bond price

  • Bond price and the interest rate move in opposite directions. When the interest rate falls, bond prices rise.
  • At a high interest rate bond prices are low and people expect them to rise: they buy bonds and hold little idle money.
  • At a low interest rate bond prices are high and people expect them to fall (rates will rise): they sell bonds and hold more idle money.
  • So the demand for money (liquidity preference, LP) slopes downward: the lower the interest rate, the more money people want to hold. In practice the opportunity cost of holding money is the interest forgone.

Shifts of the LP curve and the money supply

  • LP shifts right when income or the price level rises, when payments become less frequent, when uncertainty rises or when credit is harder to obtain.
  • LP shifts left when payments become more frequent, when credit cards and cash machines allow smaller balances.
  • The money supply (MS) is a vertical line, set by the central bank and banks.

2 · Interest rate determination: the Keynesian theory (9.4.8)

  • The equilibrium interest rate is where LP meets MS. If the rate is above equilibrium, people hold less money than is supplied, so they buy bonds, pushing bond prices up and the interest rate down. If below, the reverse.
  • An increase in the money supply (central bank buys bonds, QE) shifts MS right, so the interest rate falls; a decrease raises it.
  • A rise in income shifts LP right and raises the interest rate.
  • The transmission to the economy: lower interest rates raise investment (depending on the marginal efficiency of capital) and consumption, raising AD. The effect is weaker if investment is interest inelastic.

The liquidity trap

At a very low interest rate (near zero), bond prices are very high and people expect them to fall, so they hold any extra money rather than buying bonds. The LP curve becomes horizontal. An increase in the money supply then does not reduce the interest rate further, so monetary policy loses its effect. This is a feature of deep recessions: low inflation (or deflation), low interest rates and low growth. The Keynesian remedy is fiscal policy.

3 · The loanable funds theory

  • The interest rate is the price of loanable funds, set by the supply of funds (saving by households, firms, governments and foreign lenders) and the demand for funds (borrowing for investment, consumption and government deficits).
  • A rise in saving shifts supply right, lowering the interest rate and raising investment. A rise in investment demand (business confidence, higher expected profit) shifts demand right, raising the rate.
  • The theory focuses on real flows of saving and investment, and the interest rate brings saving and investment into balance.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Japan's decades of near-zero rates

From the late 1990s the Bank of Japan held interest rates close to zero and later bought bonds on a huge scale. Money supply rose but lending, spending and inflation stayed weak, which is often cited as an example of a liquidity trap.

Exam use: the liquidity trap and the limits of monetary policy. Link to fiscal policy as a remedy and to the Keynesian argument.

The US and UK after 2008

Central banks cut rates to 0.1% to 0.5% and then used quantitative easing. Yields on bonds fell to record lows. Even so, business investment recovered slowly because firms lacked confidence.

Exam use: low interest rates may not raise investment if the marginal efficiency of capital is low.

Rising rates in 2022

As inflation rose, central banks raised rates sharply from near zero. Bond prices fell, and some banks holding long-dated bonds suffered losses, showing the inverse link between interest rates and bond prices.

Exam use: the inverse relationship between bond prices and interest rates, and the speculative motive.

Global savings glut

Before 2008, high saving in China and oil exporters increased the supply of loanable funds worldwide. This helped to keep real interest rates low, and cheap credit may have fuelled borrowing and house prices.

Exam use: an application of the loanable funds theory: a rise in saving lowers the interest rate.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/43 · Q5(b) 13 marks Keynes argued that the rate of interest will not ensure that saving equals investment, because they are done by different people for different reasons. Discuss this view.
What the mark scheme wants
  • The classical (loanable funds) view: a rise in saving lowers the interest rate, which raises investment until saving equals investment.
  • Keynes: savers (households, mainly depending on income) and investors (firms, depending on expected profit or the marginal efficiency of capital) are different people with different motives, and the interest rate is determined in the money market, not by saving and investment.
  • Adjustment through income: if saving exceeds investment, income and output fall (the paradox of thrift) until saving is reduced to match investment.
  • Evaluation: in the long run, and when the economy is at full employment, the loanable funds view may be a good guide; in a recession the Keynesian view is more relevant.

Top tips

  • Draw the money market. Interest rate on the vertical axis, quantity of money on the horizontal axis, LP sloping down, MS vertical.
  • Name the three motives. Transactions, precautionary (income) and speculative (interest rate).
  • Use the bond price link. Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions. It explains the speculative motive.
  • Shift or move along? Money supply change shifts MS. Income change shifts LP.
  • Draw the horizontal LP for the trap. Then say monetary policy loses effect and fiscal policy is needed.
  • Contrast the two theories. Liquidity preference: money market, a stock. Loanable funds: saving and investment, flows.
  • Evaluate with practice. In reality central banks set the policy rate directly, and expectations matter.
