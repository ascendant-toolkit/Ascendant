Relationships Between Markets

2.4.3: joint demand, alternative demand, derived demand and joint supply, with cross elasticity of demand. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Markets are linked, so a change in one market can shift a curve in another. Joint demand (complements) means goods are used together, so a rise in the price of one lowers demand for the other; alternative demand (substitutes) means goods can replace each other, so a rise in the price of one raises demand for the other. Derived demand means demand for an input exists because of demand for the final product it helps make, and joint supply means producing more of one good automatically produces more of another (a by-product). The cross elasticity of demand (XED) measures the strength and sign of the link: positive for substitutes, negative for complements, zero for unrelated goods.

  • XED = % change in quantity demanded of Y ÷ % change in price of X. Example: price of rice rises 10% and demand for bread rises 14%: XED = +1.4, strong substitutes. Price of butter rises 10% and demand for bread falls 7%: XED = −0.7, weaker complements.
  • A value of zero means the goods are unrelated.

2 · Derived demand

  • Demand for steel is derived from demand for cars, buildings and appliances. Demand for labour is derived from demand for what workers make.
  • A rise in demand for the final product raises demand for the inputs, so their prices and quantities tend to rise.
  • The more inelastic the supply of the input, the more its price rises.

3 · Joint supply

  • Goods are in joint supply when producing one necessarily produces another: beef and leather, butter and buttermilk, petrol and diesel from crude oil.
  • A rise in demand for beef raises the price of beef, so farmers supply more beef and more leather. The supply of leather shifts right, so leather becomes cheaper and its quantity rises.
  • In the market for the product whose demand changed, there is a movement along supply; in the by-product's market there is a shift of supply.
  • Joint supply goods are also in joint demand only if consumers buy them together, which is usually not the case.
Exam tipAlways say which curve shifts in the second market and why. For XED, give the formula, the sign and what the size means.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Printers and ink

Printers are cheap but ink cartridges are dear. Printers and ink are complements: if the price of ink rises, fewer printers are bought. Firms may sell the printer at a low price to earn profit from the ink, which is why demand for one affects the other.

Exam use: joint demand and the negative cross elasticity.

Bus and train

A rise in train fares sends some commuters to buses. Demand for buses shifts right, so the bus company may raise fares. The positive cross elasticity between them shows how close the substitutes are.

Exam use: alternative demand and substitutes.

Steel and cars

When car sales fall, car makers order less steel. The fall in demand for the input is derived from the fall in demand for the final product, so steel prices and output both tend to fall.

Exam use: derived demand.

Beef and leather

More demand for beef raises beef output, which produces more hides. The supply of leather rises, so the price of leather falls without any change in demand for leather.

Exam use: joint supply and the movement along one curve with a shift in the other.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/21 · Q3(a) 8 marks Cross elasticity of demand for bread with respect to the price of rice is +1.4 while the cross elasticity of demand for bread with respect to the price of butter is −0.7. Explain why the cross elasticity of demand figures for bread differ.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): what is meant by XED (1) and the formula (1).
  • Application (up to 6): bread and rice as substitutes with a positive XED (1 each for the goods as substitutes and applying it to bread and rice), and a strong relationship (1); bread and butter as complements with a negative XED (1 each), and a weak relationship (1).
PlanDefine XED and give the formula. Rice: positive, substitute, strong. Butter: negative, complement, weak.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Cross elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of one good to a change in the price of another: the percentage change in quantity demanded of good A divided by the percentage change in the price of good B.

The figure for rice is positive because rice and bread are substitutes: if rice becomes dearer, consumers buy more bread. A value of 1.4 means demand for bread is quite responsive, so they are close substitutes. The figure for butter is negative because bread and butter are complements, bought together: if butter becomes dearer, people buy less bread. The value of 0.7 is smaller in size, so the link is weaker.

PlanDefine and give the formula. Positive: substitutes. Negative: complements. Zero: unrelated. Explain each.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Cross elasticity of demand measures how far the quantity demanded of one good responds to a change in the price of another, calculated as the percentage change in quantity demanded of A divided by the percentage change in the price of B.

A positive value shows the goods are substitutes, because a rise in the price of B makes consumers switch to A. A negative value shows they are complements, because a rise in the price of B lowers demand for the good used with it. A value of zero means the quantity demanded of A does not respond to the price of B, so the goods are unrelated.

Based on Feb/March 2021 · 9708/22 · Q3(a) 8 marks Explain how you would use the concept of cross-elasticity of demand to measure the impact on the demand for cars when there is a rise in the price of fuel for cars and when there is a rise in the price of public transport.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 2): what XED measures (1) and the formula (1).
  • Application (up to 3): cars and fuel are complements so the coefficient is negative (1), explained (1), and the impact is greater the closer the relationship (1).
  • Application (up to 3): cars and public transport are substitutes so the coefficient is positive (1), explained (1), and the impact is greater the closer the relationship (1).
PlanFormula. Fuel: complement, negative, demand for cars falls. Public transport: substitute, positive, demand for cars rises.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Cross elasticity of demand is the percentage change in the quantity demanded of cars divided by the percentage change in the price of the other good.

Cars and fuel are complements, so the coefficient is negative: a rise in the price of fuel makes motoring dearer, so demand for cars falls. The closer the relationship, the larger the negative value and the bigger the fall. Cars and public transport are substitutes, so the coefficient is positive: a rise in the price of public transport makes cars more attractive and demand for cars rises, by more if the link is stronger.

Top tips

  • Give the formula for XED. % change in quantity of Y ÷ % change in price of X.
  • Sign tells the relationship. Positive: substitutes. Negative: complements. Zero: unrelated.
  • Size tells the strength. A larger number means a closer link.
  • Name the curve that shifts. A rise in a substitute's price shifts demand right.
  • Derived demand needs a final product. Steel demand follows car demand.
  • Joint supply is a by-product. More beef means more leather: supply shifts right.
  • Use examples. Cars and petrol, tea and coffee, beef and leather.
