Consequences of Inflation

4.6.5: the domestic and external consequences of inflation, who gains and loses, and the effects of very high inflation. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Inflation redistributes income and wealth: borrowers and owners of real assets gain because debts fall in real terms, while savers, lenders and people on fixed incomes lose purchasing power. It also imposes costs: menu costs (changing prices), shoe leather costs (searching for the best return), uncertainty that discourages investment, and a loss of international competitiveness if domestic inflation is higher than abroad, which can worsen the current account and push the exchange rate down. High and accelerating inflation (hyperinflation) can destroy the functions of money and cause economic collapse, while deflation can be harmful too, as consumers delay spending and the real burden of debt rises. A low, stable rate of inflation is usually considered acceptable; the severity of the consequences depends on how high the rate is, whether it was expected and who is affected.

2 · External consequences and extreme cases

  • Competitiveness: if domestic inflation is higher than in trading partners, exports become dearer and imports cheaper, so the current account may worsen and the exchange rate may fall.
  • Hyperinflation: prices rise so fast that money stops working as a medium of exchange and a store of value; people switch to barter or foreign currencies and the economy can collapse.
  • Deflation: falling prices make consumers delay purchases, raise the real value of debt, and can cause a spiral of falling spending and output.
  • Possible benefits: mild inflation can reduce the real burden of debt, raise profit margins, and allow real wages to adjust in a recession without cutting money wages.
  • Is it always negative? No: it depends on the rate, whether it was expected, who gains or loses, and how it compares with other countries. Unexpected and high inflation is the most damaging.

3 · Worked examples: who gains and who loses?

  • Whether inflation gains or hurts depends on whether incomes and interest rates keep up with prices, and whether inflation was expected. Expected inflation can be built into wages and interest rates; unexpected inflation redistributes more.

4 · Costs of inflation explained

  • Menu costs: the cost of changing prices, such as reprinting price lists, repricing items and updating systems. Larger when inflation is high and frequent.
  • Shoe leather costs: time and effort spent trying to keep the value of money, such as visiting several banks to find better rates or holding smaller amounts of cash.
  • Uncertainty: firms find it harder to forecast costs and revenues, so they invest less, which reduces future growth.
  • Wage-price spiral: expected inflation feeds into wage demands, raising costs and prices again.
  • Loss of competitiveness: if domestic prices rise faster than abroad, exports become dearer and imports cheaper. The current account may worsen and the exchange rate may fall, which in turn raises import prices.
  • Distortion of signals: when all prices are rising, it is harder to tell whether a particular price rise reflects scarcity or just general inflation.

5 · Internal and external effects compared

  • Which is more serious depends on the economy: for a country that relies heavily on trade, external effects may matter most; for a large, less open economy, internal effects such as redistribution and uncertainty may matter more.

6 · Hyperinflation, deflation and disinflation

  • Hyperinflation: extremely rapid price rises. Money fails as a store of value (it loses value daily) and as a medium of exchange (sellers refuse it), so people switch to barter or foreign currency. Savings are wiped out, and the economy can collapse. Zimbabwe abandoned its currency in 2008 and Venezuela suffered very high inflation.
  • Disinflation (a lower rate of inflation) still leaves prices rising, so the problems of inflation continue, though they are smaller; disinflation caused by contractionary policy may cost jobs and output.
  • Deflation: delayed spending, a higher real burden of debt and falling profits can cause a spiral of falling output. It is generally more dangerous than mild inflation, especially if caused by falling AD.

7 · Are the consequences always negative?

  • Possible benefits: profits may rise if prices rise by more than costs, which can encourage investment and employment; debts fall in real terms; and mild inflation lets real wages adjust without money wage cuts.
  • Judging severity: consider the rate (2% or 20%), whether it is expected, who is affected, whether it is higher than in other countries, and whether incomes rise at least as fast.
  • A low, stable rate is usually seen as acceptable and even desirable; high and volatile inflation is clearly harmful.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A pensioner on a fixed income

A pension of $1,000 a month buys less each year if prices rise 5% a year. After five years it buys about 78% as much, so pensioners lose out unless the pension is index-linked.

Exam use: fixed income earners losing from inflation.

A mortgage borrower

A household owes $100,000 at a fixed rate. If prices and wages rise, the debt shrinks in real terms and is easier to repay. Lenders lose the real value of what they are owed.

Exam use: borrowers gaining and lenders losing.

A restaurant changing its menu

Rising costs force a restaurant to reprint menus and change prices several times a year. The time and cost are menu costs, which are small for mild inflation but large when prices rise rapidly.

Exam use: menu costs.

Hyperinflation

When prices rise by hundreds of percent a month, wages lose value within days. People spend money as soon as they receive it or switch to a foreign currency, and money stops working as a store of value and medium of exchange.

Exam use: extreme consequences of inflation.

Savers and fixed incomes

A pensioner lives on a fixed pension while prices rise 8% a year. Each year the pension buys less, so purchasing power falls.

Exam use: who loses from inflation.

Hyperinflation

In extreme cases, prices double within weeks. Shops reprice several times a day, workers rush to spend wages immediately, and people turn to foreign currencies, so the national currency stops working as money.

Exam use: menu costs, shoe leather costs and the functions of money.

Based on May/June 2022 · 9708/21 · Q1(c) 4 marks Explain one domestic consequence and one external consequence of a relatively high rate of inflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Identification of one possible domestic consequence (1) and an explanation (1): for example redistribution of income as real earnings of some workers rise and others fall, borrowers gaining and lenders losing, menu costs, shoe leather costs, uncertainty that could discourage investment, weaker purchasing power of money.
  • Identification of one possible external consequence (1) and an explanation (1): for example exports become less competitive, imports more attractive, the current account worsens and the exchange rate may fall.
PlanDomestic: e.g. loss of purchasing power or menu costs. External: loss of export competitiveness.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A domestic consequence is a loss of purchasing power for people on fixed incomes, because their money buys less each year as prices rise. An external consequence is a loss of competitiveness: if inflation at home is higher than in other countries, exports become dearer to foreign buyers and imports become cheaper for home buyers, so the current account is likely to worsen.

  • 1 mark for a valid judgement on the extent to which a high inflation rate is a serious problem, taking into account how it compares with other countries and the fact that inflation can have some benefits.

A very high inflation rate reduces the purchasing power of money, hurts savers and fixed-income earners, makes exports less competitive and creates uncertainty that may discourage investment. Some groups gain, since borrowers see debts fall in real terms and firms may see profits rise. Overall it is likely to be a serious problem if it is much higher than in other countries and unpredictable, but its size and whether it has been expected affect how damaging it is.

Based on Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/22 · Q4(b) 12 marks Discuss whether the domestic effects of inflation are more serious than the external effects for an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): domestic effects, such as loss of purchasing power, redistribution between borrowers and lenders and between fixed and variable incomes, menu and shoe leather costs, and the effect of uncertainty on investment (up to 4); external effects, such as loss of competitiveness of exports, a worsening current account and a falling exchange rate (up to 4). Indicative content may include these points.

PlanDomestic: purchasing power, redistribution, menu costs, investment. External: competitiveness, current account, exchange rate. Judge: depends on how open the economy is.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Domestic effects of inflation include a loss of purchasing power, especially for savers and people on fixed incomes, redistribution from lenders to borrowers, menu and shoe leather costs, and uncertainty that may discourage investment. External effects arise if domestic inflation is higher than in competitor countries: exports become less competitive, imports more attractive, and the current account and the exchange rate may worsen.

The domestic effects are probably more serious for a mainly domestic economy, since they affect most households, while the external effects matter more for a trade-dependent economy. It also depends on how high the inflation is relative to other countries and whether it was expected.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/24 · Q4(b) 12 marks Assess whether the consequences of inflation in an economy are always negative.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the negative consequences, such as loss of purchasing power, redistribution against savers and fixed-income earners, menu and shoe leather costs, uncertainty, and loss of competitiveness (up to 4); possible positive consequences, such as reducing the real value of debts, higher profit margins that may encourage investment, and allowing real wages to adjust (up to 4).
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on whether the consequences are always negative, depending on the rate, whether it was expected and who is affected, with a conclusion.
PlanNegatives: purchasing power, savers, costs, competitiveness. Positives: debt falls, profits. Judge: mild, expected inflation is acceptable; high, unexpected is damaging.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Inflation reduces the value of money, so savers and those on fixed incomes lose, firms face menu costs and uncertainty that can discourage investment, and exports become less competitive if inflation is higher than abroad. But inflation also reduces the real value of debts, helping borrowers and governments, and rising prices can raise profits, which may encourage investment.

The consequences are not always negative. Low, stable and expected inflation, around 2%, causes few problems and can help borrowers, while high, rising and unexpected inflation is damaging to most groups. The result depends on the rate, whether it was expected and who is affected.

Based on Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/22 · Q5(b) 12 marks Assess whether the internal effects of inflation are more serious than the external effects of inflation for an economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): the internal effects of inflation, those affecting the domestic economy directly: income redistribution, menu costs, shoe leather costs, uncertainty in transactions, and that it may provide a stimulus for output and be a sign of economic growth; and the external effects, those affecting international trade: declining price competitiveness of exports and increased imports, a worsening current account and effects on the exchange rate.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on which effects are more serious, with a conclusion.
PlanInternal: redistribution, menu and shoe leather costs, uncertainty. External: exports less competitive, imports up, current account, exchange rate. Judge: depends on how open the economy is and the rate.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Internally, inflation redistributes income from savers and people on fixed incomes to borrowers, imposes menu and shoe leather costs and creates uncertainty that discourages investment. Externally, higher domestic inflation makes exports dearer and imports cheaper, so the current account may worsen and the currency may weaken, which raises import prices.

Which is more serious depends on the economy. For a very open economy, external effects such as lost competitiveness may matter more, while for a less open one the internal effects such as redistribution and uncertainty may dominate. The effects are also worse when inflation is high and unexpected.

Based on May/June 2025 · 9708/21 · Q1(c) 4 marks In an attempt to bring about disinflation, a central bank used a contractionary monetary policy. Consider whether disinflation is more harmful than deflation.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 3): disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation (1), so although the problems of inflation are less they remain, such as the effect on the distribution of income, purchasing power, the competitiveness of exports, menu costs, shoe leather costs and investment planning (1); deflation is a fall in the general price level (1), which can bring problems such as a fall in AD leading to a recession, lower output and higher unemployment, and negative effects on investment (1). A maximum of 2 if only one of the two is considered.
  • Evaluation (up to 1 or 2): for example, the extent of the disinflation, that is, by how much the rate is falling.
PlanDisinflation: prices still rise, smaller problems. Deflation: delayed spending, recession, debt. Judge: deflation is usually more harmful, unless disinflation is deep.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation, so prices still rise and the problems of inflation, such as menu costs and redistribution, remain though they are smaller. Deflation is a fall in the general price level, which can make consumers delay spending, causing a fall in AD, lower output and higher unemployment, and raising the real burden of debt. Deflation is generally more harmful because it can cause a spiral of falling output, whereas disinflation lowers the cost of inflation, unless the fall in inflation is so large that it risks pushing the economy into deflation.

Top tips

  • Name winners and losers. Borrowers gain; savers and fixed incomes lose.
  • Use the technical terms. Menu costs, shoe leather costs, purchasing power.
  • Domestic and external. Competitiveness and current account are external.
  • Mention expectations. Unexpected inflation is more damaging.
  • Discuss rate. Low, stable inflation is acceptable.
  • Include deflation and hyperinflation. Extreme cases of price instability.
  • Judge with 'it depends'. Who, how high, how expected.
