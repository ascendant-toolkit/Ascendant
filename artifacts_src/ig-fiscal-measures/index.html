Fiscal Policy Measures

Changes in taxes and in government spending: what each measure does, who gains, and the catch. Syllabus 4.2.5.

The big idea

The syllabus lists two kinds of fiscal policy measure: changes in taxes and changes in government spending. Each can be expansionary or contractionary. A government picks the measure that suits its aim: speed, who it helps, and what it costs the budget.

Changes in taxes

Changes in government spending

MeasureExpansionary (increase)Contractionary (cut)
Capital spending (roads, schools, hospitals)Builds assets, creates jobs, and may raise AS laterFewer projects; jobs lost in construction
Current spending (wages, services)Staff and suppliers paid; services improvePublic sector jobs or pay cut
Transfer payments (benefits, pensions)Recipients spend most of it: strong AD effectLower incomes for poorer households

Tax cut or spending rise?

Spending

Goes into AD in full and at once. Can be targeted at an area or group. Harder to reverse once it starts.

Tax cuts

Part of the cut is saved or spent on imports, so the first effect on AD is smaller. People choose how to use the money. Easier to reverse.

Evaluation remindersWhich group gains, how fast the effect is, what it does to the budget deficit, whether the economy has spare capacity, and whether it conflicts with other aims.

Stimulus after a crisis

After the 2008 financial crisis and during the COVID-19 pandemic, many governments raised spending (support payments, wage subsidies, infrastructure) and some cut taxes temporarily such as lowering VAT rates. Deficits and national debt rose sharply as a result.

Exam use: expansionary fiscal policy in action. Cover AD, growth and jobs, then the cost: bigger deficit, higher debt and interest payments.

Austerity and the cost of tightening

Some countries with large deficits raised taxes and cut spending to reduce borrowing. Public finances improved, but critics argued the cuts slowed growth and raised unemployment, which cut tax revenue.

Exam use: contractionary fiscal policy, and a standard evaluation point: reducing the deficit may take longer or cost more output than hoped.

Tax on sugary drinks and tobacco

Many governments add excise duties to tobacco and alcohol, and some to sugary drinks, to discourage demerit goods. Demand for addictive goods is often inelastic, so consumers bear most of the tax and the fall in consumption may be small, but revenue is high.

Exam use: link inelastic demand to incidence, and evaluate: the tax is regressive because poorer households spend a larger share of income on it.

Moving from income tax towards VAT

Some governments, such as France in the 2010s according to coursebook figures, collect more revenue from VAT than from income tax. VAT is easier to collect and harder to evade, but makes the tax system less progressive.

Exam use: a "more or less progressive" question. Shifting towards indirect taxes tends to make the system less progressive.

Q1 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how an expansionary fiscal policy could affect economic growth and employment.
ReminderDraw AD shifting right on an AD/AS diagram, label Y and Y₁, then explain in words. Say how AD rises (which of C, I or G changes), then growth, then employment. Hedged wording scores: "could", "is likely to".
KnowledgeApplicationAnalysisEvaluation

An expansionary fiscal policy is a rise in government spending and/or a cut in taxation designed to increase aggregate demand. If a government cuts income tax rates, households keep more of their pay. Higher disposable income is likely to raise consumption, and higher consumption could also encourage firms to invest. Extra government spending, for example on roads, adds to G directly and could create a multiplier effect, so the rise in AD may be bigger than the initial change.

The AD curve shifts to the right, from AD to AD₁, so real GDP rises from Y to Y₁: economic growth. To produce more output firms are likely to need more workers, so employment could rise and unemployment fall. The extra demand could also raise the price level, and the budget deficit may widen in the short run.

Q2 Explain 4 marks Explain how a cut in corporation tax could affect firms.
ReminderLink tax to profit, then to a decision firms might make. Add a second effect, such as attracting foreign firms.
Corporation tax is a tax on firms' profits, so a cut raises the profit firms keep. Firms could use the extra profit to invest in machinery or expand, which could raise their output. A lower rate could also encourage firms from abroad to locate in the country, creating jobs.
Q3 Analyse 6 marks Analyse how an increase in VAT could reduce inflation.
ReminderSay which kind of inflation. Chain: VAT up, price up, real income down, spending down, AD left. Mention that VAT may first raise prices.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
VAT is an indirect tax on spending, so a rise increases the prices of goods in the shops. Households have less real disposable income to spend, so consumption could fall. Lower consumption shifts aggregate demand to the left, which reduces demand-pull pressure on the price level. However, the rise in VAT raises prices directly at first, so measured inflation could rise before it falls.
Q4 Discuss 8 marks Discuss whether cutting income tax is a better way to reduce unemployment than raising government spending.
ReminderCompare speed, size, targeting, cost. End with 'it depends on…'.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)
Both are expansionary fiscal policy, designed to raise aggregate demand and so create jobs. A cut in income tax raises disposable income, so consumption could rise and firms could take on workers. It also gives people the choice of how to spend, and may encourage people to work. However, some of the extra income may be saved or spent on imports, so the first effect on AD is smaller. Raising government spending adds to AD in full and can be targeted at areas with high unemployment, for example building projects, and the multiplier could create further jobs. However, projects may take time to start and it may need borrowing. Overall, spending is likely to be better when unemployment is high in particular regions or skill groups, whereas a tax cut may be better if the aim is to give broad support quickly and the government wants to reduce its own role. The choice depends on the cause of unemployment, the budget position and spare capacity.

Top tips

  • Name the measure exactly. Income tax, corporation tax, VAT, infrastructure spending, benefits: not just 'taxes'.
  • A spending rise adds to AD in full; a tax cut only in part. Some is saved or spent on imports.
  • Say who gains and who loses. Households, firms, the unemployed, taxpayers.
  • Evaluate with the budget. Expansionary measures usually widen the deficit.
  • Time lags. Capital projects take years; tax changes can be quick.
