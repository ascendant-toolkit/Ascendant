Consumer Equilibrium and Deriving Demand with Indifference Curves

Where the budget line touches the highest reachable indifference curve, why MRS equals Px ÷ Py, and how a series of price changes builds the individual demand curve. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

The consumer maximises satisfaction by choosing the bundle on the highest indifference curve the budget line allows. That is where the budget line is a tangent to the curve, so the slope of the curve (the MRS) equals the slope of the line (Px ÷ Py). Change the price of X and the budget line pivots to a new tangency; plotting each price against the quantity of X bought gives the individual demand curve, and a change in income shifts the line in parallel. The tool therefore explains demand without measuring utility in numbers.

1 · Consumer equilibrium

The consumer wants the highest indifference curve that the budget line allows. That is where the budget line is a tangent to an indifference curve.

Tangency: MRS = Px ÷ Py, so MUx ÷ Px = MUy ÷ Py

This is the same condition as the equi-marginal principle from syllabus 7.1: indifference curves reach it without needing to measure utility in numbers.

2 · Equilibrium in numbers

Income $120, Px = $4, Py = $2. With the preferences used on this page (equal weight on both goods) the consumer spends half of income on each good: 15 X and 30 Y. The MRS there is 30 ÷ 15 = 2, equal to Px ÷ Py = 4 ÷ 2 = 2. At any other point on the budget line the MRS differs from 2, and a higher curve is not affordable.

3 · Deriving the demand curve

  1. Start at equilibrium with the original budget line. This gives the first (price, quantity) point.
  2. Change the price of X. The budget line pivots. Find the new tangency.
  3. Repeat. The path of the equilibria is the price consumption curve.
  4. Plot each price against the quantity of X bought. Joining the points gives the demand curve. It slopes down for normal and ordinary inferior goods and up for a Giffen good.

Income changesA rise in income shifts the budget line out in parallel, and the new tangency shows how much more of each good is bought. Joining those points gives the income consumption curve, the basis for telling normal goods from inferior goods (part 4).

4 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. The numbers are simple on purpose, to show the idea.

A student's weekly budget

A student splits a fixed amount between food and travel. Each combination that uses up all the money is on the budget line. If bus fares fall, the line pivots outwards and the student may buy more of both.

Exam use: a clear everyday description of a budget line and a pivot caused by a price change.

A pay rise

When wages rise and prices stay the same, the budget line shifts out. A person who buys more of most goods is treating them as normal goods. The new tangency is on a higher indifference curve.

Exam use: an income change: parallel shift, new equilibrium, higher curve.

Seasonal fruit prices

When strawberries are cheap in season, shoppers buy more of them. Each price gives a new tangency, and joining the price and quantity pairs traces the demand curve.

Exam use: deriving an individual demand curve from a series of budget line pivots.

Top tips

  • A price change pivots the budget line. An income change shifts it in parallel.
  • Move from the diagram to the demand curve. Join the price-quantity points and say whether it slopes up or down.
  • Equilibrium is a tangency: say MRS = Px ÷ Py and draw the budget line just touching the highest curve it can reach.
