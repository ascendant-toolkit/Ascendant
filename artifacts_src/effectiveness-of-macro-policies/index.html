A Level Economics · 9708 · Syllabus 10.3.1

Effectiveness of Policy Options to Meet Macroeconomic Objectives

10.3.1: how effective fiscal policy (with the Laffer curve), monetary policy, supply-side policy, exchange rate policy and trade policy are for each objective. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Each policy works best on some objectives and badly or not at all on others. Fiscal policy (including the Laffer curve argument for lower tax rates) and monetary policy act mainly on aggregate demand. Supply-side policy, market-based or interventionist, shifts aggregate supply and acts slowly. Exchange rate policy and international trade policy work mostly through the balance of payments and are expenditure-switching. To judge effectiveness, ask which objective the policy targets, how big and how fast the effect is, what it costs and what it does to the other objectives.

2 · Fiscal policy and the Laffer curve

  • Fiscal policy raises or lowers AD by changing spending or taxes. In a downturn a larger budget deficit raises output and jobs; near capacity a smaller deficit cools inflation.
  • The Laffer curve shows tax revenue against the tax rate. At 0% revenue is zero; at 100% nobody has an incentive to work legally so revenue is also near zero. Revenue is highest at an optimum rate.
  • If the rate is above the peak, cutting the tax could raise revenue, because lower rates reduce avoidance and evasion and increase work effort, saving and enterprise. This supports supply-side tax cuts.
  • Evaluate: where the peak lies is uncertain (estimates vary), most countries are probably left of it, labour supply may be inelastic, cuts may widen inequality, and effects take time.

3 · Monetary policy

  • Raising interest rates reduces consumption and investment (the transmission mechanism), attracts hot money and may raise the exchange rate, so inflation falls but growth and jobs may fall.
  • Lowering rates or quantitative easing boosts AD and may depreciate the currency, but may be ineffective if confidence is low (liquidity trap) and may raise inflation or asset prices.
  • It can be changed quickly by an independent central bank, but with a lag of 12 to 24 months.

4 · Supply-side policy

  • Market-based: privatisation, deregulation, labour market reform, lower income and corporation taxes (using Laffer logic), competition policy.
  • Interventionist: investment in education, training, infrastructure, R&D, regional policy.
  • The best policy for long-run, non-inflationary growth and a lower natural rate, but slow and costly, and it does not solve a lack of demand.

5 · Exchange rate policy and the J curve

  • A depreciation or devaluation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home (expenditure-switching). The current account improves if the Marshall-Lerner condition holds: the sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports is greater than 1.
  • The J curve: in the short run the current account often worsens first, because demand is inelastic and import prices rise at once, then improves as volumes adjust.
  • Costs: imported inflation, higher costs of imported inputs, retaliation, and loss of reserves if a rate is defended. An appreciation lowers inflation but hurts exporters.

6 · International trade policy

  • Protection (tariffs, quotas, export subsidies) switches spending from imports to domestic goods, which can improve the current account and protect jobs and infant industries.
  • Costs: higher prices for consumers and firms using imported inputs, retaliation by trading partners, protected firms become inefficient, WTO rules limit it.
  • Trade liberalisation raises competition and efficiency and may raise long-run growth, but job losses in uncompetitive industries (structural unemployment) can follow.
How to evaluate effectivenessMatch the policy to the cause, quantify its size (multiplier, elasticities), consider timing, cost, and the effect on the other objectives (see 10.3.2). A mix of policies is usually needed.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

US tax cuts in the 1980s and 2017

Supporters of the Laffer curve argued that cutting top tax rates would raise revenue. Studies of both periods generally found that revenue fell compared with what would otherwise have been collected, suggesting rates were below the peak.

Exam use: an example for Laffer analysis, with evaluation that most economies are left of the peak.

UK devaluation in 1967 and the pound after 2016

After the pound fell in 1967 and again in 2016 the trade balance did not improve immediately. Import prices rose first and export volumes responded over a longer period.

Exam use: the J curve and the Marshall-Lerner condition. Use it to say the short-run effect may be the opposite of the long-run effect.

US tariffs on steel and aluminium

In 2018 the US imposed tariffs on steel and aluminium to protect domestic producers. Steel prices rose for US manufacturers, some trading partners retaliated, and employment gains in steel were small compared with job losses elsewhere.

Exam use: trade policy and its costs. Evaluate on retaliation, higher costs for users and consumers, and the effect on competitiveness.

Quantitative easing after 2008

Central banks bought government bonds to push down long-term interest rates when official rates were already near zero. It helped to stabilise financial markets, but its effect on spending was modest and it raised asset prices.

Exam use: monetary policy limits: a liquidity trap and distributional effects.

Practice (not a past paper question) 8 marks Explain why a devaluation might make the current account worse before it improves.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Immediately after a devaluation, import prices rise in domestic currency while the volumes bought are fixed by existing contracts and habits, so the import bill rises. Export prices in foreign currency fall, so export receipts may fall initially.
  • Over time demand for exports rises and demand for imports falls as buyers switch, so volumes adjust.
  • The J curve and the Marshall-Lerner condition: the current account improves in the long run if PEDx + PEDm is greater than 1.
  • Evaluation: imported inflation and retaliation may cancel the benefits.
PlanPrice effect first. Volume effect later. Marshall-Lerner. One limit.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A devaluation is a fall in the value of a fixed exchange rate. It makes exports cheaper for foreign buyers and imports dearer for domestic buyers. In the short run, demand for both is inelastic because contracts are already agreed and it takes time to find new suppliers and customers. Import prices rise immediately in domestic currency, so the import bill goes up, and export revenue may fall because each export earns less foreign currency. The current account therefore worsens first, which gives the J-shaped path.

As time passes, buyers at home switch to domestic goods and foreigners buy more exports, so the volumes adjust and the current account improves. Over the long run this happens only if the sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports is greater than one, the Marshall-Lerner condition. However, dearer imported inputs raise costs and could cause inflation, and trading partners might retaliate, so the improvement may be smaller than the simple theory suggests.

Top tips

  • Always link policy to objective. Say which objective, the mechanism and the size of the effect.
  • Draw the diagram the question asks for. Laffer curve, AD and AS, tariff diagram or the J curve. Without a diagram 20 mark answers are capped.
  • Say expenditure-switching or expenditure-reducing. Exchange rate and trade policy switch spending. Fiscal and monetary tightening reduce it.
  • Use Marshall-Lerner and the J curve. Depreciation works only if PEDx + PEDm is greater than 1, and the current account may worsen first.
  • Laffer: say 'above the peak'. Tax cuts raise revenue only if the rate is above the revenue-maximising rate. Most countries are probably below it.
  • Compare policies. Fiscal and monetary are fast but short-run. Supply-side is slow but permanent. Choose by cause and time horizon.
  • Evaluate with cost, lags and other aims. Every policy has a price. Name it.
