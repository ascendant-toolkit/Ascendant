This page in five sentences

The syllabus asks for the principal operation of a set of devices: laser printer, 3D printer, microphone, speakers, magnetic hard disk, solid state (flash) drive, optical disc reader/writer, touchscreens (resistive and capacitive) and a virtual reality headset. For each you need an ordered account of what physically happens, using the key terms. Mark schemes award one mark per correct stage, so learn each device as a short chain of steps. Several devices also use sensors, buffers or microprocessors, which links back to earlier pages. Papers 1 and 2 most often ask about the hard disk, flash memory, touchscreens, the 3D printer, the microphone and the VR headset.

1 · Magnetic hard disk

  • One or more platters (disks) coated with a magnetisable (ferrous oxide) material, mounted on a central spindle, inside a sealed box.
  • The platters rotate at high speed. Each surface has a read/write head on an arm just above the surface; electronic circuits control the arm movement.
  • The surface is divided into concentric tracks and sectors.
  • Writing: the head produces a magnetic field that sets the magnetic pattern (north/south) of tiny areas, representing 1s and 0s. Reading: the head detects the pattern of the magnetised areas.

2 · Solid state (flash) drive

  • No moving parts: data is stored in cells built from transistors arranged as NAND or NOR gates, using 1 or 2 transistors per cell.
  • Each cell has a floating gate that can retain electrons without power (this stores the bit) and a control gate that allows or stops current from passing.
  • Writing changes the charge on the floating gate; reading checks whether the cell conducts. Data is erased in blocks.
  • Compared with magnetic: faster access, silent, more robust, lower power; but more expensive per GB and cells wear out after many write cycles.

3 · Optical disc (CD, DVD, Blu-ray)

  • Data is stored on a spiral track as pits and lands on the surface. A laser shines on the surface while the disc spins.
  • Reading: light reflects differently from a pit and a land, and a sensor reads the changes as 1s and 0s. Writing (recordable discs): a higher-power laser burns marks into a dye layer (or changes a phase-change layer); rewritable discs can be erased by a different laser setting.

5 · Microphone and speakers

Microphone (dynamic): sound waves cause a diaphragm to vibrate; this moves a coil past a magnet (or a magnet past a coil), which produces an electrical signal that is then sampled. In a condenser microphone the vibration changes the capacitance; in a crystal microphone it deforms a crystal.

Speaker: an electrical signal (converted from digital data by a DAC) passes through a coil attached to a cone near a permanent magnet. The changing current makes the coil and cone vibrate, which creates sound waves.

6 · Laser printer

  1. The page data arrives and is held in a buffer; a raster image of the page is built.
  2. A laser draws the image onto a rotating, electrically charged drum, leaving charge in the pattern of the page.
  3. Toner (powder) sticks to the charged areas of the drum.
  4. Paper is fed through, with a charge that pulls the toner from the drum onto the paper.
  5. A fuser (heated rollers) melts the toner into the paper, which is then ejected.

7 · 3D printer

  • It uses additive manufacturing: a digital 3D model (a CAD file) is built up one layer at a time from the bottom, using x, y and z coordinates; the material is fused or cured layer by layer.
  • FDM: material is heated and pushed through a nozzle. SLA: a UV laser cures liquid resin. DLP: a projector/lamp cures resin. SLS: a laser fuses powdered material.
  • A temperature sensor stops overheating and ensures the material is hot enough. The printer has a buffer (often DRAM) for print data.

8 · Virtual reality headset

  • One or two (LCD) displays/lenses output the image; speakers give surround sound.
  • A gyroscope/accelerometer detects head movement; a microprocessor works out the direction and speed of movement and updates the image.
  • Some headsets use digital cameras to record eye movements, and a buffer to hold data from the computer.

Common ways to lose marks

  • Writing "the head reads the disk" without saying that the surface is divided into magnetised areas or that the platters spin.
  • Describing flash memory as having a "moving part" or saying that it uses magnetism.
  • Mixing up resistive (circuit completed) and capacitive (change in current).
  • Not saying "layer by layer" for a 3D printer.

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A phone screen

Your finger changes the electric field at one spot on a capacitive screen; a gloved hand or plain plastic stylus often does not work.

Exam use: capacitive needs a conductive touch.

A supermarket checkout screen

Staff can use gloves or a pen because many are resistive: pressing makes two layers touch.

Exam use: resistive works with any object.

A laptop upgrade

Swapping a hard disk for an SSD makes start-up faster because there is no head to move and no disk to spin up.

Exam use: no moving parts.

A 3D-printed part

Engineers print prototypes and replacement parts from a CAD file layer by layer, wasting less material than cutting from a block.

Exam use: additive manufacturing.

Gaming with a VR headset

Turning your head changes the view straight away because the sensors report the direction and speed of your head movement to the microprocessor.

Exam use: gyroscope and accelerometer.

Top tips

  • One mark per stage. Write each device as a chain of steps.
  • Hard disk: platters, spindle, head, arm. Say rotating and magnetised areas.
  • Flash: floating gate, control gate. NAND and NOR, 1 or 2 transistors.
  • Resistive: circuit. Capacitive: change in current. Do not swap them.
  • 3D printer: additive, layers, CAD. Name x, y and z if you can.
  • VR: gyroscope, direction and speed. Cameras for eye tracking.
