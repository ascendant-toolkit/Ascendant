A Level Economics · 9708 · Syllabus 9.1 · Paper 4 worked essays

Two Paper 4 Essays on 9.1, Marked in Depth

A 13-mark question on monetary policy and the inflationary gap (May/June 2020) and a 20-mark question on the multiplier and fiscal policy (May/June 2026). Each comes with a plan, diagrams, a colour-coded model answer and a line-by-line account of where the marks come from.

This resource in four sentences

Both questions test the same 9.1 core: aggregate expenditure, the full employment level of income, the multiplier and the gaps, but they ask you to do different things with it. Question 1 asks how far you agree that one policy (monetary) is the most effective way to close an inflationary gap, so you must compare it with fiscal and supply-side policy. Question 2 asks you to evaluate a statement about the multiplier and expansionary fiscal policy with a negative output gap, so you must show why it is true in theory and then test it. In both, marks come from developed chains of reasoning applied to the question, a correct diagram and explained evaluation.

4 · Model answer

Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

An inflationary gap exists when aggregate expenditure at the full employment level of income is greater than potential output, so real output cannot rise to meet demand and the excess demand pulls up the price level. Monetary policy is the use of interest rates, the money supply and credit by the central bank to influence aggregate demand. I agree to a considerable extent that monetary policy is the most effective of the main options in the short run, because it can be changed quickly and acts on several parts of aggregate demand, but it is not the most effective in every case: it works with a lag and may not be enough for a large gap or for inflation caused by costs.

In the diagram the economy is in equilibrium at 650, above full employment income of 500, because AE1 is 60 above the 45° line at YF. A rise in the central bank's interest rate raises the cost of borrowing, so firms may cancel investment projects financed by loans and households with mortgages or credit cards have less to spend. The higher reward for saving may reduce consumption, and higher rates may attract foreign funds, raising the exchange rate and making exports dearer and imports cheaper, so net exports fall. The Bank of England raised Bank Rate from 0.1% in late 2021 to 5.25% by August 2023 as UK inflation rose above 11% in 2022. As C, I and (X − M) fall, AE shifts down to AE2, and because the multiplier is 2.5, a fall in autonomous spending of 60 lowers income by 150, back to full employment, which removes the excess demand. However, this only works if spending is sensitive to interest rates. If firms are confident about profits, or many households hold fixed-rate mortgages, investment and consumption may fall by less than 60, so some of the gap remains. Interest rate changes also take around a year or more to work fully, so the gap could close for other reasons first and the policy could push the economy into a deflationary gap.

A second type of monetary policy is a reduction in the money supply, for example the central bank selling government bonds or requiring banks to hold more reserves. This reduces banks' ability to lend, so credit becomes scarcer and dearer, and spending on items bought on credit, such as cars and houses, is likely to fall. However, banks may find other sources of funds, and the effect on spending is less predictable than a change in the policy rate, so central banks mainly use the interest rate.

Fiscal policy is the main alternative. A cut in government spending of 60, or a tax rise that cuts consumption by 60, shifts AE down straight away, because government spending is itself a component of AE. This makes fiscal policy more direct than monetary policy, whose effect depends on how households and firms respond. However, spending cuts and tax rises are politically unpopular and take time to pass, they could reduce public services for the poor, and the multiplier may make the fall in income larger than the government intended. An independent central bank can change rates monthly, which makes monetary policy more flexible.

Supply-side policy, such as investment in training and infrastructure, raises potential output so YF moves to the right and the gap is closed by raising the supply of output rather than cutting demand. This avoids lower growth and jobs, but it takes years to work, and so it is unlikely to deal with a gap that already exists.

Overall I agree to a limited but considerable extent. Monetary policy is likely to be the most effective single tool for a small inflationary gap in the short run, because it can be adjusted quickly by an independent central bank and acts on consumption, investment and net exports at once. It is less likely to be the most effective when the gap is large, when inflation is caused by cost-push factors such as energy prices, which higher rates do not reduce directly, or when spending is not sensitive to interest rates. The most effective response is therefore likely to combine monetary policy with fiscal and supply-side measures, and which is most effective depends on the size and cause of the gap and how quickly it must be closed.

Question 2 · 20 marks · 9708/41 May/June 2026 Q4. Due to the multiplier process, governments conducting an expansionary fiscal policy do not need to increase spending by the full amount of the negative output gap to bring the output of the economy to its full employment level. Evaluate this statement.

1 · Decode the question

PhraseWhat it asks you to do
Evaluate this statementThe question gives you a claim to test. Decide how far it is true and under what conditions. 6 of the 20 marks are for evaluation, and the conclusion must answer the statement.
Due to the multiplier processThe claim rests on the multiplier. You must explain the process: rounds of spending, leakages, induced consumption and investment.
expansionary fiscal policyDefine it: higher government spending and/or lower taxes, financed by a budget deficit. Not monetary policy.
do not need to increase spending by the full amount of the negative output gapThe core claim: required spending = output gap ÷ multiplier, which is less than the gap if the multiplier is above 1. Test it with numbers.
negative output gap, full employmentDefine both. Actual output below potential. All resources fully used at the target.

2 · What the mark scheme says (indicative content)

Knowledge and analysis (14 marks)

  • Negative output gap: actual output below potential output.
  • Full employment: all resources fully and efficiently used, no cyclical unemployment.
  • AD as a determinant of output; expansionary fiscal policy as lower taxes and higher G, creating a deficit.
  • G as an injection and a component of AD.
  • Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ MPW = 1 ÷ (MPS + MTR + MPM) = change in income ÷ change in injection.
  • Multiple rounds of spending; induced consumption and investment.
  • Examples of what the government can spend on and the rounds it creates.
  • A diagram with more than one shift of AD (or one shift with a written explanation that the rise is more than the government spent).

Evaluation (6 marks)

  • The multiplier may be uncertain, so spending could be insufficient.
  • Uncertainty could also cause overshooting and higher inflation.
  • G is a small part of AD, so the impact could be small even with the multiplier.
  • Crowding out.
  • National debt: debt servicing, lower investor confidence, debt crises.
  • A large MPM or a small MPC gives a small multiplier.

5 · Model answer

Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A negative output gap exists when actual output is below potential output, so some resources, including labour, are unemployed and there is cyclical unemployment. Full employment is the level of output at which all available resources are fully and efficiently used. Expansionary fiscal policy is an increase in government spending (G) and/or a cut in taxes, financed by a budget deficit, to raise aggregate demand. The multiplier is the ratio of the final change in national income to the initial change in an injection. The statement is correct in theory, because every dollar of government spending is re-spent in later rounds, but in practice a government cannot be sure how large the multiplier is or how big the gap is, so it may spend too little or too much.

Suppose potential output is $500bn and actual output is $400bn, a negative output gap of $100bn. Government spending is an injection into the circular flow and a component of aggregate demand. If the government spends $40bn on building roads and railways, that spending becomes income for construction firms and their workers. The workers spend part of their wages in shops and restaurants, which pay their own staff and suppliers, so the spending passes through several rounds. With an MPS of 0.1, a marginal rate of tax of 0.2 and an MPM of 0.1, the marginal propensity to withdraw is 0.4, so the multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.4 = 2.5. Of the first $40bn, $24bn is spent in the second round, $14.4bn in the third, and so on, so national income rises by 40 × 2.5 = $100bn. As incomes rise, households also increase induced consumption, and firms with spare capacity may invest in new equipment to meet growing demand, which adds to later rounds. The government therefore needs to spend only the output gap ÷ the multiplier, which is $40bn or 40% of the gap.

In the diagram, AD1 meets the Keynesian AS curve at $400bn. The initial rise in G of $40bn shifts AD to AD2. The extra income is re-spent, so AD shifts again to AD3, where output reaches the full employment level of $500bn. The total shift of $100bn is much larger than the $40bn the government spent, which supports the statement.

However, the multiplier is not known with certainty. If MPM is large, as in a small, open economy that imports much of what it consumes, or if MPC is small because households in a recession save more out of fear of unemployment, the multiplier may be much lower. With an MPS of 0.2, a tax rate of 0.2 and an MPM of 0.4, the multiplier falls to 1 ÷ 0.8 = 1.25 and the government would need to spend $80bn, four-fifths of the gap, so the statement would be true but hardly useful. If the government spends only the $40bn it estimated, output would remain well below full employment, unemployment would persist and the policy would be judged to have failed. If the multiplier were larger than expected, the extra spending could overshoot full employment, so that demand outruns potential output and inflation rises.

A second problem is crowding out. Expansionary fiscal policy creates a larger deficit, and the government borrows by selling bonds. This raises the demand for loanable funds and may push up interest rates, which reduces private investment, so the net multiplier is smaller and the government would need to spend more than the formula suggests. However, in a deep recession, with spare capacity and a central bank that keeps interest rates low, crowding out is likely to be small. A higher deficit adds to national debt and the cost of servicing it, which may reduce investor confidence and, if debt is already high, raise the risk of a debt crisis in which the government has to cut spending later.

The size of the effect also depends on scale and timing. Government spending is only part of aggregate demand, and if consumer and business confidence are falling, an increase of $40bn could be offset by falling private spending, so the economy still does not reach full employment. Spending on infrastructure takes time to plan, so it may arrive after the economy has already started to recover, and potential output is estimated and may be wrong, so the gap itself is uncertain.

Overall, the statement is correct as a matter of economic theory: the spending required is the output gap divided by the multiplier, which is less than the gap whenever the multiplier is greater than one. But the statement is likely to hold in practice only when the multiplier is large, there is real spare capacity and crowding out is weak, as in a closed, low-import economy in a deep recession. For a small, open economy with a high MPM and high debt, the required increase may be close to the full gap. Because governments cannot measure the multiplier or the gap precisely, the most important factor is the size of the multiplier, as every other limitation works by making it smaller. The statement is therefore best seen as a rule of thumb that gives a minimum required spending, and not as a reason to spend less.
