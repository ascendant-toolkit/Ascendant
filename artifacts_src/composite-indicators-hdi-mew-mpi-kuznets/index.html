Composite Indicators: HDI, MEW, MPI and the Kuznets Curve

11.3.3: the Human Development Index, the Measure of Economic Welfare, the Multidimensional Poverty Index and the Kuznets curve of inequality. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

Composite indicators combine several measures into one number. The Human Development Index (HDI) combines health (life expectancy), education (years of schooling) and income (GNI per capita at PPP); the Measure of Economic Welfare (MEW) adjusts GDP by adding leisure and unpaid work and deducting environmental damage and regrettable spending; and the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) measures deprivation in health, education and living standards as the share of poor people times the intensity of their poverty. The Kuznets curve suggests that inequality first rises and then falls as income per head grows, an inverted U. All of them give a fuller picture than income alone but involve judgements about what to include and how to weight it.

1 · The Human Development Index (HDI)

  • Published by the UN Development Programme. Three dimensions, each turned into an index between 0 and 1: health (life expectancy at birth), education (mean years of schooling of adults and expected years of schooling of children) and standard of living (GNI per capita at PPP, with a logarithm so each extra dollar counts less at high incomes).
  • The HDI is the geometric mean of the three indices, so a country cannot make up for a very low score in one dimension with a high score in another. It ranges from 0 to 1.
  • Countries are grouped as very high (about 0.8 and above), high, medium and low (below about 0.55) human development.
  • Why it is better than income alone: two countries with the same income can have very different health and education, and the HDI shows this. Countries with a high GDP rank but a lower HDI rank show that growth has not translated into well-being.
  • Limits: equal weights are a judgement; it ignores inequality (the Inequality-adjusted HDI corrects this), the environment, political freedom and personal safety; the data on schooling and life expectancy may be inaccurate in some countries; the three components are correlated so it may add little to income at the top; it changes slowly.

2 · The Measure of Economic Welfare (MEW)

  • Developed by Nordhaus and Tobin. Start with GDP (or consumption) and adjust: add the value of leisure and unpaid household work; deduct the cost of environmental damage and "regrettable" spending such as defence, commuting and crime prevention that does not raise welfare.
  • Merits: it measures welfare, not just output, and shows that GDP can rise while welfare falls (for example with pollution).
  • Limits: valuing leisure, unpaid work and pollution involves subjective estimates; data are hard to collect, especially in low-income countries; it is not published regularly like GDP and HDI, so it is rarely used for comparison.

3 · The Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)

  • Measures poverty directly, using ten indicators grouped into three equal dimensions: health (nutrition, child mortality), education (years of schooling, school attendance) and living standards (cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, assets).
  • A person is multidimensionally poor if deprived in at least about one-third of the weighted indicators.
  • MPI = H × A, where H is the headcount ratio (share of people who are poor) and A is the average intensity of deprivation among the poor (share of weighted indicators in which they are deprived).
  • Merits: shows how people are poor (which deprivations), can be broken down by region and group, and does not depend on income measurement. Limits: needs household survey data, the cut-offs and weights are a judgement, and it does not capture inequality within households.

4 · The Kuznets curve

  • Simon Kuznets suggested that income inequality rises in the early stages of growth, reaches a peak and then falls, tracing an inverted U when inequality (for example the Gini coefficient) is plotted against income per head.
  • Rising stage: workers move from low-income farming to higher-paid industry in cities, and the owners of capital and skilled workers gain first, so inequality widens.
  • Falling stage: as more people get education and skills, the population ages and urbanises, and governments introduce progressive taxes and welfare spending, so inequality falls.
  • Evaluation: evidence is mixed; some countries have seen inequality rise again in rich economies (technology, globalisation); policy choices, not just income, shape the path; it describes a tendency, not a law, and does not say how long the rise lasts. It also suggests that early inequality is a price of growth, which governments may not accept.

GDP rank versus HDI rank

The UN's HDI tables often show countries whose HDI rank is well above or below their GDP per head rank. Some middle-income countries with strong public health and education rank higher on the HDI than richer countries with weaker services, which is the usual evidence that income is an incomplete measure.

Exam use: why alternative measures are used, and how the HDI and income can tell different stories.

The MPI and the poverty picture

Many people who are close to the income poverty line are also deprived in sanitation, cooking fuel or schooling. The MPI shows which of these deprivations is the largest in each region, helping governments target aid and public spending.

Exam use: how the MPI shows the nature of poverty, not just its extent.

Fast growth and pollution

A country can raise GDP rapidly with heavy industry and coal power while air quality falls and health costs rise. A welfare measure like the MEW deducts the environmental damage, so welfare may rise by much less than GDP, or fall.

Exam use: why GDP can overstate welfare and how the MEW adjusts for this.

Inequality over time

Studies of some East Asian economies suggest inequality rose during rapid industrialisation. Evidence for rich economies since the 1980s shows inequality rising again in some countries, which does not fit a simple inverted U.

Exam use: evaluating the Kuznets curve.

The HDI is designed to measure well-being using health, education and income. If GDP were a good measure, countries with higher GDP should have a higher HDI. The table shows this is only partly true. Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Real GNI per capita is income from residents, adjusted for inflation and divided by population, and is often used as a proxy for living standards. It is easy to calculate, but ignores income distribution, unpaid work, leisure and environmental damage, and is less accurate in economies with large informal sectors.

The HDI adds life expectancy and education to income at PPP, so it shows health and knowledge that GNI misses. The MEW adjusts GDP by adding leisure and unpaid work and deducting environmental damage and regrettable spending, so it focuses on welfare rather than output. However, the HDI uses equal weights and ignores inequality and the environment. The MEW involves subjective valuation and is rarely published. In developing countries, the cost and accuracy of data collection affect all three measures.

Overall, the HDI and MEW may be better guides to well-being than GNI, but GNI remains a useful, easily compared starting point, so the best approach may be to use several measures together.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/42 · Q7(b) 13 marks Discuss why alternatives to GDP are increasingly used to measure the standard of living.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Limitations of GDP: inflation, population, PPP, non-marketed goods, crime, negative externalities, the nature of the goods produced, distribution and the informal economy.
  • Alternatives: HDI, MEW, MPI and others, with how each deals with the limitations.
  • Evaluation: alternatives have their own problems of weighting and data, so GDP is still widely used.
PlanLimits of GDP. Two alternatives. How they fix the limits. Their own problems.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

GDP counts the market value of output but not distribution, unpaid work, leisure or the environment, and it includes output that may reduce welfare, such as spending on pollution clean-up. Alternatives such as the HDI add health and education, so they show outcomes that matter to people, and the MEW adjusts for leisure, unpaid work and environmental damage. The MPI measures deprivation directly. These measures could give a better indication of well-being, which is why they are increasingly used by international organisations.

However, they require judgements about weights, and data on health and education may be poor in some countries, so GDP remains popular because it is simple and widely available.

Top tips

  • Know the three HDI parts. Health (life expectancy), education (years of schooling), income (GNI per capita at PPP).
  • Know what MEW adds and deducts. Add leisure and unpaid work. Deduct environmental damage and regrettable spending.
  • MPI is H times A. Headcount of poor people times the intensity of their deprivation.
  • Draw the inverted U. Gini coefficient up the axis, income per head along it, a peak at the turning point.
  • Evaluate with limits. Weights, data quality, inequality, environment and what is left out.
  • Compare with GNI. Income is easy to measure but incomplete. Composite measures add well-being but add judgement.
  • Say 'could' or 'may'. The Kuznets curve describes a tendency, not a law.
