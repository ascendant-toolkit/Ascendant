Effects of Mergers and Takeovers, and the Survival of Small Firms

Whether mergers and takeovers help or harm consumers, why competition authorities review them, and why small firms survive next to large ones. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and the past paper questions.

This part in four sentences

A merger or takeover can benefit consumers through economies of scale passed on in lower prices, and can benefit the firm through more profit, but it can also create market power, fewer rivals and higher prices. Whether consumers gain depends on how big the cost saving is, how much is passed on and how much competition remains, which is why competition authorities review large deals. Small firms survive in the same economy because cost is not the only thing customers value: niche products, personal service, flexibility and small markets protect them. Both sides of the argument are needed for evaluation.

1 · Effects of mergers and takeovers

Possible benefits

  • Economies of scale, so lower average cost and possibly lower prices
  • More money for research and development, more innovation (dynamic efficiency)
  • Less duplication, a stronger firm in world markets
  • Workers may gain job security, and managers gain from higher profit

Possible costs

  • Higher prices and less choice if competition falls (monopoly power, supernormal profit)
  • X-inefficiency: a large dominant firm may have less reason to keep costs low
  • Job losses from closing duplicate sites
  • Culture clashes, debt and management problems, so the deal may fail

Whether a merger is in the public interest depends on whether the cost savings are passed on, how strong the remaining competition is, and what the competition authority decides. Authorities may block a merger, approve it, or approve it with conditions (for example selling off some outlets). A demerger reverses a merger when the parts work better apart.

2 · Why do small firms survive?

If big firms have lower costs, why are there so many small ones? Because cost is not the only thing customers care about.

Reasons small firms survive

  • Niche or specialist products that big firms ignore (handmade, organic, craft)
  • Personal service and local knowledge (hairdressers, corner shops)
  • Small MES: the market is large compared with the output needed for lowest cost, so there is room for many small efficient firms
  • Flexibility: quick decisions, a close link between owner and customer
  • Small or local markets, or high transport costs, which protect them
  • Support: grants, tax breaks, cheap loans, supply to big firms as sub-contractors
  • Innovation: new ideas that may later grow the firm

Threats to small firms

  • Large rivals' economies of scale and price cutting
  • Difficulty raising finance (higher interest, less collateral)
  • Low barriers to entry, so new rivals appear
  • Costs of regulation that weigh heavily on a firm with few staff
  • Owner dependence: one person's illness or retirement
  • Shifts in tastes towards standard, cheap products
Small firms survive when what customers value (price + service + variety) beats what the firm loses on higher unit cost

Note the link to syllabus 7.6: where many firms share the market (monopolistic competition) small firms are common. In an oligopoly the high MES leaves room for only a few firms.

5 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Daimler and Chrysler: merger, then demerger

The two car makers merged in 1998 and parted ways in 2007. Reports at the time pointed to differences in company culture and in the way the businesses were run, and the expected gains from combining did not come through.

Exam use: a strong evaluation point for "mergers do not always succeed": culture clash, management problems, diseconomies of scale.

The corner café and the craft brewery

Both compete against much bigger firms. The café offers personal service and a local base. The craft brewery sells a distinctive product to a niche who may pay more. Neither needs a huge output to be efficient.

Exam use: reasons small firms survive: niche, service, local knowledge, small MES. Add that they could be threatened if a large rival copies the product or cuts prices.

A merger blocked or approved with conditions

Competition authorities sometimes block large mergers or approve them only if the firms sell off parts of the business, because the merged firm could raise prices. The authority weighs possible higher prices against cost savings.

Exam use: the 2023 merger question. Draw the diagram, show a cost saving against a price rise, and evaluate.

May/June 2025 · 9708/41 and 43 · Q3 20 marks The growth of a firm using a takeover is desirable because it enables consumers to benefit from lower prices and the firm to gain additional profits. Evaluate this statement.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge and analysis (14): a takeover as external growth; takeover versus merger; horizontal and vertical (backward or forward) takeovers; economies of scale leading to lower costs and possibly lower prices; more market share and revenue leading to higher profit.
  • Evaluation (6): savings may not be passed on; more market share may mean more monopoly power and higher prices; takeovers can be costly and lower profit; communication problems and culture clashes may cause diseconomies; competition authorities may intervene.
  • The conclusion must address both halves of the statement: lower prices and higher profit.

Both the AO1/AO2 and AO3 sections have their own level descriptors: do not leave the evaluation to the last line.

KANE

A takeover occurs when one firm buys another, which is a form of external growth, and it can be horizontal (same stage), vertical (a supplier or a seller) or conglomerate (an unrelated business). It differs from a merger, where two firms agree to join and form a new company.

A takeover could enable consumers to benefit from lower prices if the combined firm gains economies of scale. For example, if a supermarket chain buys a rival chain it could negotiate lower prices from suppliers through bulk buying and close duplicate warehouses, so average cost may fall. If competition remains strong, the firm may pass part of the saving on in lower prices to avoid losing customers to rivals. However, this depends on how much competition is left, and consumers may see little benefit if the firm keeps the saving as profit.

A takeover could also help the firm gain additional profit. A larger market share raises total revenue, and lower unit costs widen the profit margin on each unit, so profit may rise. Backward integration, such as a bakery buying a flour mill, could secure supply and remove the supplier's mark-up. This may not hold if the price paid for the target is high or financed by heavy borrowing, because interest payments could cut profit in the long run.

The statement may be less convincing when the takeover reduces competition. A firm with a much larger share could use its market power to raise prices and earn supernormal profit, which would help the firm but harm consumers. Competition authorities may then block the takeover or set conditions. Culture clashes and communication problems in a larger organisation could also bring diseconomies of scale, which raise costs and may lead to higher prices.

Overall, a takeover is likely to be desirable for the firm in most cases, but it is only likely to be desirable for consumers if the cost savings are large, competition remains strong and the savings are passed on. The statement therefore holds only in some circumstances.

Oct/Nov 2023 · 9708/41 · Q3 20 marks A government allows the merger of two large firms in the same industry. With the help of a diagram, evaluate the view that this merger should not have been allowed.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (14): a horizontal merger, likely to lead to a monopoly or near monopoly. Dominant firms can raise prices and drive out smaller rivals, less choice, X-inefficiency, effects on the local labour market. A relevant diagram is needed: without one the answer is capped at Level 2.
  • Evaluation (6): the firms may argue the merger gives more choice; dynamic efficiency (more investment, lower prices in the long run); economies of scale; regulation can limit harm; a conclusion that judges whether the merger should proceed.

Paragraph 3: choice, X-inefficiency, labour market. Paragraph 4: the other side (economies of scale, dynamic efficiency). Paragraph 5: regulation and a judgement.
Oct/Nov 2021 · 9708/43 · Q4(b) 13 marks 'In large firms the long-run average cost falls as output increases and consumers benefit from lower prices. In small firms it does not. There is, therefore, no place in a modern economy for small firms.' Do you agree with these statements?
What the mark scheme wants
  • Whether costs necessarily fall as output rises (economies then diseconomies).
  • Whether consumers necessarily benefit if costs fall.
  • Whether there is no place for small firms: the purpose and reasons for small firms.
  • A conclusion about the statement as a whole.
LevelWhat earns it
L4 (9–13)Clear analysis and discussion of all parts, including the purpose and reasons for small firms, and a conclusion.
L3 (7–8)Two of the three parts developed, or all three with little analysis.
L2 (5–6)Mostly economies of scale and falling costs, the rest not considered in detail.
L1 (1–4)Some basic correct facts, with irrelevance and errors.
KANE

In large firms the long-run average cost may fall as output increases because of economies of scale. Economies of scale are the fall in LRAC as a firm grows, for example through bulk buying, specialist machinery and cheaper finance. However, LRAC does not fall for ever. Beyond the minimum efficient scale, communication and coordination problems could create diseconomies of scale and push LRAC up, so large firms do not always have lower costs.

Consumers may benefit from lower prices if large firms pass on their lower costs. A supermarket chain facing strong competition could cut prices to keep its customers. But if a large firm has market power it could keep the savings as profit, or raise prices, so the benefit to consumers is not certain.

There is likely to be a place for small firms in a modern economy because many of them do not compete on cost alone. A local café or a craft brewery may offer personal service, a distinctive product or local knowledge that customers value, so they may accept a higher price. Where the minimum efficient scale is small compared with the market, small firms can reach the lowest cost, and many can exist side by side. Small firms may also be flexible and innovative, and supply parts to large firms. This may not be enough where a small firm sells a standard product and a large rival can undercut it.

In conclusion, the statements are only partly correct. Costs may fall for large firms up to a point, consumers may benefit if savings are passed on, but the claim that there is no place for small firms is unlikely to hold in markets with niche demand or a small minimum efficient scale.

Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/41 · Q4(b) 13 marks 'Mergers of firms in the same industry are against the public interest. It is better that firms remain small.' Discuss this opinion.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Why mergers might be against the public interest: price rises, lower output, less competition, possible price discrimination, supernormal profit.
  • Against this: cheaper unit costs from economies of scale.
  • Comment on the advantages of small firms.
  • Top level needs both sides. Staying on larger firms and not commenting on small firms is held at Level 2.
PlanDefine a horizontal merger. For: economies of scale, R&D, global competitiveness. Against: market power, price, choice, jobs. Small firms: niche, service, flexibility. Judgement: the effect depends on the market and on regulation.
Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/42 · Q3(b) 13 marks Discuss whether mergers between firms always operate against the consumers' interest.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Against consumers: supernormal profit from monopoly power, higher prices, less choice, price discrimination that harms consumers.
  • For consumers: economies of scale and cost reduction, R&D and better quality, natural monopolies that avoid duplication, price discrimination that helps some consumers.
  • Level 4 needs a balanced discussion. Maximum 11 marks if there is no conclusion.
Watch the word "always"The question uses a strong word. Argue it is unlikely to be "always": show the harm and the benefits, then judge which is more likely and when.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/43 · Q4(b) 13 marks Discuss why costs per unit might fall as an industry's output grows, and consider whether consumers are likely to benefit.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Explanation of why LRAC falls: internal economies (technical, purchasing, financial, managerial, marketing) and external economies.
  • Link to price: lower costs may raise the profit-maximising output and could lower price, if demand is large enough to keep that output sold.
  • Whether consumers gain depends on the firm's attitude to profit and on competition: savings may be kept as extra profit.
  • Level 4 (9 to 13) needs both the causes and a judgement on consumer benefit. Little on consumers is held to Level 2 or 3.
PlanTwo or three economies of scale with an LRAC diagram. Then a cost-to-price link using MC and MR. Evaluate: competition, regulation, and diseconomies at larger sizes. Conclude with when consumers are most likely to gain.
Based on May/June 2023 · 9708/42 · Q3 20 marks Evaluate, with the help of a diagram or diagrams, how the size of the total market and the minimum efficient scale of production may decide how many firms an industry can support.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge and analysis (14): economies of scale and their effect on the LRAC curve; MES as the output where LRAC first stops falling; market demand divided by MES gives roughly how many firms can operate at MES.
  • Worked examples: if MES is half the market, room for about two firms (duopoly); if MES is 2% of the market, room for about 50 (competition).
  • Link to market structures: many, few or one firm, matching perfect or monopolistic competition, oligopoly and monopoly.
  • Diagram: LRAC with MES marked, against the market demand curve.
  • Evaluation (6): MES changes with technology; the definition of the market matters (a village shop suits a village market); niche products keep small firms alive even where the main market is concentrated.
PlanDefine MES, draw LRAC with demand. Do two numerical cases (large MES, small MES). Link each to a market structure. Evaluate with technology change, market definition and niche markets, then give a judgement on which factor matters most.

D · Multiple choice questions (Paper 3)

Choose an answer, then read the reason. Source: paper code and question number.

Top tips

  • Do not assume savings reach the consumer. They only do if competition is strong enough, or regulation forces it.
  • Draw the diagram when the question says so. The 2023 merger essay was capped at Level 2 without one. Use LRAC for costs, monopoly for price and output.
  • For small firms, give reasons that are not about cost. Niche, service, local knowledge, flexibility, small MES, support.
  • Use evaluation specific to the case: competition authority, culture clash, debt, size of market, time period.
  • Check the strong words ("always", "no place", "should not have been allowed") and answer on their terms with a clear judgement.
