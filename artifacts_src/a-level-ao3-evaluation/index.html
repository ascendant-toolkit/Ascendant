AO3 Evaluation: How the 6 Marks Are Earned in Paper 4

Exactly what earns 1 to 6 evaluation marks: Table B explained, the levers, Condition, If, Then, how to write the judgement, and Section A evaluation. Animations, labs and practice.

This part in five sentences

AO3 is evaluation: recognising the assumptions and limitations of economic information and models, assessing the strengths and weaknesses of arguments, recognising that some decisions involve priorities and value judgements, and communicating reasoned judgements and conclusions. It is 25% of the A Level but 30% of Paper 4, far more than the 13% on Paper 3. In a 20-mark essay it is worth 6 marks in Table B: E1 (1 to 3) is a vague or general conclusion with simple, undeveloped comments, and E2 (4 to 6) is a justified conclusion that answers the question, together with developed, reasoned, well-supported evaluative comments. In Section A it appears in the later parts, where the syllabus asks for evaluation "where appropriate". Examiner reports call the usual fault evaluation as a list: a factor named and never explained. This page shows what earns each mark, with examples.

1 · Where AO3 marks are, and what they are worth

Question typeAO3What earns it

So 30% of Paper 4 is evaluation. In a 20-mark essay it is about a third of your writing, not a final line.

3 · How to get each mark: 1 to 6

MarkWhat is neededTypical length
1 to 2One or two simple comments, or a general conclusion. Nothing is explained.1 to 3 sentences
3Several comments or a list of factors with some reasoning, and a conclusion that is still vague or does not use the question's wording. Top of E1.5 to 6 sentences
4One developed point (condition, reason, consequence) and a conclusion that answers the question. Bottom of E2.about 80 words
5Two developed points, one of them linked to the context, and a justified conclusion.about 130 words
6Two or three developed points woven through the answer, a conclusion that weighs them and states what the verdict depends on.about 180 words across the answer

The lengths are a guide to what it takes to develop the points, not a mark scheme rule. A long list of unsupported factors stays in E1, while a short, well-reasoned judgement can reach E2.

4 · One question, three evaluations

Question: Assess whether a carbon tax is the most effective way to reduce pollution from coal-fired power stations in a country where coal supplies 70% of electricity. (The analysis is assumed to be developed, with a diagram.)

2 marks (E1, about 25 words)

A carbon tax could work, but there are other policies and it might be expensive. Overall it has advantages and disadvantages.

Why 2: two simple comments and a vague conclusion. Nothing is explained, and the conclusion does not answer "most effective".

4 marks (bottom of E2, about 80 words)

The tax may not be very effective in the short run if demand for electricity is price inelastic. With few alternatives to coal in this country, a higher price could cut the quantity bought only slightly, so emissions would fall little while household bills rise. A carbon tax is therefore unlikely to be the most effective policy on its own at first.

Why 4: one point developed with a condition (elasticity), a reason (few alternatives) and a consequence (emissions barely fall), and a conclusion that uses "most effective". There is only one point, so it stops at 4.

6 marks (top of E2, about 170 words)

First, the effect depends on elasticity and time. In the short run, with 70% of power from coal and few substitutes, demand is likely to be inelastic, so the tax mostly raises revenue; over a decade, solar and gas could replace coal and demand for coal power may become far more elastic. Second, the effect depends on who pays. Poorer households spend a larger share of income on electricity, so the tax could be regressive unless the revenue is used to compensate them, which would weaken the case on equity. Third, regulation, such as a phase-out date for coal plants, achieves a guaranteed cut but without the revenue.

On balance, a carbon tax is likely to be the most effective policy only if it is paired with investment in substitutes, because without them the quantity of coal power barely changes. Its effectiveness depends mainly on how fast alternatives can be built.

Why 6: three developed points (time and elasticity, stakeholders, alternatives), each reasoned, one tied to the context; the conclusion uses the question's wording, gives a verdict, the reason and what it depends on. It introduces conditions rather than repeating the analysis.

6 · The evaluation levers

LeverQuestion to askExample
TimeShort run against long run? Policy lags?"Interest rate cuts may take 18 months to work, by which time the economy could have recovered."
ElasticityHow responsive are the key variables?"If PES is low, a subsidy may raise the price more than output."
MagnitudeIs the effect large enough to matter?"A 1% fall in demand is small compared with the revenue gained."
Spare capacityHow close to full capacity is the economy?"Extra AD raises output with a large negative output gap but mostly prices near full capacity."
ContextDoes it differ by type of economy or market?"In a country with a large informal sector, higher income taxes may be harder to collect."
Trade-off and stakeholdersWho gains, who loses, which objective suffers?"Lower inflation may be bought with higher unemployment, falling mainly on lower-income households."
AlternativesIs there a better policy?"Regulation guarantees the cut; a tax raises revenue."
Assumptions and data limitsWhat does the model assume or the data leave out?"The model assumes perfect competition and rational consumers."

7 · Lever, because, so what

Condition → If → Then → so what for the judgement
  • Condition: name the lever, using the good, group or country in the question.
  • If: say what happens if it holds, and what happens if it does not.
  • Then: give the outcome for the claim in the question, with the reason.
  • So what: say which case is more likely here and why, so it feeds the final judgement.
Named (E1)Developed (E2)
"There could be time lags.""If interest rate cuts take 18 months to work, the economy may already be recovering, so the cut could add to inflation rather than ease a recession. That makes the policy less reliable than the fiscal alternative here."
"It depends on elasticity.""If demand for cigarettes among long-term smokers is inelastic, a 20% tax raises revenue but barely cuts consumption, so the tax serves revenue better than health."
"Overall it is mixed.""Overall the policy is likely to work for goods with close substitutes but not for addictive goods, so it is not the most effective option here."

8 · Writing the judgement

  • Answer the scope word: always, only, alone, best, most effective, to what extent. A scope word asks for a position.
  • Use the question's wording so that the marker can see the specific requirements are met.
  • Give the verdict, the strongest reason and what it depends on. "On balance... because... This would change if..."
  • No new analysis, and not a summary. Introduce a condition or weigh the points.
  • Evaluate inside each paragraph and again at the end. One evaluative paragraph on the last page earns little if it is one-sided.

9 · Common ways to lose evaluation marks

  • Evaluation as a list: time lags, liquidity trap, elasticity, each named and left.
  • Mistaking analysis for evaluation: a third advantage is more AO2.
  • No attempt to question the statement in the question.
  • A conclusion that only summarises, or "overall there are pros and cons".
  • Generic conditions ("the economy", "consumers") instead of the good, group or country.
  • Evaluating a different question: whether a policy works when the question asked whether it is the best.
  • Running out of time so that all the evaluation is two lines at the end.
Exam tipPlan your evaluation before you start. Put one evaluative comment at the end of each chain and leave about 8 to 10 minutes for the final judgement. If you run short of time, write the evaluation, not a fourth chain.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

Evaluation as a list

Reports on Paper 4 describe 'liquidity trap' or 'time lags' named and left, and analysis mistaken for evaluation. These stay in E1.

Exam use: using lever, because, so what for every evaluative point.

The statement is not questioned

A frequent comment is that candidates make no attempt to question the statement in the question, or never answer the scope word (always, only, alone, to what extent).

Exam use: finding a case where the claim fails, then saying how likely it is here.

Conclusions that only summarise

The best judgements weigh the arguments and answer the scope word; weaker ones repeat what has been said.

Exam use: verdict, strongest reason, what it depends on.

Context-based evaluation

In questions about a developing economy, the mark schemes expect the analysis and evaluation to refer to that context. A generic answer is limited.

Exam use: naming the type of economy in your conditions and your conclusion.

Short evaluation in Section A

In the last part, a counter-point developed with the passage and a reasoned judgement earn the evaluation marks. A single well-aimed sentence with a reason can earn a mark.

Exam use: being brief but explained: 'however... because... so...'.

A · Written questions (reworded)

The wording, context and figures are our own, so they are safe to set as practice. The source paper is named for reference. Mark scheme points are in our own words.

Practice question 6 marks Table: unemployment fell from 9% to 7% over four years as GDP growth averaged 3% a year. Consider whether the table shows that economic growth was the main cause of the fall in unemployment.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Select and quote the figures.
  • Link growth to higher demand for labour.
  • Evaluation: the table does not show other possible causes, such as emigration, a change in the size of the labour force or government schemes.
  • A brief judgement on how far it supports the claim.
PlanQuote, link, limit, judge.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Unemployment fell by 2 percentage points while growth averaged 3% a year. Higher growth may have raised demand for labour. However, the table cannot show if emigration or training schemes also lowered unemployment, so it supports the claim only partly.

Practice question 8 marks Extract: 'A new tariff will protect jobs in the steel industry.' To what extent do you agree with this advice, using the extract and your own knowledge?
What the mark scheme wants
  • Support: a tariff raises the price of imports, so domestic steel is more competitive and jobs could be protected in the short run.
  • Counter-point: the extract says steel users rely on imported inputs, so their costs rise and jobs elsewhere could fall.
  • Further limits: retaliation, and a higher price for consumers.
  • A judgement that answers 'to what extent' and uses the extract.
PlanFour marks for support with the extract, four for the counter-point and a judgement.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A tariff raises the price of imported steel, which could protect jobs in domestic steel plants. However, the extract says car makers import most of their steel, so their costs would rise and jobs there could fall, which makes the advice only partly sound.

Practice question 20 marks Assess whether a minimum wage is the best way to reduce poverty in a developing economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Chains: higher pay for low earners; the possible fall in employment if the wage is above equilibrium; the effect on the informal sector.
  • Context: a developing economy with a large informal sector.
  • Evaluation (6 marks): the size of the wage against the median, elasticity of labour demand, enforcement, alternatives such as cash transfers or education.
  • Justified conclusion in the question's words.
PlanEvaluate inside each chain, then conclude: a minimum wage is not the best policy on its own where enforcement is weak.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

A minimum wage may help only workers in the formal sector. In a developing economy where most work is informal, enforcement is likely to be weak, so cash transfers could reach more of the poor. On balance, a minimum wage is unlikely to be the best policy on its own, because it misses the informal sector, although it could help alongside transfers.

Practice question 20 marks To what extent can monetary policy alone control inflation?
What the mark scheme wants
  • Chains: interest rate, borrowing costs, AD; exchange rate effect on import prices; expectations.
  • Evaluation (6 marks): cost-push inflation that AD policy cannot address, time lags, the effect of rates on unemployment, a liquidity trap.
  • Scope word: 'alone' asks for a case where monetary policy is insufficient.
  • A conclusion that says to what extent, and why.
PlanExplain each limit and then conclude. 'Alone' means find where it fails.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Monetary policy may work well against demand-pull inflation, but it is unlikely to control cost-push inflation caused by a rise in oil prices, because higher rates do not lower input costs. Therefore monetary policy alone is likely to control inflation only to a limited extent, depending on the source of the inflation.

B · Multiple choice questions (Paper 1)

Choose an answer, then read the reason. Reworded from the paper and question number shown.

Top tips

  • Do the analysis first. Evaluation of a thin argument has little to work on.
  • Name the lever and explain it. Because, so what.
  • Evaluate inside each chain. And again in the conclusion.
  • Answer the scope word. Always, only, alone, best, to what extent.
  • Use the question's words. In the conclusion.
  • Leave time for it. About a third of the writing.

Weighing"[Factor A] is more significant here than [factor B], because [reason]."
