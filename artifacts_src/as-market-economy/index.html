The Market Economy and the Price Mechanism

1.4.1 to 1.4.2: how a market economy allocates resources, the functions of price, and its strengths and weaknesses. Animated diagrams, labs, drills and past paper questions.

This part in five sentences

In a market economy resources are allocated by the price mechanism: private individuals and firms own the factors of production and make their own decisions, consumers decide what is produced by spending their money, firms choose how to produce in the cheapest way to make a profit, and output goes to those able and willing to pay. Price does three jobs: it rations scarce goods, signals what consumers want and incentivises producers to supply it. The system rewards efficiency, innovation and risk-taking and responds quickly to changes in preferences, with little need for central planning. But markets can fail: they do not provide public goods, under-consume merit goods, over-consume demerit goods, may give monopoly power and inequality, and cannot reach those with no income. In practice no economy is a pure market economy, which is why governments intervene and most economies are mixed.

1 · Features of a market economy

  • Private ownership of factors of production and firms.
  • Profit motive: firms produce what earns a profit and exit if they make losses.
  • Consumer sovereignty: consumers' spending decides what is produced.
  • Competition: many firms compete for customers, which pushes them to cut costs and improve quality.
  • Price mechanism: prices set by demand and supply allocate resources, with little government intervention.
  • Freedom: people choose their jobs, what to buy and whether to start a firm.

2 · The functions of price

FunctionWhat it meansExample
RationingA higher price reduces quantity demanded so that scarce supplies go to those willing and able to payA rise in the price of concert tickets after a sell-out
Signalling (transmitting preferences)Price movements pass information: a higher price shows rising demand or scarcity, a lower price a surplus or falling demandA rising price of electric cars tells firms consumers want more
IncentivisingA higher price and profit encourage firms to produce more and new firms to enter; a lower price encourages firms to cut output or leaveFirms switch to producing what is profitable
  • Price as a signal of scarcity: where resources are limited and wants unlimited, price rations the supply among competing buyers; rising demand relative to supply pushes up price, which attracts more supply.
  • These functions operate in goods markets and in factor markets (wages, rent, interest), which is how resources move to where they are most valued.

3 · How the market answers the three questions

QuestionAnswer in a market economy
What to produce?What consumers will pay for, shown by prices and profits
How to produce?The lowest-cost method chosen by competing firms
For whom?Those with income and the ability to pay, not necessarily those with need

4 · Strengths of a market economy

  • Efficiency: competition pushes firms to use resources efficiently and to keep costs down.
  • Consumer choice: firms produce a wide range of goods that people want.
  • Innovation and enterprise: profit rewards risk-taking and new ideas.
  • Flexibility: prices adjust quickly to changes in preferences and costs, so resources move without waiting for instructions.
  • Low bureaucracy: no need for a large planning authority; decisions are decentralised.

5 · Weaknesses of a market economy (market failure)

  • Public goods are not provided (non-excludable, non-rival, so free riders).
  • Merit goods such as education and healthcare are under-consumed and under-provided; demerit goods such as tobacco are over-consumed (see 3.1).
  • Prices do not always reflect all costs and benefits (external costs such as pollution are ignored).
  • Monopoly power: a firm with little competition can raise prices and restrict output.
  • Inequality: output goes to those with money; people with little income may lack essentials.
  • Immobility of factors (workers cannot move quickly) can cause unemployment.
  • Instability: prices of some goods, such as farm products, are unstable, and the economy may suffer booms and slumps.
Exam tipName the function of price and explain it with an example. When discussing weaknesses, always say what the market does wrongly (under-supplies, over-supplies, not at all). Do not say a market economy has "no" government; say it has little intervention.

6 · Multiple choice

Examples are explained in general terms. Check current details before quoting them in an exam answer.

A surge in demand

A sudden rise in demand for hand sanitiser pushed up its price. The higher price rationed the scarce supply and signalled to firms that more was wanted, so firms switched production to it.

Exam use: rationing, signalling and incentivising together.

Rising rents attract builders

When rents in a city rise, builders see higher profits from housing and build more. The price signal moves resources into housing without any planner deciding.

Exam use: the incentive function of price.

Ticket prices for popular events

Tickets for a popular concert are quickly sold at a high price. Price rations the limited seats to those most willing to pay, but those with less income are excluded.

Exam use: rationing and the 'for whom' question: ability to pay.

Hong Kong and Singapore

These economies are often described as close to the market end of the spectrum: low taxes, limited regulation and open trade. Even so, both governments provide public goods and housing, so neither is a pure market economy.

Exam use: no real economy is purely a market economy.

Street lighting that nobody pays for

A private firm cannot charge for street lighting, since everyone nearby benefits whether they pay or not. The market does not supply it, so governments do.

Exam use: a market failure: public goods.

Based on Oct/Nov 2022 · 9708/21 · Q1(a) 2 marks Explain how 'a product's price acts as a signal of its scarcity'.
What the mark scheme wants

  • A development: rising demand for a product relative to its supply will push up the price and act as an incentive to increase the quantity supplied; scarcity gives rise to the need to make choices between uses of scarce resources (1).
PlanScarcity: limited supply, unlimited wants, price rations. Higher demand pushes the price up, which signals scarcity and encourages more supply.

Resources are limited but wants are unlimited, so price rations the available supply among competing buyers. When demand for a product rises relative to supply, its price goes up. The higher price signals that the good is scarce and gives firms an incentive to supply more.

Based on Feb/March 2023 · 9708/22 · Q2(a) 8 marks Explain the functions of price in resource allocation and consider the importance of these functions in relation to the potential effectiveness of a market economy.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Knowledge (up to 3): at least two functions of price: rationing (1), signalling or transmitting preferences (1), incentivising (1); two functions well explained can gain 3.
  • Analysis (up to 3): the importance of rationing, signalling and incentivising to the operation of a market economy, in the context of a market economy.
  • Evaluation (up to 2): a judgement on the importance of at least two functions for the effectiveness of a market economy, and a justified conclusion.
PlanRationing. Signalling. Incentive. Why each matters for a market economy. Judge which matters most.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Price has three functions in allocating resources. Rationing means a higher price reduces demand to match the limited supply. Signalling means prices pass information about consumers' preferences to producers, so that a rise in price shows that more of the good is wanted. Incentivising means higher prices and profits encourage firms to produce more, while lower prices push them to leave. Together they let a market economy work without central direction: scarce goods go to those who value them most, firms produce what is wanted, and resources move into growing industries automatically.

The functions are important for effectiveness, but they only work well if prices reflect all costs and benefits and if consumers and producers can respond. Signalling and incentives are probably the most important for long-run efficiency, while rationing is the most immediate. Where markets fail, as with public goods, price cannot do these jobs.

Based on Oct/Nov 2020 · 9708/21 · Q2(b) 12 marks Discuss why in most mixed economies, resources are mainly allocated using market forces and the price mechanism.
What the mark scheme wants
  • Analysis (up to 8): analysis of the market forces and price mechanism element (up to 4): the price mechanism, prices as signals of consumer preferences, private ownership and decision-making with little government intervention; and of the government intervention element (up to 4): the use of the word 'mainly' makes clear that market forces are not the exclusive method, with a public sector providing public and merit goods.
  • Evaluation (up to 4): a judgement on why resources are mainly, but not exclusively, allocated using market forces and the price mechanism, with a reserved mark for a conclusion.
PlanMarket: price signals, efficiency, choice, incentives. Government: public and merit goods, regulation. Judge: markets are efficient for most goods, but fail for some.
Knowledge (K)Application (A)Analysis (N)Evaluation (E)

Most resources are allocated by markets because the price mechanism rations scarce goods, signals consumer preferences and gives firms an incentive to be efficient, with little need for central planning. Private ownership and competition encourage innovation and choice. But markets fail to provide public goods, under-consume merit goods and over-consume demerit goods, so governments provide or regulate these, which is why resources are only mainly, not entirely, allocated by markets.

The market is the main method because it is generally efficient and flexible, whereas heavy government control tends to be slow and inefficient. A government role remains necessary where markets fail, so a mixed system, with the market doing most of the allocation, is likely to work best.

Top tips

  • Name the three functions. Rationing, signalling, incentivising.
  • Use an example for each. A price rise after a surge in demand.
  • Answer what, how, for whom. Consumer spending, lowest cost, ability to pay.
  • Give both sides. Strengths and market failure.
  • Be precise on weaknesses. Say which goods are under-, over- or not supplied.
  • Avoid 'no government'. Say 'little intervention'.
  • Link to mixed. Market failure is why governments intervene.
